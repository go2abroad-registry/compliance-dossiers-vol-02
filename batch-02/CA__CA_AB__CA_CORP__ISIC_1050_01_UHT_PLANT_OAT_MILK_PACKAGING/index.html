VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter or CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies over four consecutive calendar quarters; once exceeded, regular GST registration is mandatory for commercial activity

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to supplies of goods exported from Canada (ETA Schedule VI, Part V); for UHT oat milk, zero-rating requires proof that possession of the goods was transferred to the foreign buyer or its agent outside Canada for outbound shipment, with commercial documents evidencing the sale and physical movement to a destination outside Canada; certain offshore services relating to the goods and rendered in Canada may also be zero-rated when supplied to a non-resident who has no Canadian nexus.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CADEX/CAED) or equivalent CBSA export record
  • Bill of Lading / Airway Bill evidencing shipment to a foreign destination
  • Commercial Invoice identifying the foreign buyer and invoice value
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or LC/SWIFT MT103 evidence of payment from the foreign buyer (supporting the export transaction)

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal e-invoicing, government clearance, or digital stamping; GST/HST filers may issue electronic invoices provided they meet ETA section 223(1) information requirements, and large registrants are subject to CRA digital record-keeping regulations; mandatory electronic filing (GST/HST net-file) applies to certain businesses, and third-party electronic clearing is neither required nor supported by the CRA.

CRA may reassess exported-supply zero-rating and claw back Input Tax Credits if the exporter fails to retain satisfactory export evidence; penalties include ETA section 280 penalties (up to CAD 25 per day, maximum CAD 10,000) for failure to provide information, gross negligence penalties under ETA section 286/ITA section 163(2), and criminal fraud charges under section 381 of the Excise Tax Act for deliberate false claims, which can lead to a fine up to CAD 10,000 or imprisonment up to 7 years; interest on reassessed amounts applies from the original due date.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully liberalized capital account under the Currency Act and the Investment Canada Act. The Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely convertible for both current and capital transactions, with no restrictions on inbound or outbound fund transfers, no exchange control approval requirements, and no prior authorization hurdles for trade payments, dividend remittances, intercompany loans, or equity contributions. Profit remittances are subject only to applicable withholding taxes under the Income Tax Act (e.g., Part XIII non-resident withholding tax) and standard AML compliance. Contractual obligations with a Canadian bank are denominated in CAD or freely selected foreign currencies. The OECD Code of Liberalisation of Capital Movements is respected. Any exception arises from sanctions administered under UN Act and the Special Economic Measures Act, not from general FX restrictions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory timeline or mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts under Canadian law. Exporters are entitled to retain all proceeds in any currency, hold them in foreign currency accounts at domestic banks, or convert them to CAD at any time. The only obligation is reporting to Statistics Canada under the International Transactions Reporting System (ITRS) for statistical purposes, typically submitted by the financial institution, and compliance with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) for cross-border transaction records above CAD 10,000.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada under the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act; foreign exchange administration authority rests with the Minister of Finance and is operationally supported by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium with mandatory regulatory localization. Offshore digital EMI structures (e.g., UK-or EU-licensed) can provide ancillary liquidity and payment routing rails, but are not a substitute for a Canadian prudentially regulated bank account. A fintech EMI operating in Canada must either acquire a licensed bank or be registered as a Money Service Business (MSB) with FINTRAC, unless it operates only as an unregulated software orchestrator. In practice, a province-incorporated entity would be well advised to open a direct clearing account with an OSFI-regulated Schedule I bank, and use an EMI exclusively for cross-currency sweeps where cost efficiency exists. Legal constraints: EMI accounts cannot hold statutory deposits in Canada without bank licensing; many EMIs are not able to hold CAD balances as 'deposits' in the strict legal sense, which raises counterparty and insolvency risk.

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Combined 23% general corporate income tax rate for Alberta business corporations (federal Part I 15% + Alberta general corporate tax 8%); combined 21.5% for qualified manufacturing and processing (M&P) profits.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on dividends paid to a non-resident shareholder; no separate Alberta withholding on dividends.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T2 must be filed no later than six months after the fiscal period end. The unpaid balance of federal and Alberta corporate tax is due not later than two months after year-end, extended to three months for a Canadian-controlled private corporation qualifying under the small-business deduction.

Box-by-Box Return Breakdown (non-resident focus)

BoxField Non-resident guidanceTax-evasion pitfall
Box 100 - Identification and fiscal period Legal corporate name, actual industrial classification (NAICS 311999 and ISIC 1050), Business Number/associated accounts, Alberta corporate account code, fiscal period-end date. Non-resident corporation carrying on business through a Canadian permanent establishment must identify the PE's address and treaty-based PE status rather than head office data. Late-filing penalty is 5% of the unpaid balance plus 1% per full month late for up to 12 months; a second/third offence within three years lifts the base to 10%. Gross negligence civil penalty on understated tax is up to 50%, with CRA daily compound interest on unpaid balances; evasion or fraud can produce fines from 50% to 200% of the federal tax evaded and imprisonment.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Contemporaneous Local File documentation is mandatory where the aggregate value of reportable non-arm's length cross-border transactions with a non-resident exceeds CAD 1,000,000 in a taxation year; a group Master File is additionally required when the multinational group's annual consolidated revenue exceeds CAD 1,000,000,000 (or when specially requested by CRA).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain an Economic Substance Regulation registry similar to Gulf Cooperation Council 'ESR' scholarships; however, the Income Tax Act applies a general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) and CRA requires real economic substance for inter-financing and IP migration. A UHT oat milk plant with no local personnel, no local decision-making and artificially interposed non-resident marketing or procurement companies can be re-characterized, denied treaty benefits, or have its place of central management and control challenged as Canadian resident.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion / false statements under the Income Tax Act

Statutory sanction: Under s.239(1) ITA, a person who makes false statements or gets someone to do so is guilty of an offence and liable to a fine of at least 50% and up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and, on indictment, imprisonment for a term not exceeding five years.

Director personal liability: Directors who 'direct, authorize, assent to, acquiesce in, or participate in' the offence may be charged as parties or agents; civil liability under s.227.1 can attach to directors for unremitted withheld tax, and severe fraud findings can expose personal bank accounts and travel bans under CRA's creditor collections measures.
Transfer pricing miscalculation and missing documentation

Statutory sanction: CRA can reassess taxable income using the arm's-length principle under s.247(2), levy a 10% transfer-pricing penalty on the understatement of Canadian tax payable as a result of adjustment (or a $5,000 fine per reporting omission), plus compound arrears interest.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for the amount of taxes that the corporation was required to remit but failed to remit (s.227.1), and can be assessed personally for transfer-pricing penalty taxes if they signed the false T2 as taxpayers; a civil fraud claim may pierce the corporate veil on a 'facade of incorporation' theory.
Non-resident withholding tax evasion / unreported dividends or royalties

Statutory sanction: The payer becomes liable for the full 25% un-withheld amount under s.215(5), plus a 10% Part XIII penalty, 50% gross-negligence penalty, daily compound interest, and the amounts are not deductible unless withheld and paid; continuing abatement can lead to asset seizure.

Director personal liability: Directors who fail to ensure the corporation withholds and remits Part XIII tax are personally liable under s.227.1 (joint and several for the full amount, interest and penalties), and the CRA may secure seizure, garnish receivables, and place the individual in a tax collection order with travel restrictions if criminal evasion is found.
Corporate residence manipulation / 'shell company' treaty abuse

Statutory sanction: If central management and control is effectively exercised in Canada, the corporation is deemed resident of Canada, taxed worldwide income, subject to Part XIII re-characterizations of foreign distributions, and GAAR can collapse the structure; penalties include full reassessment, interest, up to 50% gross-negligence penalty, and possible exemption of treaty benefits under the principal-purpose test.

Director personal liability: Directors who execute the sham structure can be liable for restitution and unpaid CRA assessments where the corporate veil is a 'companion' used for fraud; Canada can issue departure/restraint orders and criminal forfeiture under a tax-evasion prosecution.
AML/economic substance and anti-money laundering failures (proceeds-of-crime exposure)

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), failure to report suspicious transactions or maintain compliance program risks administrative monetary penalties, criminal prosecution for money laundering; income tax law may disallow interest deductions and treat unrepaid advances as deemed dividends or shareholder benefits.

Director personal liability: A director who is used as a conduit bears personal risk for aggravated money laundering (penalties to imprisonment up to 10 years under the Criminal Code); CRA and FINTRAC can report individuals, and directors can be ordered to personally restore diverted funds and barred from leaving Canada pending resolution.
