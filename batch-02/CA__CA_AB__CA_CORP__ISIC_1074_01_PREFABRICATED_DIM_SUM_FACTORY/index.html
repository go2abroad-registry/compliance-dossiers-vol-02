Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including Business Registration Online and My Business Account. Canadian federal and Alberta corporate income tax returns are generally administered and filed through the CRA under the federal-Alberta tax administration arrangements. The Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA), through the CARM Client Portal, administers commercial import and export customs accounts and related trade documentation.

E-government filing portal

A corporation must obtain a nine-digit Business Number (BN) from the CRA. A newly incorporated Canadian corporation may receive a BN through federal incorporation or must apply through CRA Business Registration Online or Form RC1. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada, importing or exporting commercial goods, employing personnel, or otherwise having Canadian tax obligations must apply for the relevant CRA program accounts, including Corporation Income Tax (RC), GST/HST (RT), payroll deductions (RP), and import-export (RM), as applicable. Registration normally requires the legal name, incorporation jurisdiction and date, registered address, directors, principal business activity, fiscal year-end, authorized representative, and Canadian business contact information. Registration in Alberta or federally does not by itself create a GST/HST account; the corporation must separately register where required or elects voluntary registration. Commercial importers and exporters must obtain a CBSA importer-exporter RM account and complete CARM onboarding. Non-resident corporations may be required to provide additional identity, business-activity, security, or representative information. Provincial corporate registration, extra-provincial registration, municipal licensing, and food-processing permits are separate from CRA tax registration.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A Canadian corporation is generally taxed as a separate legal entity; Canada has no general United States-style check-the-box election for corporations. Legal-form classification is determined under Canadian corporate and tax law. Available elections may include section 85 transfers of eligible property to a taxable Canadian corporation, section 86 capital reorganizations, section 97(2) partnership rollovers, and other reorganization elections where statutory conditions are satisfied. These elections do not convert an ordinary corporation into a general pass-through entity. Canadian hybrid-mismatch rules can deny deductions or require income inclusions for arrangements producing deduction/non-inclusion or duplicate deduction outcomes. Alberta has no general territorial tax-exempt-zone classification for an ordinary frozen-food manufacturing corporation.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding supplies of financial services and goodwill, measured under the small-supplier rules. The threshold generally applies to taxable supplies in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters. A person exceeding the applicable threshold must register within the statutory period and generally becomes liable to collect GST from the effective registration date. Voluntary registration is permitted where the business is below the threshold and conducts commercial activities.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada and not consumed, used, or supplied in Canada may generally be zero-rated at 0% GST where the statutory export conditions and documentary evidence requirements are met. The supplier should retain evidence that the goods left Canada, such as a customs export declaration, carrier documentation, commercial invoice, shipping records, and proof of delivery outside Canada. Services supplied to a non-resident may be zero-rated where the service falls within the applicable exported-service rules and the statutory exclusions do not apply. Services relating to real property situated in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada, certain personal services performed in Canada, and other excluded categories may remain taxable. A GST registrant may generally claim input tax credits for GST incurred on inputs used in commercial activities, including zero-rated export activities, subject to valid documentary support. Exporters must comply separately with CBSA export reporting, controlled-goods, sanctions, food, labeling, and destination-country requirements.

  • Canadian customs export declaration or applicable Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) filing, where reporting is legally required
  • Commercial invoice identifying the exporter, consignee, goods, value, currency, terms of sale, and destination
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, carrier manifest, or equivalent transport document
  • Packing list and product description sufficient to reconcile the shipment
  • Proof of export or delivery outside Canada, including carrier tracking, delivery confirmation, or foreign import records where available
  • Purchase order, sales contract, and accounting records linking the export to the zero-rated supply
  • Evidence of GST registration and input tax credit support where a GST refund or net tax recovery is claimed
  • Inward remittance or bank payment evidence where commercially available and relevant to audit support; Canada does not generally require an Inward Remittance Certificate as a universal statutory GST zero-rating document

Canada has no general nationwide real-time GST invoice-clearance, government e-invoicing, or digital tax-stamping mandate for ordinary business-to-business transactions. Electronic invoices and electronic accounting records are generally acceptable if they are reliable, complete, retrievable, and retained for the statutory record-retention period. GST registrants must issue invoices or other supporting documentation containing information sufficient to substantiate the tax charged and input tax credits, including the supplier identity and GST registration number where required, invoice date, description, consideration, and GST amount or applicable rate. CRA electronic filing and payment obligations apply to GST returns according to the registrant's filing frequency. CARM electronic customs processes and the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) apply to relevant customs transactions but are not general domestic GST invoice-clearance systems.

Canada has no general statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds and no universal 180-day repatriation rule for GST zero-rating. CRA may reassess GST, deny input tax credits or export zero-rating, and require repayment of improperly refunded or unremitted amounts where export conditions or records are not substantiated. Reassessed amounts may attract arrears interest and applicable administrative penalties. False statements or omissions, gross-negligence conduct, intentional tax evasion, fraudulent refund claims, and obstruction may produce additional penalties, interest, and criminal prosecution under the Excise Tax Act and related legislation. Customs non-compliance may result in CBSA seizure, forfeiture, administrative monetary penalties under the Administrative Monetary Penalty System, reassessment of duties and taxes, and loss or suspension of trade privileges. The corporation should preserve GST, sales, shipping, customs, payment, and production records for the applicable statutory retention period, generally at least six years from the end of the relevant year, subject to extensions and specific record rules.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full Article VIII IMF status with unrestricted current and capital account convertibility. The Canadian dollar (CAD) is a freely floating, fully convertible fiat currency with no prior approval requirements for dividend or profit remittance by foreign-owned Canadian corporations. Repatriation of profits is subject only to: (i) corporate solvency tests under the Canada Business Corporations Act or Alberta Business Corporations Act; (ii) Part XIII federal withholding tax on dividends (normally 25%, often reduced by treaty); and (iii) compliance with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) reporting thresholds (C$10,000). No capital gains licensing, no registration with the Bank of Canada for equity injections, and no advance exchange control approval is required for a qualified ISIC 1074 frozen food manufacturing subsidiary.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None exist. Canadian law imposes no statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no surrender requirement for export proceeds. Receipts from frozen dim sum and dumpling exports may be retained in CAD or any foreign currency at the exporter's discretion; foreign currency balances may be held in Foreign Currency Accounts with Schedule I banks. Mandatory reporting obligations are limited to: (i) FINTRAC large transaction reports for cash equivalents at/above C$10,000; (ii) Statistics Canada/International Transactions in Banking Accounts (ITBA) quarterly reporting for enterprises with annual foreign receipts or payments above C$3 million; and (iii) Canada Border Services Agency export declaration for shipments exceeding C$2,000 or controlled goods.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential supervision; Canada has abolished all exchange controls under the Currency, Mint and Exchange Fund Act amendments of 1951. Foreign exchange administration functions are distributed between Bank of Canada, OSFI, and FINTRAC for reporting; no separate foreign exchange control ministry exists.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility: Low as a primary bank but viable as an ancillary payment facilitator. Under the Bank Act only resident incorporated Schedule I/II banks or cooperative credit societies may take deposits in Canada. Foreign electronic money institutions (EMIs) have no direct licensing path for deposit-taking; since November 16, 2024, payment service providers must register under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) with the Bank of Canada. Offshore EMIs (e.g., Wise Platform, Airwallex, Payoneer) may legally provide cross-border payment settlement, currency conversion, and payroll disbursement, but cannot hold the statutory capital escrow nor act as the principal operational treasury bank. Recommended structure: Schedule I corporate account for all compliance-critical flows; a contracted RPAA-registered PSP only for low-value trade settlement where FTZ or platform marketplaces require e-wallet rails. Legal constraint: Canadian aml/kyc obligations cannot be delegated to a foreign EMI; the reporting entity remains the CA_CORP account holder under FINTRAC.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant routing via SWIFT MT103 wire from the investor's remitting bank to the beneficiary account at the Canadian Schedule I bank. Since Canada uses no IBAN or IFSC, the beneficiary field must contain the Canadian bank code (3 digits), transit number (5 digits), and account number; SWIFT BIC and the bank name must be exact. Narrative field required: 'Shareholder capital contribution – CA_CORP / Alberta dim sum manufacturing' and, where available, the balance-of-payments purpose code 'LO' (equity injections) or 'LT' (loan disbursement). The receiving bank will classify the flow as direct investment under OECD BPM6. FINTRAC large cash reporting applies if any leg of the contribution settles in cash ≥ C$10,000. For foreign-controlled CA_CORP, no prior Economic Investment Approval is needed; however, if the shareholder first establishes a new Canadian business, the Investment Canada Act requires a simple notification filing for non-Canadian investors where the entity is WTO-based; for the majority of OECD inbound investors this is a no-review notification, not an approval. Directors should prepare: (i) bank's Corporate New Account Application; (ii) capital injection cover letter; (iii) board resolution approving the contribution; (iv) source-of-funds documentation; and (v) Form T106 (if non-arm's-length with non-resident shareholder) for CRA filing.Treasury clearing rails for settlement of trade payables and receivable conversion: (1) primary rail: commercial Letter of Credit (L/C) issued via SWIFT MT700 with the Canadian bank's issuing branch, settlement upon presentation of conforming documents via MT103/MT202; (2) open account trade settlements via SWIFT MT103 using the standard correspondent bank corridor (RBC, TD, BMO, or Scotiabank North American corridor); (3) documentary collections (DP/DA) via MT400 series for lower-value or transacting-country-specific clients; (4) domestic Canadian inter-bank CAD clearing via Payments Canada's Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) for intra-country vendor payments (refrigerated packaging, food ingredients, utilities), with Real-Time Rail (RTR) available for low-value payments; (5) for procurement of Asian-origin ingredients (bamboo shoots, soy sauce, shrimp) from suppliers in China, Vietnam, or Indonesia, banks will require a back-to-back L/C or transferable L/C arrangement; frozen dim sum export receivables are typically collected in USD or CAD under e-commerce marketplace (Amazon/Whole Foods distributor) rails with daily wallet sweeps into the Schedule I account. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility: Low as a primary bank but viable as an ancillary payment facilitator. Under the Bank Act only resident incorporated Schedule I/II banks or cooperative credit societies may take deposits in Canada. Foreign electronic money institutions (EMIs) have no direct licensing path for deposit-taking; since November 16, 2024, payment service providers must register under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) with the Bank of Canada. Offshore EMIs (e.g., Wise Platform, Airwallex, Payoneer) may legally provide cross-border payment settlement, currency conversion, and payroll disbursement, but cannot hold the statutory capital escrow nor act as the principal operational treasury bank. Recommended structure: Schedule I corporate account for all compliance-critical flows; a contracted RPAA-registered PSP only for low-value trade settlement where FTZ or platform marketplaces require e-wallet rails. Legal constraint: Canadian aml/kyc obligations cannot be delegated to a foreign EMI; the reporting entity remains the CA_CORP account holder under FINTRAC.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not directly implement OECD Local File/Master File frameworks in domestic law. Comprehensive contemporaneous documentation is expected under Income Tax Act s.247(4) when aggregate cross-border controlled transactions exceed approximately CAD 1,000,000 for tangible goods or CAD 250,000 for services. Mandatory Country-by-Country Reporting (CbCR) applies to MNE groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750,000,000 (~CAD 1.13 billion).

Economic substance scope: Alberta and federal Canadian law do not have a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime. Economic substance is nonetheless tested through the federal General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under s.245 of the Income Tax Act, through the transfer-pricing arm's-length standard, and through bank/AML due diligence procedures requiring physical premises, employees, and operational records.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, civil penalties range from 50% to 200% of the evaded tax plus compounded daily interest. Criminal prosecution under s.239 may result in a fine of up to 200% of the taxes evaded or 2 years' imprisonment, or both.

Director personal liability: CRA can pierce the corporate veil where a director participated in or silently assented to the evasion. The director may be personally liable under s.227.1 for unpaid withholding taxes and GST/HST, subject to separate criminal prosecution; travel bans and revocation of director status can be imposed through Canadian courts.
Shell Company / Artificial Arrangement

Statutory sanction: GAAR permits the CRA to re-characterize the arrangement and deny all tax benefits. Transfer-pricing adjustments under s.247 may trigger a penalty of 10% of the net adjustment; treaty relief may be denied under the principal purpose test, and the CRA may assess the corporation on a look-through basis.

Director personal liability: Directors may be prosecuted for conspiracy to defeat the Income Tax Act, a criminal offence under the Canadian Criminal Code; courts may order restitution to the Crown, impose a travel ban pending proceedings, and sentence directors to imprisonment for up to 5 years where property is fraudulently derived from the scheme.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) / FINTRAC Compliance Failures

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, non-compliance attracts administrative penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation; for repeated wilful offences, criminal liability reaches fines up to CAD 2,000,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for corporate AML penalties, but s.74.1 of the PCMLTFA imposes personal liability where the director directed or authorized the offence. Remedies include personal financial restitution, a Canada-wide travel ban, and imprisonment up to 5 years.
