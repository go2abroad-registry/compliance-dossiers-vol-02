Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – Business Number registration via the 'My Business Account' portal; Customs registration via the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for export reporting.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity seeking a Canadian TIN must apply to the CRA for a Business Number (BN). Where the entity carries on a commercial activity in Canada, the GST/HST program account is included in the same BN application. The foreign entity may apply online through the Non-Resident GST/HST account process or submit Form RC1, Request for a Business Number, together with corporate constitutive documents, proof of director identity, and a completed Canada-foreign treaty residence questionnaire. In Alberta, no separate provincial tax account is required as Alberta has no provincial sales tax; provincial corporate income tax is administered through the CRA under an agreement. Non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada must also register with the CRA under subsection 116(1) if they dispose of taxable Canadian property, and may be required to provide a security deposit for GST/HST where the Minister deems collection risk exists.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Not applicable for Canadian corporations (Canada does not operate a 'check-the-box' regime). Canadian incorporated entities are automatically taxable at the entity level under Part I of the Income Tax Act. Foreign entity classifications are determined by CRA administrative guidelines based on legal form and limited liability characteristics. Hybrid mismatch rules under the Income Tax Act (sections 18.2-18.8, effective for transactions after July 1, 2022) disallow mismatches arising from hybrid financial instruments or entities and override certain treaty interpretations. Available elections relevant to cross-border structures include the section 216 election for non-resident rental income, the joint venture election, and treaty-based relief filings under Form T2059.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 – a GST/HST registrant is required when the entity's worldwide taxable supplies from commercial activity in Canada exceed $30,000 in any single calendar quarter or in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (subject to exceptions for non-resident acquirers or qualifying treaty exemptions)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available for supplies of goods that are exported from Canada by the supplier or in accordance with the regulations, and for services supplied to a non-resident person who is not in Canada at any time of the supply where the use occurs outside Canada. The vehicle manufacturer must prove actual physical removal of the goods from Canada pursuant to an export contract, delivery to a common carrier, or export by the foreign purchaser. In addition, the CBSA export report must be filed and the goods must not be further processed, modified, or consumed in Canada. For controlled military equipment, the zero-rating claim requires a valid Export Permit or International Traffic in Arms-type clearance from Global Affairs Canada and compliance with the Export and Import Permits Act; zero-rating does not apply to goods that remain in Canada for a period of time without export evidence.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA CAED report)
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill (with cargo manifest reference to 336992)
  • Commercial Invoice and Purchase Order
  • Export Permit / Controlled Goods Certificate (Global Affairs Canada)
  • Proof of Foreign Payment (SWIFT / wire instruction evidencing inward remittance from non-resident purchaser)

No real-time electronic fiscal clearance or mandatory e-invoicing is imposed by the Canada Revenue Agency as of the current compliance year. Taxpayers are required to keep all invoice records and supporting documents in a legible format for six years; electronic invoices (EDI or XML) are accepted if the prescribed GST/HST information is present. The CBSA imposes mandatory electronic export reporting through the Canadian Automated Export Declaration (CAED) system or a recognized service provider for all regulated exports, including controlled military goods. No digital stamping or blockchain clearance mechanism is currently used.

The CRA may disallow zero-rating, reassess uncollected GST, and recover input tax credits previously claimed if insufficient export evidence is maintained. Penalties under the Excise Tax Act include a failure-to-register penalty of 1% of net tax at issue, failure-to-file penalties of 5% plus 1% per month, and gross negligence penalties of up to 50% of the net tax amount. Interest (prescribed daily rate) runs from the date the tax should have been remitted. Intentional fraudulent export claims may trigger criminal prosecution under the Excise Tax Act (sections 327-328) and the Criminal Code, exposing directors to fines, imprisonment, and debarment from federal procurement programs. Retroactive clawbacks may extend to the prior six tax years in cases of misrepresentation that qualifies as negligent conduct.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a free-floating currency with full convertibility on both current and capital account transactions. There are no general exchange control restrictions on the conversion or transfer of funds for trade-related payments, dividends, royalties, or capital repatriation. However, transactions involving sanctioned jurisdictions, military goods, and dual-use items fall under the authority of the Special Economic Measures Act (SEMA), the United Nations Act, and the Export and Import Permits Act (EIPA), which may effectively create licensing-based restrictions on the use of Canadian dollars and banking channels for such transactions. Profit remittance is permitted without central bank approval if the underlying commercial transaction is lawful and the corporate entity has filed all required tax returns and has no outstanding arrears to the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA). Dividends paid to non-residents are generally subject to Canadian non-resident withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, but treaty relief may reduce the rate.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada does not impose any statutory timeline or mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. Exporters are free to retain foreign currency in any bank account inside or outside Canada, settle invoices through hedging instruments, and convert funds at their discretion. However, any inbound or outbound transfer exceeding CAD 10,000 must be reported to FINTRAC. For military and strategic goods governed by the EIPA and the Controlled Goods Directorate (CGD), the exporter must ensure that payment routing does not contravene export permit conditions and that no funds are derived from or routed through proscribed entities. There is no legal requirement for Canadian exporters to surrender foreign exchange to the Bank of Canada or any other government body.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), with administrative oversight of the Currency Exchange and Reporting System administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for cross-border fund movement reporting

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Limited Feasibility. Canadian-chartered banks remain the primary licensed institutions for holding operational funds. Non-resident fintech EMIs (e.g., Wise, Payoneer, AirWallex) are generally not permitted to provide full domestic Canadian banking services without an OSFI-regulated deposit-taking license or partnership with a Canadian Schedule I bank. They may provide payment facilitation and foreign exchange settlement as unlicensed Money Service Businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC, but such entities are not acceptable as primary treasury accounts for a defense-sector holding. High-Risk_Special_License classification under ISIC 3040 requires cleared banking counterparties with demonstrable AML/KYC programs and sanctions screening aligned with OSFI Guideline E-13. Offshore EMIs without physical Canadian presence cannot issue Canadian paper-based payment instruments or comply with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) reporting requirements on behalf of the corporate account holder. Therefore, fintech EMIs are rated only as ancillary collection/collection-account rails, not as treasury liquidity custodians.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections must be routed through the Canadian banking system using SWIFT MT103 messages with proper purpose codes. The corporate treasury should use the Bank of Canada's Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) or an international wire through a Canadian correspondent. Foreign direct investment and equity contributions should use purpose code '70' (Direct investment) or the appropriate ISO 20022 code under the payment header, such as 'EQUI' for equity contributions, or 'LOAN' for shareholder loans. For all inbound wires exceeding CAD 10,000, the receiving bank must complete FINTRAC reporting; the corporate client must provide underlying transaction documentation including a board resolution, foreign corporate registration, certificate of incumbency, and a certified copy of the wire transfer instructions. If the sender is a sanctioned jurisdiction or a jurisdiction subject to SEMA restrictions, the wire will be blocked. For defense-tactical sector, all inbound capital from government clients should carry an end-user certificate and a valid export permit to avert the clearing bank's enhanced due-diligence refusal.Outbound payments for raw materials, subcontracting, and equipment procurement should be executed through SWIFT MT103 corporate-to-corporate transfers when the beneficiary is a known supplier. For large defense contracts with sovereign clients, documentary payment rails are preferred, specifically irrevocable confirmed Commercial Letters of Credit (L/C) routed through Canadian and foreign correspondent banks. The treasury should also deploy the Canadian Payments Association (now Payments Canada) rails for domestic disbursements, including Real-Time Rail (RTR) if commercially available, and pre-authorized debits for recurring service contracts. For overseas operations, the entity should maintain multi-currency accounts at a tier-1 Canadian bank with foreign currency clearing capabilities. Outbound payments to jurisdictions under the Canadian sanctions list are prohibited. For transactions with NATO or Five-Eyes allies, expedited clearing may be available through government-designated payment channels. To avoid money-laundering cross-border review delays, submit the commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, customs entry proof, and export permit for every disbursement above CAD 100,000. SWIFT MT103 should carry the correct transaction purpose code, such as 'TREA' for trade-related payments, or the ISO 20022 'PURP' code reflecting trade services. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% (15% federal base rate under s.123 ITA + 8% Alberta general corporate rate under the Alberta Corporate Tax Act; applicable to a large non-CCPC defense manufacturing corporation)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due within six months after the end of the corporation's fiscal year-end; for a December 31 year-end, the deadline is June 30 of the following calendar year; instalment payments are due on the 15th day of the month following each instalment month.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No fixed CAD-dollar threshold in the Income Tax Act triggers a Local File or Master File as in OECD BEPS Action 13; instead, every Canadian taxpayer engaged in cross-border controlled transactions must maintain contemporaneous documentation sufficient to demonstrate an arm's-length result. The s.247(3) penalty applies where the net upward adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5 million or 10% of the taxpayer's gross revenue and reasonable efforts are absent; Country-by-Country Reporting (Form RC4649) is mandatory for multinational groups with annual consolidated revenue of at least CAD 750 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada is not an Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) jurisdiction. Economic substance and commercial purpose are nevertheless decisive under the GAAR (s.245 ITA), the Canadian judiciary's 'overall business purpose' doctrine, and the beneficial-ownership requirement found in Canada's tax treaties. A contracting party, agency, or financing conduit lacking real activity, personnel, and risk control will be recharacterized and denied treaty benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (s.239 Income Tax Act)

Statutory sanction: On indictment, a fine of 50%-200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years; on summary conviction, a fine at the court's discretion up to the same percentage range and imprisonment for up to two years. Civil consequences include the s.163(2) gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax, s.161 accrued interest, asset seizure, and mandatory restoration of records.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorize, participate in, or knowingly benefit from evasion are exposed to personal criminal liability as parties; the CRA may use s.160 to collect unpaid taxes from assets transferred to directors; criminal restitution orders, travel bans, and denial of passports can be imposed, and the corporate veil is pierced where the corporation operates as a sham.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing and False Documentation (s.247 ITA)

Statutory sanction: CRA reassessment to arm's-length terms plus a s.247(3) penalty of 10% of the net upward adjustment; false statements attract an additional s.163(2) gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax; prescribed-rate daily compound interest is added to all arrears and penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or approve false transfer-pricing documentation can be held personally liable under s.163(2) for culpable conduct, and the CRA will seek restitution from directors who participate in misrepresentation. Egregious misconduct is referred to the Public Prosecution Service of Canada for criminal evasion charges with consequential travel bans and imprisonment.
GAAR and Treaty Abuse (s.245 ITA, Shell Company Structures)

Statutory sanction: Denial of the tax benefit under GAAR, reassessment of all prior years, a s.245(6) penalty of 25% of the tax benefit for transactions entered after March 21, 2016, accrued interest, and disallowance of treaty relief under the beneficial-ownership limitation. Repeated failure may also trigger a recommendation for criminal referral.

Director personal liability: Directors of the Canadian corporation who sign the resolution implementing the conduit structure may be named in the GAAR reassessment and held jointly liable for restitution where assets have been stripped from the taxpayer; the corporate veil is pierced if the conduit is shown to be a mere puppet, and directors face civil money judgments and possible travel restrictions.
Corruption of Foreign Public Officials and Unlicensed Defense Exports (CFPOA / Export and Import Permits Act)

Statutory sanction: CFPOA violation is an indictable offence carrying imprisonment for up to 14 years and a fine determined at the court's discretion with no statutory maximum; an EIPA cross-border violation is prosecutable on indictment with fines, imprisonment up to five years, seizure and forfeiture of the goods, revocation of all export permits, and debarment from Canadian federal procurement for up to ten years under the Integrity Regime.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize or ignore bribery face personal criminal liability under the CFPOA and the Criminal Code; travel bans and asset freezes are routinely sought; the corporate veil is pierced where the corporation is a façade for illicit commissions, exposing directors to civil restitution, punitive damages, and long-term imprisonment.
Part XIII Withholding Failure and Anti-Money-Laundering Non-Compliance (s.227 ITA / PCMLTFA)

Statutory sanction: The full unremitted withholding tax is collectible from the corporation under s.227(8), plus daily prescribed interest and a penalty of up to 20% of the unremitted amount; PCMLTFA administrative monetary penalties can reach CAD 500,000 per violation for a corporation, and laundering the proceeds of foreign corruption is independently prosecutable under s.462.31 of the Criminal Code.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII withholdings under s.227.1 unless they exercised reasonable due diligence; if the failure conceals bribes or sanctions evasion, directors face criminal laundering charges, seizure of personal property under provincial unexplained-wealth orders, travel bans, and imprisonment.
