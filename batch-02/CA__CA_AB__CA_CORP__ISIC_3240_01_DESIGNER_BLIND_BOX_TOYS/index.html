Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including the Business Registration Online portal, My Business Account, and Represent a Client. Alberta corporate income tax administration is coordinated through Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration; Alberta has no general provincial sales tax.

E-government filing portal

A corporation incorporated federally or in Alberta obtains a nine-digit CRA Business Number by registering through Business Registration Online, by telephone, or through the applicable incorporation or provincial registration process where integrated. The corporation then adds the applicable program accounts, including GST/HST, payroll, import-export, and corporation income tax accounts as required. A non-resident entity carrying on business in Canada or making taxable supplies in Canada must determine whether it is required to register for GST/HST. A non-resident may register voluntarily where permitted, subject to security, filing, and documentary requirements. The GST/HST account number is the Business Number followed by the RT program identifier and four-digit account suffix. Importers and exporters generally require an RM import-export program account. A Canadian corporation must also obtain and maintain its corporate income tax account and file T2 corporation income tax returns. Separate provincial corporate registration does not replace CRA registration.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canadian corporations are generally taxed as separate legal entities. Canada has no United States-style check-the-box election and no general entity election converting a corporation into a pass-through entity. A private corporation may qualify as a Canadian-controlled private corporation if statutory ownership and control conditions are satisfied, potentially accessing the small business deduction and other rules; this is a factual tax status rather than an elective classification. Corporations may make specific elections under the Income Tax Act and Excise Tax Act, including certain GST/HST elections, but these do not generally convert the corporation into a fiscally transparent entity. Hybrid mismatch rules can deny deductions or impose inclusion consequences for certain arrangements involving foreign entities or instruments.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding certain supplies, for a person that is not a listed financial institution. The threshold is generally tested over a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters. Registration is required when the relevant threshold conditions are exceeded; voluntary registration may be available below the threshold. A non-resident's Canadian GST/HST obligations depend on its taxable supplies, place-of-supply rules, business activities, and applicable simplified or ordinary registration regime.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Qualifying goods exported from Canada may generally be zero-rated at 0% GST where the supplier delivers or transfers the goods for export in circumstances satisfying the Excise Tax Act and its regulations. The supplier must retain evidence that the goods left Canada and that the transaction meets the applicable export conditions. Goods sold to a non-resident are not automatically zero-rated merely because the purchaser is non-resident. Goods delivered in Canada, including goods supplied to a non-resident who takes possession in Canada, may remain taxable unless a specific zero-rating rule applies. Services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated only where the statutory place-of-supply and export-service conditions are met; exclusions apply, including many services connected with real property, tangible personal property, personal services, litigation, and services performed in Canada. Exporters may claim input tax credits or GST/HST refunds only for properly documented GST/HST incurred in commercial activities and subject to the applicable filing, limitation, and non-resident rules.

  • Canada Border Services Agency export declaration or applicable electronic export reporting record, where required
  • Commercial invoice identifying the supplier, purchaser, goods, value, currency, delivery terms, and destination
  • Packing list and transport documentation, such as bill of lading, airway bill, courier record, or carrier delivery confirmation
  • Proof that the goods were shipped or delivered outside Canada, including carrier tracking, customs export records, or foreign import documentation where available
  • Sales contract, purchase order, customer order, and payment records linking the exported goods to the zero-rated supply
  • GST/HST invoice and accounting records supporting the reported zero-rated sale
  • Import or export permits and product-specific regulatory records where required
  • Foreign exchange or banking records where relevant to corroborate payment; Canada generally does not require an inward remittance certificate as a universal condition for export zero-rating

Canada has no general federal real-time GST invoice-clearance or mandatory e-invoicing clearance system for ordinary business-to-business transactions. GST/HST registrants must issue invoices or other supporting documents containing prescribed information when required, retain adequate books and records, and maintain electronic records in an auditable form. Digital invoices are generally acceptable if their authenticity, integrity, readability, and availability are preserved. CRA electronic filing and payment requirements apply to specified registrants and filing categories, but do not constitute real-time invoice clearance. Import and export data may be transmitted electronically through the Canada Border Services Agency systems. Alberta has no general provincial e-invoice clearance mandate.

Canada does not impose a general statutory requirement to repatriate export proceeds within 180 days, and there is no universal foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for qualifying Canadian exports. CRA may reassess GST/HST where export conditions are not met, documentation is insufficient, the supply was not exported, or a service was incorrectly treated as zero-rated. The reassessment may include the GST/HST that should have been collected, denial or recovery of input tax credits or refunds, arrears interest, and applicable administrative penalties. False statements or omissions may attract penalties, including gross-negligence penalties generally calculated at the greater of CAD 500 or 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit. Repeated or intentional non-compliance may result in prosecution under the Excise Tax Act or Criminal Code, including fines or imprisonment. The normal reassessment period is generally four years for GST/HST, subject to extended or unlimited periods in cases involving misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, wilful blindness, fraud, or waiver. Amounts improperly refunded or credited may be recovered through reassessment and collection proceedings.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada affords full current-account and capital-account convertibility of the Canadian dollar. Both equity injections and operating cash flows (trade receivables, platform settlements) can be freely converted into CAD without exchange-control approval. There are no restrictions on repatriating profit, repaying intercompany debt, or remitting royalties/dividends, subject solely to Income Tax Act (Canada) Part XIII withholding: dividends 25% statutory (treaty-reduced to 5–15%, e.g., 5% direct holdings under Canada–US treaty); arm's-length interest on term loans effectively exempt from withholding (non-participating debt); industrial royalties 25% statutory (treaty-reduced to 0–10%). No capital tax on foreign currency receipts; no mandatory hedging or conversion requirements exist for non-resident shareholders or inbound wire transfers.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory obligation to surrender, repatriate, or convert export proceeds in Canada. Exporters (including retail toy distributors selling via Shopify/Stripe) may maintain foreign-denominated bank balances indefinitely. However, FINTRAC imposes an electronic funds transfer report (EFTR) threshold of CAD $10,000 for any international wire; cross-border currency declarations apply above CAD $10,000 under the Customs Act; and banks apply dual-use trade control screening under EIPA. No minimum conversion ratio is imposed; CAD floatation fully accommodates USD/CAD settlement.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as the sole issuing authority for the Canadian dollar under the Bank of Canada Act. There is no dedicated Foreign Exchange Administration; prudential oversight of deposit-taking institutions rests with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), while anti-money-laundering supervision and cross-border transactional monitoring are enforced by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) pursuant to the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Canada adheres to IMF Article VIII, maintaining no exchange restrictions on payments for current international transactions.

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium (3/5). Privileged non-bank EMI solutions (Wise Business, Payoneer, Stripe Treasury, Mercury US) are viable as complementary collection and settlement channels but cannot replace a Canadian Schedule I bank for statutory escrow, FX derivative execution, or trade finance. Legal constraints: EMIs must be registered as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC and may not accept 'deposits' as defined under the Bank Act (Canada); funds are not CDIC-insured; EMIs cannot issue commercial letters of credit. Use fintechs only for marketplace payouts (< CAD $10,000 per transaction thresholds) and keep the main capital account at a chartered bank to preserve corporate governance and audit trail integrity.
