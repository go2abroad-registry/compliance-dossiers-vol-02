Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC). Primary portal: CRA My Business Account (mybusinessaccount-services-compteprofessionnels-dentreprises.cra-arc.gc.ca) and Business Registration Online (BRO) portal administered by Canada Revenue Agency and Service Canada. Provincial corporate registry: Alberta Corporate Registry / Service Alberta (corporate-registry.servicealberta.ca).

E-government filing portal

For a non-resident corporation establishing an Alberta business: (1) Incorporate federally or extra-provincially register under the Alberta Business Corporations Act via the Corporate Registry, obtaining an Alberta Corporation Access Number; (2) Complete CRA Business Number (BN) application through BRO using Form RC1 or RC1513 (GST/HST and corporate income tax registration for non-residents); (3) Elect for GST/HST program account with stated effective registration date; (4) Appoint an Alberta physical agent / permanent establishment representative if non-resident; (5) Obtain required program accounts such as GST/HST (RC), corporate income tax (RM), and payroll (RP) if hiring Canadian employees; (6) Register within 15 business days of the CRA notice or immediately upon exceeding registration thresholds; validation of non-Canadian investment by registering under the Investment Canada Act may also be triggered.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation: A federal or Alberta business corporation is a separate taxable Canadian corporation under s. 89(1) of the Income Tax Act (Canada) and is taxed at the entity level — no flow-through or partnership transparency. Subject to Part I federal corporate income tax and Alberta provincial corporate tax (Alberta general rate 8%, plus federal 15%, combined 23% approximate headline; small business deduction may reduce combined rate on first CAD 500,000 qualifying business income if Canadian-controlled). A non-resident-owned Canadian branch corporation is further exposed to Part XIV branch tax and withholding rules. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: A federal or Alberta business corporation is a separate taxable Canadian corporation under s. 89(1) of the Income Tax Act (Canada) and is taxed at the entity level — no flow-through or partnership transparency. Subject to Part I federal corporate income tax and Alberta provincial corporate tax (Alberta general rate 8%, plus federal 15%, combined 23% approximate headline; small business deduction may reduce combined rate on first CAD 500,000 qualifying business income if Canadian-controlled). A non-resident-owned Canadian branch corporation is further exposed to Part XIV branch tax and withholding rules. Territorial Scope: Canada taxes worldwide income of resident corporations; the Alberta allocation is determined by the T2 provincial allocation formula based on permanent establishment and gross revenue/payroll factors.

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a US-style check-the-box classification regime. Available elections of relevance: (1) Section 216 Income Tax Act election for non-residents to file net income tax on certain Canadian property income instead of withholding tax; (2) Section 216.1 election (foreign pass-through entity, e.g., US LLC) to determine Canadian taxation of the entity or its members; (3) Section 85(1) rollover election to transfer assets into a corporation on a tax-deferred basis; (4) Section 256(2.1) or equivalent elections affecting Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) status and the small business deduction; (5) GST/HST elections: s. 156 election for annual filing, s. 150 agent/principal election, s. 178.8 streamlined non-resident supplier registration, and s. 167(2) election for closely related corporations; (6) Hybrid mismatch elections under amended s. 18.4 and rules denying deductions for hybrid arrangements — election to apply alternative deferred deduction treatment in certain cross-border structures.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the small supplier threshold under ETA s. 240(1)(b). Registration becomes mandatory once total worldwide taxable revenue (excluding zero-rated supplies) from carrying on business in Canada exceeds CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Exceeding the threshold into a second qualifying quarter makes registration mandatory effective on the first day of the month following the first quarter in which the threshold was exceeded; voluntary registration is immediately available for exporters, and an exporter that charges no GST may still register to recover ITCs.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available to the manufactured goods wholesale or retail exporter when all following conditions are met: (1) the supply is a sale of tangible personal property made by way of export; (2) the goods are physically exported by the supplier, by the buyer, or by the supplier's agent in direct shipment to a destination outside Canada; (3) possession of the goods is transferred outside Canada within 60 days of the time of supply; or delivery is made to a common carrier, customs broker, or freight forwarder for export within 60 days of the time of supply; (4) the exporter obtains proof of export, e.g., a customs B13A export declaration, bill of lading, waybill, or customs officer certification, within two years of the time of supply; (5) the goods remain for use outside Canada and are not returned to Canada for consumption. Cosplay prop apparel, synthetic wigs, and theatrical costume robes manufactured in Alberta and sold to foreign retail buyers through SHOPIFY or STRIPE qualify for zero-rating where payment is received from the non-resident buyer and the goods leave Canada through international mail, courier, or freight channels. Ancillary services tied to export zero-rating include design and fitting services supplied exclusively to a non-resident, provided the benefit is realized entirely outside Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration: B13A Export Declaration filed through the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) Canada Export Reporting System (CERS) for each shipment exceeding CAD 2,000. For low-value courier or postal exports below CAD 2,000, the exporter must instead retain the courier manifest, postal customs entry, or equivalent proof of export from the carrier.
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill: Original or electronic carrier document (ocean bill of lading, airway bill, international courier airway bill, or road freight waybill) showing the consignee name, destination country, port of exit, shipping date, and description of goods matching the commercial invoice; for US cross-border transfers, a Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) highway export manifest or a US Customs import entry is also retained.
  • Inward Remittance and FX Settlement Evidence: bank SWIFT MT103 receipt, Stripe payout settlement report, or Shopify Payments payout statement showing receipt of foreign currency from the non-resident buyer, including the conversion to Canadian dollars and the transaction date; this serves as Canada's functional equivalent of the inward remittance certificate.

Canada has no real-time fiscal clearance, government pre-approval, or mandatory digital stamping of electronic invoices. However, CRA requires that all GST/HST invoice records be electronically retained in linked failure-proof format. Voluntary electronic invoicing is permitted (PDF, EDI, or API-submitted), and the Canadian federal government has adopted the Pan-Canadian PEPPOL e-invoice standard for suppliers selling to federal departments, but it is not mandatory for commercial B2B exports. The CRA accepts GST/HST returns through GST/HST NETFILE, via EDI, or via REST/SOAP web services and the GST/HST Internet File Transfer (GIFT) API; all return filing must be fully digital for registered corporations. Export invoices must show the supplier BN, invoice date, total consideration, and the 0% zero-rating reference; electronic records must be sufficient to prove export within Canada's two-year documentation window.

The Canada Revenue Agency subjects export zero-rating claims to targeted audit verification of the complete export trail — B13A/CERS report, transport document, airline/courier tracking, and the foreign currency payment record. If the exporter cannot produce contemporaneous evidence of export within two years of the time of supply, the CRA will deny zero-rating and reassess the full gross revenue at 5% GST. Penalties and clawback exposure include: (1) arrears interest on the unreported GST from the original filing due date; (2) s. 280(1) penalty equal to 5% of the net tax omission, increasing by 1% per full month for up to 25% of the omitted amount; (3) gross-negligence penalty of 25% under s. 285.1 where false statements or omissions were made knowingly or under circumstances of gross negligence; (4) criminal prosecution under ETA s. 239(1) for wilful evasion, punishable by fines of up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment of up to five years; (5) tax evasion penalties under the Income Tax Act for linked corporate income tax positions; (6) extension of the normal four-year reassessment period to ten years where misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, wilful default, or fraud exists (ETA s. 298/ITA s. 152(4)(a)); and (7) director's liability under ETA s. 323 — directors may be assessed personally for the corporation's unremitted GST, penalties and interest for the denial of export zero-rating. Any ITCs claimed on inputs consumed in the falsely zero-rated export are simultaneously reversed, and the total GST clawback is payable together with the corresponding Alberta income tax adjustment if the export sale was also misreported.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a freely floating, fully convertible CAD under a liberalised capital-account framework. Both current-account (trade, royalty, licensing and service payments) and capital-account transactions (foreign equity injections, shareholder loans, profit repatriation, liquidation distributions) are permitted without prior currency-authorisation or case-by-case converter restrictions. Dividend and branch-remittance remittances are subject only to income-tax withholding procedures and corporate attestation of undistributed surplus; there are no exchange-control gating requirements. For cosplay prop apparel manufacturing, the repatriation of net earnings to a non-resident shareholder is unrestricted and may be settled in CAD, USD or any deliverable currency via a Canadian chartered bank.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada does not impose any statutory timeline or mandatory conversion ratio for export proceeds. Export receipts may be held, reinvested, or converted freely in any currency. Nonetheless, all incoming electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported by the receiving Canadian financial institution to FINTRAC, and the exporter must maintain underlying commercial invoices, bills of lading, and customs entry declarations for a five-year record retention period to evidence the current-account nature of the receipt.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central monetary authority; Canada maintains no exchange-control authority. Foreign-exchange-related compliance is administered through the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) and overseen by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), while the Minister of Finance retains reserve-management functions under the Currency and Bank of Canada Acts.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility: LIMITED, viable only as a non-primary payment facilitation rail. Canada does not recognise the EU-style Electronic Money Institution (EMI) passport; cross-border digital money operators (e.g. Wise, Stripe, Shopify Payments, Payoneer) must be registered as Money Service Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC and, in Alberta, may require a provincial money-lenders/check-casher or MSB licence under the Consumer Protection Act. A non-resident EMI cannot accept deposit-taking business under the Bank Act and therefore cannot hold escrow, capital-injection or statutory corporate funds. For a cosplay prop apparel manufacturer, an EMI may be used to collect e-commerce sales or make low-value supplier payments, but the principal treasury accounts and all capital-related clearing must remain with a Schedule I chartered bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Non-resident capital injection must be routed via protected SWIFT MT103 or equivalent interbank payment to the Canadian receiving bank, with the payer using the clear business purpose description 'Equity subscription / paid-up capital into CA_CORP under the Alberta Business Corporations Act'. The receiving bank will apply the FINTRAC large EFT reporting threshold (CAD 10,000 or more) and will require the company to furnish its incorporation number, bank account reference, a signed board resolution authorising the new share issuance, and an executed subscription agreement. At the clearing level, inbound Canadian-dollar wires settle through Payments Canada ACSS on an intraday basis; USD wires settle through the New York Chips/FedWire corridors and are converted to CAD at the receiving bank's spot rate or booked as USD treasury balances. The capital infusion must be recorded as a direct investment credit in Canada's international transactions reporting system, and the entity should preserve source-of-funds documents, including a confirmation of no ML/TF indicia, for at least five years.Trade payments to overseas suppliers of fabrics, synthetic wig fibres, fasteners and packaging are settled predominantly by SWIFT MT103 telegraphic transfers for open-account purchases and by documentary collections when shipping documents are released against payment or acceptance. For larger commissioned batches to US licensed costume distributors, commercial letters of credit (confirmed, irrevocable at sight) with bill-of-lading terms are recommended; these may be issued by the relationship Schedule I bank under an approved trade-limit facility. Domestic supplier payments can be cleared via Payments Canada's Real-Time Rail (RTR) or ACSS batch clearing. All outbound wires over CAD 10,000 attract FINTRAC large EFT reporting and must progress through the bank's automated sanctions-screening; the remittance instruction must include detailed line items (invoice numbers, HS codes, commercial terms) to avoid false positives from the description 'cosplay prop apparel' which may inadvertently match embargoed-prop keywords.Vanilla delivery-out CAD/USD forward contracts (one-month to twelve-month tenors) with a Tier 1 Schedule I bank, which are fully acceptable under OSFI's Risk Management and Internal Controls requirements.Vanilla CAD/USD currency options (call/put) used as cost-effective downside protection on trade settlements.Cross-currency basis swaps between CAD and USD or EUR for matching long-term inventory funding and foreign-currency receivables.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) are permissible only for counterparties whose currency lacks a full deliverable onshore market, and are not standard for USD/CAD or EUR/CAD because the Canadian and US markets are fully deliverable.Spot conversions and pre-export FX pre-sales hedging when the underlying trade invoice has been received and documented, subject to the bank's client-categorisation and sophisticated-investor eligibility limits for the cosplay prop apparel manufacturing entity.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% (combined federal 15% + Alberta 8%)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under subsection 212(2) of the Income Tax Act on gross dividends paid to non-residents; treaty-reduced rates may apply, e.g., 15% for portfolio dividends and 5% for corporate shareholders controlling at least 10% voting ownership under Canada-U.S. and other DTA agreements.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File: mandatory when aggregate cross-border transactions with non-arm's-length non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000 in a fiscal year; Master File: required for multinational enterprise groups with consolidated group revenue exceeding CAD 1 billion (aligned with CbC report threshold of EUR 750 million).

Economic substance scope: Offshore Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) are not domestically applicable in Canada. However, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245 ITA) permits recharacterization of transactions lacking economic substance; s.163.2 penalizes misrepresentation, and treaty relief requires beneficial ownership and substance. Tax authorities can disregard sham entities or transactions that artificially shift profits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Willful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Civil penalty of 50% of the tax evaded under s.163(2); criminal conviction under s.239(1) carries a fine of between 100% and 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and/or imprisonment up to 5 years; plus interest and compounded arrears.

Director personal liability: Directors who consented, procured, or acquiesced in the evasion may be jointly and severally liable under s.227.1 for unremitted source deductions; corporate veil pierced for fraudulent conduct; personal criminal liability, asset freeze, or prohibition on leaving Canada may be imposed.
Shell Company / Sham / GAAR Abuse

Statutory sanction: GAAR allows the CRA to deny the tax benefit by recharacterizing the transaction and recalculating tax; penalty under s.163.2 for false statements or omissions; increased tax liability plus daily interest; potential 25% penalty for certain tax shelter arrangements.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly use a sham structure to defraud creditors or evade taxes can be pursued by a liquidator under fraudulent trading; Canadian courts may pierce the corporate veil, leading to personal liability of directors for the company's tax debt; a director may also be disqualified from serving on any Alberta board.
AML / Failure to Implement CDD

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 500,000 for a first violation and CAD 1,000,000 for a subsequent violation; criminal penalties up to CAD 2,000,000 fine and 5 years imprisonment; proceeds may be subject to restraint or forfeiture.

Director personal liability: Directors who direct, participate in, or knowingly fail to prevent money laundering can face personal criminal prosecution; restitution orders and travel bans may follow; the law also permits seizure of directors' assets derived from the offence.
Transfer Pricing Understatement

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing penalty under s.247(4): 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment, in addition to the recomputed federal and Alberta taxes plus interest and punitive late-filing penalties if documentation is absent.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically personally liable for the corporate TP adjustment, but if they signed a false or misleading return knowing the transfer prices were unrealistic, they may face s.163(2) civil penalties; serious involvement could trigger s.239 criminal exposure, leading to personal fines and imprisonment.
