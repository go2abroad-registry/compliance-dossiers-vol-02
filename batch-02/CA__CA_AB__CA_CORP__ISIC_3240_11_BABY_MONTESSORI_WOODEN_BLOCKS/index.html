Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency / Agence du revenu du Canada (CRA) – National tax administration; registration portal: My Business Account and the Canada Business Registry; corporate registration via Corporations Canada and Alberta Corporate Registry.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity must obtain a Business Number (BN) from the CRA, either by completing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts) or, for a federal corporation, the BN is issued automatically upon articles of incorporation. In Alberta, the entity must also complete extra-provincial registration with the Alberta Corporate Registry. Separate CRA program accounts (GST/HST, import/export, payroll) must be activated before taxable commercial activity begins.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — A foreign entity must obtain a Business Number (BN) from the CRA, either by completing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts) or, for a federal corporation, the BN is issued automatically upon articles of incorporation. In Alberta, the entity must also complete extra-provincial registration with the Alberta Corporate Registry. Separate CRA program accounts (GST/HST, import/export, payroll) must be activated before taxable commercial activity begins.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box or entity-classification election analogous to the U.S. Federal corporation is taxable per se at the entity level. Relevant statutory elections include: section 216 election for non-residents with rental/disposition income, section 219 branch tax election for non-resident corporations, and GST/HST sections 156/273 elections for closely related corporations or joint ventures. Hybrid mismatch rules under the Income Tax Act apply to arrangements exploiting double deduction or deduction/non-inclusion outcomes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 – the annual worldwide taxable supply threshold, excluding zero-rated supplies, above which GST/HST registration becomes mandatory for a non-resident carrying on taxable business in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act, goods exported by a GST/HST registrant are zero-rated provided they are exported as soon as reasonably possible after the supply. Services performed for non-residents, and intangible personal property supplied to non-residents, may also be zero-rated when the non-resident is not registered for GST/HST and the supplier satisfies prescribed documentary conditions.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (A8/A6 or G13/G7 as applicable)
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill evidencing shipment from Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank/foreign exchange payment evidence
  • Commercial invoice describing goods and confirming export sale
  • Internal proof-of-export documentation retained per ETA s.169 and Export and Import Permits Act obligations

Canada does not currently mandate real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or continuous e-invoicing for GST/HST. Registered taxpayers must electronically file GST/HST returns through CRA-certified software or the CRA portal. High-volume exporters may use EDI/XML APIs and batch upload systems, and CRA processes GST/HST rebate claims electronically; no transaction-level government clearance or API authorisation is required at the federal level.

The CRA may reassess GST/HST returns within four years (or indefinitely for misrepresentation). A denied zero-rating claim results in retroactive tax, interest, and repayment of input tax credits. Fraudulent export claims attract penalties under section 285 of the Excise Tax Act of up to CAD 250,000 plus up to 25% of the evaded tax, criminal liability, and possible director/officer liability; conviction may also lead to permanent denial of zero-rating privileges.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible and freely floating on both current and capital accounts. There are no exchange-control approvals, no repatriation ceilings, and no restrictions on converting CAD to foreign currency for trade, dividends, interest, royalties, capital subscriptions, or loan repayments. Profit remittance is constrained only by tax compliance, not by foreign-exchange law: non-resident dividends/royalties/interest are subject to Part XIII withholding tax (statutory 25%, generally reduced by treaty), and cross-border non-arm's-length payments must satisfy Canadian transfer-pricing rules. Inbound share capital and shareholder loans may be freely repatriated provided the amounts are bona fide and properly documented; the Canadian tax filer must report certain non-resident transactions using applicable forms such as CRA T106 and issue NR4 slips for withheld amounts.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline for repatriation, surrender, or conversion of export receipts, and there is no mandatory conversion ratio into CAD. Exporters may hold foreign currency in Canadian or offshore accounts indefinitely, book export revenue in CAD for income-tax purposes using the exchange rate prevailing at the relevant time, and convert funds at commercial bank rates as needed. All cross-border electronic funds transfers equal to or above CAD 10,000 are reportable to FINTRAC by financial intermediaries, but this is a reporting obligation, not an exchange-control restriction.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC); prudential regulation and bank licensing by Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing supervision by Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); no centralized foreign-exchange administration exists because Canada maintains an open exchange-control system.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium-High as a payment-disbursement utility, but Low as a primary treasury account. Foreign electronic money institutions (EMIs) are not authorized to accept deposits or operate as banks under the Canadian Bank Act, and they generally do not have CDIC deposit insurance. An offshore EMI may provide multicurrency payment initiation, card acquiring, or conversion services only through a licensed Canadian partner bank. If the EMI acts as a payment service provider, it may require registration under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), supervised by the Bank of Canada. For Stage 4 treasury purposes, the underlying long-term liquidity and capital-escrow balance must reside in a Schedule I bank account; EMI rails may be used only for low-balance disbursements, refunds, e-commerce settlement, and FX conversion convenience.

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined general corporate rate (federal 15% + Alberta 8%); 11% combined small-business rate for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income (federal 9% + Alberta 2%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax under Income Tax Act s.212 on the gross amount of taxable dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident beneficial owner. Under Canada's tax treaties, the rate is commonly reduced to 5% for a beneficial owner holding at least 10% of the voting shares and 15% for portfolio dividends.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The return must be filed within six months after the corporation's taxation year-end under Income Tax Act s.150(1). The balance-due date for unpaid tax is two months after year-end, or three months for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations, under s.157.

Late filing attracts a penalty of 5% of the unpaid federal and Alberta tax, plus 1% for each full month of lateness up to 12 months, resulting in a maximum 17% penalty. A second failure within three years incurs a 10% penalty plus 2% per month up to 20 months. Unpaid balances bear daily compound prescribed interest. Information-return violations, such as failure to file Form T106, may carry additional fixed penalties.

A non-resident with an Alberta permanent establishment and direct e-commerce sales into Alberta must allocate income to Alberta and compute Alberta corporate tax on that allocated income. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Aggregate non-arm's-length remittances to a single non-resident related party exceeding CAD 1,000,000 in the taxation year triggers mandatory Form T106 Information Return filing and a contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation expectation. The CRA may nevertheless request documentation for transactions below that threshold where the facts warrant audit.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations of the type used in some offshore financial centres. Instead, CRA applies the general anti-avoidance rule under ITA s.245, Part XIII 'beneficial owner' tests, and OECD permanent establishment analysis. For a non-resident-owned Alberta company manufacturing Montessori wooden blocks, meaningful substance consists of Canadian employees, tangible production assets, management decisions, bank accounts, and real premises. A letter-box company holding IP or invoicing sales without Canadian substance will be challenged on treaty residence, treaty entitlement, and GAAR grounds.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Willful evasion under Income Tax Act s.239 is punishable by a fine of at least 50% and up to 200% of the evaded tax, imprisonment for up to five years on indictment, or both; Criminal Code fraud for amounts exceeding CAD 5,000 carries up to fourteen years and gross negligence penalties under s.163 ITA can apply at 50% of understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false T2 returns or direct the concealment may be prosecuted as parties to the offence. Courts may order restitution, impose bail conditions including passport surrender, and in serious cases imprisonment. The corporate veil may be pierced where the director is the directing mind of the criminal conduct.
Failure to File and Late Remittance

Statutory sanction: Late filing penalty of 5% of unpaid tax plus 1% per full month up to 12 months; repeated late filing penalty of 10% plus 2% per month up to 20 months. Prescribed interest compounds daily on unpaid tax. Failure to file an information return can trigger additional fixed penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable under ITA s.227.1 for unremitted employee source deductions and under s.323 of the Excise Tax Act for unremitted GST/HST. CRA can pursue directors' personal bank accounts and assets without first exhausting the corporation.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess using the arm's-length amount under ITA s.247(2). Penalties under s.247(3)(a) include a 10% penalty on the net transfer-pricing adjustment, plus CAD 24,000 for repeated failure to file Form T106 or lack of documentation. Gross negligence penalties can also be stacked.

Director personal liability: Directors who personally sign false transfer-pricing documentation or who instruct staff to conceal contemporaneous studies may be assessed as participants in a misrepresentation. A director who is also the sole or controlling shareholder may be subject to civil restitution in an anti-avoidance reassessment where assets are transferred to the shareholder without consideration.
Shell Company and Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: Disallowance of the 'tax benefit' under GAAR (ITA s.245), denial of treaty benefits for lack of beneficial ownership, reversal of Part XIII withholding exemptions, and reassessment of additional federal and Alberta tax plus interest. Culpable-conduct gross-negligence penalties may be applied.

Director personal liability: Directors who certify false Canadian residence, sign false declarations of effective management, or issue invoices for artificial intercompany transactions can be personally named in a civil claim by the CRA where they knowingly participated. Travel restrictions and criminal liability may arise under fraud or false-pretences provisions.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Unlawful Cross-Border Money Movement

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act can reach significant amounts for missing reports, and Criminal Code s.462.31 imposes imprisonment for up to ten years for laundering the proceeds of crime.

Director personal liability: Directors who are the directing mind of a laundering scheme face personal criminal liability. FINTRAC compliance officers and directors who recklessly fail to implement controls may also be held personally liable where they knowingly allow the corporate vehicle to be used for financial crime.
