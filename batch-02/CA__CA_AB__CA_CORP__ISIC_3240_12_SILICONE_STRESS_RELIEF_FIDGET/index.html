Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — GST/HST and corporate income tax administration; online portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-business-account.html); Alberta provincial tax administration for corporate income tax is delegated to the CRA under the Alberta Corporate Tax Act and Tax Administration Agreement, with no separate provincial sales tax in Alberta

E-government filing portal

A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada or Alberta must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA before registering for GST/HST, payroll, or corporate income tax. Registration may be completed online through CRA My Business Account, by mail using Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number), or by telephone. The BN is a 9-digit identifier with program account suffixes (e.g., RC0001 for GST/HST, RM0001 for corporate income tax). Foreign corporations without a Canadian presence may register for GST/HST using the CRA's non-resident registration process, which requires the entity's legal name, registered address, foreign tax identification number, and description of Canadian activities. Where the entity has a permanent establishment in Alberta, the corporate income tax account is registered with the CRA and taxes are paid to the Receiver General for Canada under the Alberta corporate tax collection agreement.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a Canadian business corporation incorporated federally or under the Alberta Business Corporations Act is a separate taxable person subject to Part I federal corporate income tax and Alberta provincial corporate income tax under Part 6.0 of the Alberta Corporate Tax Act; for GST/HST purposes, the corporation is a separate registrant if its worldwide taxable supplies exceed the $30,000 small supplier threshold; Alberta has no provincial retail sales tax, and the default classification is not a pass-through entity for income tax purposes, although a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) enjoys small business deduction eligibility subject to passive income limits.

Available election forms

Under the Income Tax Act (Canada), no general check-the-box election exists for federal or provincial corporate classification; a business corporation is mandatorily taxed at the entity level. Available statutory elections include: (i) the s. 256(2) associated corporations election to allocate the federal small business deduction among associated CCPCs; (ii) the s. 89(11) election to deem a corporation to be a CCPC; (iii) the s. 142.2 and s. 142.3 elections for mark-to-market treatment of specified equity interests or debt; (iv) the s. 152(4)(b) election for loss carry-back; (v) the GST/HST s. 156 election for closely related corporations to treat supplies between them as made for nil consideration; (vi) the GST/HST s. 167 election for a taxable sale of a business as a going concern; and (vii) the GST/HST streamlined registration election under s. 240(2.1) for non-resident registrants with minimal Canadian nexus. For hybrid mismatch purposes, Canada's rules under s. 18.2 and s. 18.21 are mandatory and do not provide elective treatment, although certain designated-entity elections under the applicable anti-hybrid regulations may be made with CRA advance consent.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated exports) over any trailing four consecutive calendar quarters, as determined under s. 148 of the Excise Tax Act; a non-resident is required to register if it makes taxable supplies in Canada or has a permanent establishment in Canada and exceeds the threshold; for a foreign entity whose only Canadian activities are zero-rated exports, registration remains voluntary; once registered, the registrant must remain registered for at least one year

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under the Excise Tax Act applies when the supplier provides tangible personal property by way of sale and the goods are exported from Canada as part of the supply, or when the supply is made to a non-resident for delivery outside Canada, provided the supplier maintains evidence of export (s. 1 of Part V, Schedule VI). Manufacturing and design services performed on goods destined for export may be zero-rated under s. 20 of Part V, Schedule VI. Supplies of intangible personal property (e.g., IP or software) to a non-resident may be zero-rated under s. 21 or s. 21.1 of Part V, Schedule VI where the recipient's use is wholly outside Canada. Food-grade silicone stress-relief fidgets classified under ISIC 3240 will be treated as tangible personal property for zero-rating; the exporter must obtain and retain records evidencing that the goods were exported within one month after the supply, including for drop-shipping scenarios and FOB export sales. Services relating to export promotion or support are zero-rated only where the recipient is a non-resident and no taxable supply of the service is acquired by a resident in Canada for consumption in Canada.

  • Canada Customs Invoice (CCI) or commercial invoice with complete exporter and consignee details
  • Canada Export Declaration (B13A) filed via the Canadian Automated Export Declaration (CAED) or the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) — mandatory for goods with a value exceeding CAD 2,000
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill evidencing shipment from a Canadian port or border crossing

  • Freight forwarder's certificate of export and any applicable insurance certificate showing the goods left Canada

Canada does not currently mandate a real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or continuous transaction control (CTC) model for GST/HST; electronic invoicing is permitted but not universally required. A GST/HST registrant must issue a valid tax invoice containing mandatory disclosure elements (vendor name, BN, GST/HST registration number, invoice date, itemized taxable supplies and GST rate, and total amount) within prescribed timeframes; invoices may be transmitted electronically via EDI, API, PDF, or XML, subject to CRA record-keeping requirements under s. 286 of the Excise Tax Act (six years). No API pre-clearance or government e-invoice clearance platform is in force; the CRA may issue digital-asset compliance requests and audits via My Business Account, and large filers must file electronically (GST/HST NETFILE or GST/HST TelegFILE) depending on annual taxable supplies; third-party electronic invoicing platforms are not required to connect to CRA in real time.

CRA may deny zero-rating retroactively where export evidence is incomplete or the goods are later redirected back to Canada, resulting in reassessment of GST/HST at 5%, plus interest under s. 280 of the Excise Tax Act and penalties under s. 280.1 (10% of unremitted amount) or gross negligence penalties under s. 285.1 (up to 50% of additional tax payable for false statements or omissions made knowingly or under circumstances amounting to culpable neglect). Where zero-rating is applied fraudulently or as part of a fictitious export scheme, CRA may invoke the general anti-avoidance rule under s. 274 and the criminal provisions of s. 327 of the Excise Tax Act, with fines up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment up to five years for tax evasion. For income tax purposes, uncollected export receivables remaining outstanding for more than 180 days may trigger a deemed low-interest or imputed interest adjustment under s. 80.4 of the Income Tax Act (Canada) and foreign affiliate loan provisions, and CRA will generally disallow input tax credits (ITCs) claimed on inputs where the corresponding zero-rated export evidence is missing, with a 15-year limitation period for audit reassessment in cases of misrepresentation; any refund claimed under s. 261 of the Excise Tax Act may be subject to interest clawback and a specific penalty equal to 5% of the overstated refund or credit plus 1% per month of neglect up to 20% of the total.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a floating exchange rate regime with full convertibility on both current and capital accounts. No exchange controls or approvals required for profit remittances, dividend payments, repayment of intercompany debt, or capital repatriation. The Canadian dollar (CAD) trades freely in global markets. Withholding taxes on dividends, interest, and royalties apply under the Income Tax Act but do not restrict conversion.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no requirement to surrender export proceeds or to convert foreign currency receipts to CAD. Exporters may retain earnings in any currency in domestic or foreign bank accounts, subject only to FINTRAC reporting for cross-border transactions above CAD 10,000 and general anti-money laundering obligations.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as central monetary authority; foreign exchange rate and monetary policy under the Bank of Canada Act. International capital flows and trade reporting administered by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Moderate for standalone cross-border operations. Canadian fintech and neo-banks (e.g., Tangerine, Wealthsimple Cash) are chartered under bank or trust company regulations and do not act as electronic money institutions (EMIs) in the EU sense. Payment service providers must register as Money Service Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC and may access SWIFT through partner banks, but they generally lack direct SWIFT, LC, and FX hedging capabilities. For high-volume trade flows, a Tier 1 bank relationship is the preferred legal and operational route.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal plus 8% Alberta. A Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally qualify for an 11% combined small-business rate: 9% federal plus 2% Alberta, subject to the applicable business-limit rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to a valid income-tax treaty or other statutory relief. Treaty rates commonly reduce dividends to 5% for qualifying corporate shareholders meeting ownership conditions and 15% for other portfolio dividends. The Canada-United States treaty generally provides 5% for qualifying corporate ownership and 15% otherwise.

A Canadian-controlled private corporation meeting the statutory conditions may have a three-month balance-payment deadline, but the T2 filing deadline remains six months.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is unnecessary. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires contemporaneous documentation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. Form T106 reporting generally applies where the aggregate reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1 million, subject to detailed rules. A Master File and Local File are not generally prescribed as separate OECD-format filings for all Canadian taxpayers, although equivalent contemporaneous documentation is expected. Country-by-Country reporting generally applies to an in-scope multinational enterprise group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance remains relevant under the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty principal-purpose or anti-abuse provisions, sham and beneficial-ownership principles, transfer-pricing rules, interest limitation rules, foreign-affiliate rules, and specific anti-avoidance provisions. The corporation should maintain real commercial functions, decision-making, personnel, premises, risk control, assets, records, and appropriately priced transactions in Alberta and Canada. A conduit or shell arrangement may be challenged even if formal legal documentation exists.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraud

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, conviction may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to two years, in addition to reassessment, interest, civil penalties, and collection action. Fraud under the Criminal Code may carry imprisonment of up to 14 years where the statutory elements and amount thresholds are met.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal liability where they authorized, acquiesced in, or participated in the conduct. Directors may also be assessed personally for unremitted source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts under statutory director-liability provisions. Civil restitution and asset seizure may apply. A travel ban is not an automatic tax penalty but may arise from bail conditions, arrest warrants, or court orders.
False Statements, Gross Negligence, and Tax Return Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: A gross-negligence penalty is generally 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit attributable to the false statement or omission, in addition to tax and prescribed interest. Late filing and installment penalties may apply separately.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally exposed where they directed or knowingly permitted false filings. Professional, accounting, and corporate records can be compelled or audited. Imprisonment generally requires a criminal offense and proof beyond the civil assessment standard.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may make a transfer-pricing adjustment under section 247, deny or reduce deductions, impute income, apply a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory documentation and reasonable-efforts conditions are not met, and charge interest.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable for a corporate transfer-pricing assessment solely because an adjustment was made. Personal exposure may arise for fraud, knowing participation, obstruction, or director-liability taxes. The corporation may also face treaty competent-authority disputes and double taxation.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Non-Resident Tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be liable for the unremitted 25% statutory amount, applicable penalties, prescribed interest, and information-return penalties. CRA may deny treaty relief where beneficial ownership, residence, entitlement, or anti-abuse requirements are not satisfied.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for certain unremitted source deductions and related amounts where the statutory director-liability conditions are met. Criminal prosecution is possible for deliberate evasion or falsification.
Payroll and GST/HST Remittance Failure

Statutory sanction: Assessments may include unremitted deductions, employer contributions, GST/HST, penalties, interest, and gross-negligence penalties. Repeated or deliberate failures may lead to criminal charges.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for unremitted source deductions and certain GST/HST liabilities under the Income Tax Act and Excise Tax Act. Criminal fines and imprisonment may apply for deliberate conduct.
Shell Company, Beneficial Ownership, and Corporate Record Failure

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include corporate-law penalties, tax reassessment under anti-avoidance doctrines, denial of treaty benefits, AML reporting and enforcement, administrative monetary penalties, asset restraint, and criminal prosecution for fraud or laundering.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal civil and criminal exposure for knowingly false records, obstruction, fraud, or money laundering. Imprisonment can apply under the Criminal Code and Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Travel restrictions are possible only where imposed by a court or connected to criminal proceedings.
AML, Proceeds of Crime, and Sanctions

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties can be substantial and may reach up to CAD 1 million for a violation, depending on the violation and classification. Criminal money-laundering offenses may carry imprisonment of up to 10 years on indictment; sanctions offenses may carry additional fines and imprisonment under applicable Canadian sanctions legislation.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct, authorize, consent to, or participate in prohibited conduct may be prosecuted personally. Corporate indemnification does not eliminate criminal liability. Asset forfeiture, account restraint, and court-ordered restrictions may apply.
Import, Product Classification, and Customs Valuation

Statutory sanction: The Canada Border Services Agency may reassess duties and taxes, impose administrative monetary penalties, seize goods, suspend importing privileges, and pursue prosecution under customs legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal criminal liability for knowing customs fraud or obstruction. Civil corporate assessments generally remain with the importer of record unless a statutory personal-liability or criminal basis exists.
Record Retention and Obstruction

Statutory sanction: The corporation may face record-keeping penalties, adverse assessments, disallowed deductions, repeated audit action, contempt-related consequences, and criminal prosecution for obstruction or destruction of evidence.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize destruction or obstruction may incur personal criminal exposure, fines, and imprisonment. Courts may impose production, preservation, or restraint orders.
