Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not offer the U.S. check-the-box regime; a Canadian Business Corporation and a foreign registered corporation carrying on business in Canada are always separate taxable entities. Available statutory elections relevant to group structures include: (i) section 156 GST/HST election for closely related corporations to net inter-company taxable supplies; (ii) elective capital cost allowance and depreciation rates under the Income Tax Regulations; (iii) joint venture GST/HST elections under section 273; (iv) Alberta corporate allocation election for permanent establishment provincial allocation; and (v) non-resident corporation branch tax election under subsection 219(4) to use taxable income computed as a Canadian corporation instead of branch tax. Hybrid mismatch treatment is governed by Canada’s section 18.4 and section 18.3 rules, with no generic pass-through election.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains unrestricted convertibility on both current and capital account items under its IMF Article VIII obligations (accepted 1945) and operates without a general foreign exchange control regime; the Foreign Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951, and no central-bank approval or licensing gate applies to current or capital transfers. Profit remittance and dividend distributions are entirely free of FX control hurdles; the only pre-wire compliance conditions are those imposed by tax law, namely Part XIII withholding tax (Rate: 25% statutory, generally reduced under the Canada-US Treaty or other applicable bilateral treaty) on dividends, interest, royalties, and certain management-fee transfers, plus the reporting obligations of the recipient Canadian entity under the Income Tax Act (T106 transfer-pricing return, T1135 foreign-property report, T1134 foreign-affiliate information return where applicable). No exchange-rate conversion quota or rationing applies to any corporate account.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory export-proceeds surrender obligation, no mandatory inbound-repatriation timeline, and no required conversion ratio for foreign-currency export receipts under Canadian federal law or Alberta provincial law. Export proceeds may remain indefinitely in a designated foreign-currency account at a Schedule I bank and may be hedged or converted at the corporate treasury's discretion. Nevertheless, every physical export of intraocular acrylic lenses must be reported to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) through a valid export declaration (B13A), properly classified under HS 9001.30; the Canadian exporting corporation must also maintain transfer-pricing documentation for intercompany sales under section 247 of the Income Tax Act. For FINTRAC purposes, receipt of any single incoming wire of CAD 10,000 or more is reported by the depository bank via an Electronic Funds Transfer Report (EFTR), and currency transactions above CAD 10,000 require a Large Cash Transaction Report (LCTR).

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC) in coordination with the Department of Finance Canada (Minister of Finance) and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI). The BoC administers the Exchange Fund Account and conducts monetary policy; OSFI prudentially supervises all Canadian Schedule I banks under the Bank Act (Canada); FINTRAC administers AML/CFT and cross-border transaction reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) and associated Regulations.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Conditional Medium. Canada does not issue the European-style Electronic Money Institution (EMI) licence; foreign digital payment entities must instead register as Money Service Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC and maintain a Canadian-domiciled settlement relationship. Wise, Payoneer, and Stripe Treasury can provide low-value cross-border disbursement corridors and USD/CAD receivables conversion, but they cannot issue commercial Letters of Credit, cannot clear CAD transactions through Lynx (Payments Canada RTGS), and generally segment or decline accounts serving high-risk medical-device exporters. The recommended architecture is therefore a segregated fintech corridor for small supplier disbursements with all LC, large capital flows, and derivative operations confined to a Schedule I bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound shareholder capital contributions must be executed via SWIFT MT103 (or an ISO 20022 pacs.008 message) using the ISO 20022 Purpose Code 'DINV' (Direct Investment) or 'CAPC' (Capital Contribution), with the ultimate originator and beneficiary fully identified in field 50 and field 59. The receiving Schedule I bank will automatically file a FINTRAC EFTR for any wire of CAD 10,000 or more. The Canadian corporation must maintain source-of-funds documentation, issue share certificates, complete a T106 transfer-pricing return where the subscription involves a non-arm's-length foreign subscriber, and report any foreign-held property or affiliate interests via T1135/T1134 as applicable. Where the investment value crosses the Investment Canada Act (ICA) enterprise-value threshold for a Canadian-controlled medical-device business, the foreign direct investor must file the appropriate ICA Notification or Application for Review before completion. Funds arriving in USD should be cleared through the bank's US correspondent (e.g., via Fedwire or CHIPS) and converted into CAD at commercial rate, or retained in a USD sub-ledger for later trade disbursements.Outbound settlement must use a layered rail strategy: (i) for CAD-denominated supplier and receivable settlements, clear via Payments Canada's Lynx (RTGS) for amounts above the multilateral-truncation threshold and via ACSS for lower-value cheques and pre-authorized debits; (ii) for USD-denominated global supplier and logistics payments, use SWIFT MT103 carried through the bank's US clearing correspondent over Fedwire or CHIPS; (iii) for high-value capital-equipment purchases, issue an irrevocable documentary Letter of Credit (MT700) at sight or usance, preferably confirmed by a prime bank in the seller's jurisdiction; (iv) for recurring intercompany settlements, use SWIFT MT101/MT103 with netting agreements and treasury-center documentation; and (v) for critical-sterilization and tooling vendors, a Standby Letter of Credit (SBLC, MT760) may be posted as performance security. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23.0% combined (federal 15.0% general corporate rate + Alberta 8.0% provincial general corporate rate); effective 11.0% on the first C$500,000 of active business income for a qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC), allocated between federal 9.0% and Alberta 2.0%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% for dividends paid to non-resident shareholders under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act; rate may be reduced to 5% by the Canada-US tax treaty for a shareholder owning at least 10% of the corporation's voting shares, or 15% under other comprehensive DTAs; no DTA relief applies without a valid residence certificate.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T2 return must be filed within 6 months after the fiscal year-end; any balance of tax due is payable between 2 and 3 months after fiscal year-end (2 months for non-CCPCs and CCPCs not claiming SBD; 3 months for CCPCs eligible for SBD), with instalment payments for larger corporations under Parts I and VI.1

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate covered transactions per fiscal year with non-resident related parties triggering mandatory contemporaneous local-file documentation; master file and country-by-country (CbCR) reporting only required for multinational enterprise groups with consolidated group revenue of C$900,000,000 or more (aligned to EUR 750m OECD threshold).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a UAE-style Economic Substance Regulation, but the Income Tax Act imposes economic-substance conditions through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s. 245), which permits the CRA to recharacterize transactions that are artificial and lack a bona fide business purpose. Transfer-pricing rules in s. 247 require transactional net tangible and intangible substance, and a related-party invoice for intraocular lens manufacturing will be disregarded if the offshore supplier or licensor lacks employees, physical assets, or risks to bear. CRA also applies the treaty shopping provisions (OECD Multilateral Instrument Article 29) to deny treaty benefits to an intermediate licensee that exists principally to reduce Canadian withholding tax.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Income Tax Act s. 239)

Statutory sanction: On summary conviction: fine between 50% and 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, and imprisonment for up to 2 years; on indictment: fine up to 200% of tax evaded and imprisonment for up to 5 years. CRA may also assess a civil 50% gross-negligence penalty (s. 163) plus interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who directed, authorised, or acquiesced in the evasion may be jointly and severally liable for the corporation's unpaid tax and penalties under the Common Law trusts and the CBCA/ABCA's liability framework; they may be indicted individually for conspiracy to evade tax, placed under a travel ban pending trial, and sentenced to imprisonment; the CRA may also impound their personal passports under Canada's criminal proceedings and freeze personal assets through the proceeds-of-crime regime.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing and Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: The CRA may reprice all non-arm's-length cross-border contracts, increasing taxable income to the arm's-length amount under s. 247(2); a 10% penalty is imposed on any net upward transfer-pricing adjustment under s. 247(3); interest at the prescribed 8%-plus rate is charged retroactively; failure to file CbCR can trigger a C$2,000 per-month penalty of CRA assessment or a monetary assessment up to C$500,000.

Director personal liability: Directors who executed the inflated invoices with knowledge of the imbalance can be held personally liable as tax officers for the 10% transfer-pricing penalty if they filed the T2 return, under the 'responsible signor' doctrine; in egregious cases, they face charges of fraudulent statements under the Criminal Code (s. 380) with penalties of up to C$10,000,000 fine and 14 years of imprisonment; the OBCA/ABCA allows CRA to pierce the corporate veil when the arrangement was a sham, attaching personal assets and prohibiting the directors from leaving the country.
Shell Company / Artificial Corporate Interposition

Statutory sanction: Under the GAAR, the transaction will be disregarded and the tax benefit denied; a GAAR penalty of 25% of the tax benefit plus interest applies under s. 173; under Canada's Anti-Money Laundering Rules (PCMLTFA), the shell company may be reported to FINTRAC; corporate assets and bank accounts are subject to seizure orders under the Criminal Code (s. 462.4) if they are proceeds of crime; the Canadian director may be subject to a C$500,000 fine and criminal imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors of the Canadian entity who knowingly operate through a shell to hide the true source of IOL profits may be charged with tax evasion under s. 239, money laundering under s. 462.31 (maksimum 10 years imprisonment) and fraud over $5,000 (up to 14 years); courts will pierce the corporate veil to impose joint and several restitution on the directors' personal residences and foreign bank accounts; travel bans and passport confiscation are routinely granted in such cases to prevent flight.
Dividend Stripping and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA will invoke s. 212.1 (dividend stripping) and the treaty anti-abuse provision under the Multilateral Instrument; the entire special dividend will be re-classified as a dividend taxable under Part XIII at 25%, adjusted for the treaty rate actually obtained; a GAAR penalty of 25% of the tax differential is applied; interest and penalties may double if the scheme is found to be wilfully concealed.

Director personal liability: The directors who contrived the treaty-stripping structure may be personally liable as trustees for unreported Part XIII withholding tax under s. 227.1 and as co-conspirators for tax evasion; they face civil liability for the excisable amount plus punitive damages, a permanent prohibition from serving as directors under the CBCA/ABCA, travel bans, and imprisonment for up to 5 years; the CRA and OSC/AMF may impose reciprocal cumulative penalties under the Tax Act, the Income Tax Act, and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) Act.
