Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA); official national portal: CRA My Business Account and Business Registration Online (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses.html). Alberta corporate income tax is administered by the CRA under the federal-provincial tax collection agreement; no separate Alberta tax identification number is issued.

E-government filing portal

A foreign corporation establishing an Alberta tax identity must first complete its extra-provincial registration under the Business Corporations Act (Alberta) or obtain a federal incorporation/continuation and Alberta extra-provincial registration. Then it must file Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number' (BN) with the CRA, or online through Business Registration Online, supplying the corporate legal name, Canada Revenue Agency mailing address, board resolution evidence, Canadian representative for service, estimated annual taxable supplies, and intended GST/HST registration effective date. The CRA issues a nine-digit Business Number (BN), with a separate programme account for GST/HST (RTxxxx) and corporate income tax (RCxxxx). A non-resident corporation may also need to designate a recognized Canadian agent or representative under the Excise Tax Act for service and record-keeping purposes.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No check-the-box or pass-through election is available for a Canadian Business Corporation; the entity is a separate taxable person for Canadian income tax and GST/HST purposes. Statutory elections relevant to non-residents include the Income Tax Act subsection 216(1) election to file a Canadian return on net rental income, treaty-based residence/tie-breaker determinations, and the hybrid mismatch rules (sections 18.2, 18.3, 20.2 and 212.3 of the Income Tax Act) which restrict deduction/non-inclusion outcomes for foreign-owned Canadian entities. Alberta does not provide any additional entity classification election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in total taxable supplies (before expenses) measured over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters, or per CRA administrative rules once taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter; non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada or supplying through a Canadian permanent establishment are subject to the same small-supplier threshold, and may voluntarily register for GST/HST below the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under the Excise Tax Act, Schedule VI, Part V to tangible personal property (including dental clear aligner sheets) exported by the supplier, or delivered to an independent common carrier or air/road carrier for shipment to a non-resident consignee outside Canada, provided the supplier retains prescribed evidence of export and the goods are not further processed, altered, or intermingled within Canada. Clear aligners sold to foreign orthodontic clinics and dental distributors under international commercial terms qualify as zero-rated supplies when the exporter proves physical export from Alberta. Offshore services supplied to non-residents are generally zero-rated unless they relate to Canadian real estate, tangible personal property situate in Canada, or otherwise constitute a supply of a service rendered in Canada that is excluded by the rules.

  • Canadian Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A, with electronic filing through the Canada Border Services Agency's Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) where required for the shipment value and commodity code)
  • Bill of Lading / through ocean bill / air waybill / postal export receipt issued by an independent carrier evidencing shipment from Canada to the non-resident buyer
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank payment and foreign-exchange records (e.g., SWIFT MT103) demonstrating receipt of export proceeds from the non-resident purchaser
  • Commercial invoice and itemized packing list identifying the clear aligners, quantity, harmonized system code, and destination
  • Foreign destination customs entry or delivery confirmation issued by the importing jurisdiction
  • Carrier pick-up, warehouse dispatch, and stock movement records showing the goods left Canada unaltered

No federal real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or mandatory electronic invoicing regime exists for GST/HST in Canada or Alberta. The CRA accepts electronically issued invoices and returns through GST/HST NETFILE, EDI, or XML/JSON file uploads, and requires businesses to maintain reliable audit trails; there is no API-based pre-clearance of individual invoices before issuance to customers.

The CRA may reassess a zero-rated export within the normal four-year reassessment period to charge 5% GST/HST on the export invoice if the exporter fails to maintain the complete prescribed documentary bundle; the period can be extended indefinitely where misrepresentation, fraud, or neglect is established. False or grossly negligent claims for zero-rating or input tax refunds trigger substantial civil penalties under section 281.1 of the Excise Tax Act, together with daily compounding arrears interest. Wilful evasion is prosecuted under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act and may result in fines and imprisonment; export zero-rating claims under audit are also subject to clawback of any GST/HST refunds paid and revocation of voluntary registrations where the registrant's export documentation is deficient.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current and capital account. No government approval is required for trade settlements, service payments, dividend remittances, or capital repatriation. Profit remittance hurdles arise solely under tax law: non-resident shareholders face Part XIII withholding tax at 25%, reducible under Canada's income tax treaties (e.g., US-Canada treaty generally reduces to 5% on dividends for substantial shareholdings and 15% otherwise). No certification of foreign receipts or repatriation lock-in applies.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory surrender requirement in Canada. Export proceeds may be retained in any currency, held in Canadian or offshore accounts, and used freely for operating expenses, capital expenditure, and tax payments. Reporting obligations are limited to filing T2125 / corporate tax schedules and maintaining books and records for CRA audit. Canadian financial institutions may require completion of a Foreign Income Verification Statement (T1135) for foreign accounts above C$100,000 for the Canadian entity, but this is tax reporting, not currency surrender.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada / Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) - No dedicated foreign-exchange control authority; foreign exchange administration is governed by the Bank of Canada Act, the Financial Administration Act, and OSFI prudential rules applicable to banks.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium for cross-border payment and FX execution; Low for primary treasury/deposit holding. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Wise, Payoneer, Mercury) are not authorized to take Canadian deposits absent OSFI licensing. They may operate as payment service providers / money services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC, but cannot offer insured deposit accounts or central clearing functionality. A domestic bank account remains mandatory for capital escrow, payroll, and CRA remittance obligations. Fintechs are suitable only as auxiliary rails for supplier payments and small-value receivables collection.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Ensure supporting wire origin is a regulated financial institution and the beneficiary account is in the same legal entity name. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal plus 8% Alberta. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access an 11% combined small-business rate on up to CAD 500,000 of eligible active business income, subject to federal and Alberta limits, associated-corporation aggregation, taxable-capital restrictions, and passive-income grind rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. Common treaty rates are 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting the ownership threshold and 15% in other qualifying cases. The Canada-United States treaty commonly provides 5% for a qualifying corporate shareholder owning at least 10% of voting power and 15% otherwise.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 and Alberta AT1 are generally due within 6 months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Any balance of tax is generally due 2 months after year-end, or 3 months after year-end for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations meeting the statutory conditions. Installments are due monthly or, where permitted, quarterly.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Monthly installments are generally due by the last day of each month. Quarterly installments are generally due by the last day of each quarter, subject to eligibility conditions and required installment methodology.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not use a statutory Local File/Master File threshold in the same terminology as many OECD jurisdictions. Contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is required under Income Tax Act section 247(4) for non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents, regardless of transaction size, unless an exception applies. Form T106 generally applies where aggregate reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000 and other statutory conditions are met. Canada generally requires a country-by-country report from an ultimate parent or surrogate entity resident in Canada where consolidated group revenue is at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year. A Canadian corporation should maintain a functional analysis, industry analysis, agreements, segmented financial data, tested-party rationale, comparables, and reconciliation to the T2 and T106.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the regimes of certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance remains relevant under the general anti-avoidance rule, specific anti-avoidance provisions, treaty interpretation, beneficial-ownership analysis, sham and agency principles, foreign-affiliate rules, interest deductibility rules, and transfer-pricing legislation. The corporation should maintain real Canadian management and operational evidence where Canadian profit, deductions, treaty benefits, or intellectual-property returns are claimed, including employees, premises, decision-making records, manufacturing capacity, risk control, capitalization, contracts, and contemporaneous accounting records. A corporation carrying on manufacturing or automated trimming of aligner sheets should align reported profit with the functions, assets, and risks actually controlled in Alberta.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment of tax, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties generally equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, and potential criminal prosecution. Criminal tax evasion may result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to 5 years; indictable fraud-related conduct may attract more severe penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST under statutory director-liability rules, subject to due-diligence defenses. Criminal conviction may result in imprisonment, fines, restitution, probation, production orders, and asset seizure. Civil liability does not automatically pierce the corporate veil for ordinary corporate tax debts.
Failure to Remit Payroll, GST/HST, or Withholding Tax

Statutory sanction: Trust liability for the withheld or collected amount, arrears interest, remittance penalties, failure-to-deduct penalties, failure-to-remit penalties, and possible criminal prosecution for deliberate non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors can be jointly and severally liable for prescribed unremitted amounts, interest, and penalties where statutory conditions are met. Directors may face garnishment, liens, collection proceedings, prosecution, and restitution; travel restrictions are not a routine tax sanction but may arise through criminal process or court orders.
Transfer-Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Primary transfer-pricing adjustment under Income Tax Act section 247, possible 10% transfer-pricing penalty on the adjustment where reasonable efforts and documentation requirements are not met, reassessment interest, withholding-tax exposure, and potential double taxation absent competent-authority relief.

Director personal liability: Directors generally have no automatic personal liability for a transfer-pricing adjustment, but can face liability for knowingly false returns, tax evasion, obstruction, or unremitted withholding. Corporate veil piercing remains exceptional.
Treaty Abuse and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty relief, reassessment at the 25% domestic Part XIII rate, withholding shortfall assessment, interest, penalties, and application of the principal-purpose or anti-avoidance rules where applicable.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for unremitted withholding tax and may face criminal exposure for deliberate false certificates or fraudulent filings. Civil restitution may be ordered following fraud or misappropriation; ordinary treaty denial does not itself create criminal liability.
Shell Company and Artificial Canadian Presence

Statutory sanction: Reallocation of income and expenses, denial of deductions or treaty benefits, application of the general anti-avoidance rule, transfer-pricing adjustments, provincial allocation reassessment, interest, and penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for statutory trust amounts, fraudulent conduct, oppression-related claims, or knowing participation in tax offences. A shell structure alone does not automatically pierce the corporate veil.
Corporate Records and Beneficial-Ownership Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative penalties, refusal or cancellation of corporate filings, dissolution or loss of good standing, audit reassessment, and potential offences under the Canada Business Corporations Act, Alberta Business Corporations Act, or applicable corporate statute.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be prosecuted for statutory offences, fined, disqualified, or held liable for losses caused by breaches of fiduciary or statutory duties. Imprisonment depends on the governing offence and facts.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, publication of violations, seizure or forfeiture proceedings, and criminal prosecution under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and Criminal Code. Money-laundering offences may carry imprisonment of up to 10 years on indictment.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and responsible compliance personnel may be prosecuted personally, fined, imprisoned, and ordered to forfeit proceeds. Travel restrictions may arise through bail, judicial release, arrest warrants, or criminal court orders.
Sanctions, Export-Control, and Restricted-Party Violations

Statutory sanction: Administrative or criminal penalties under Canadian sanctions and export-control legislation, including substantial fines, asset freezes, transaction prohibitions, seizure, and imprisonment depending on the statute and conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be prosecuted for authorization, acquiescence, or wilful blindness. Personal fines, imprisonment, forfeiture, and travel restrictions may follow criminal proceedings.
Medical-Device and Product-Safety Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Inspection orders, stop-sale or recall orders, seizure, product detention, licence suspension or cancellation, administrative measures, fines, and prosecution under the Food and Drugs Act and Medical Devices Regulations.

Director personal liability: Corporate officers and directors may be prosecuted where they directed, authorized, or participated in the offence. Conviction can result in fines and imprisonment; civil product-liability claims may include compensatory and, in exceptional cases, punitive damages.
Accounting Records and Document Destruction

Statutory sanction: Repeated reassessments using available evidence, adverse inferences, denial of deductions or credits, record-keeping penalties, obstruction offences, and potential criminal prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face fines or imprisonment for obstruction or destruction of records and may be personally liable for statutory trust amounts. The ordinary corporate veil remains intact for bona fide accounting errors.
Improper Profit Repatriation and Corporate Distribution

Statutory sanction: Dividend or distribution recovery, Part XIII withholding assessment, denied deductions, shareholder-benefit or shareholder-loan consequences, interest, penalties, and possible creditor remedies under corporate insolvency law.

Director personal liability: Directors may be required to repay unlawful distributions or be liable for creditor losses where statutory solvency and fiduciary duties were breached. Fraudulent distributions can result in civil restitution, asset recovery, fines, and imprisonment.
