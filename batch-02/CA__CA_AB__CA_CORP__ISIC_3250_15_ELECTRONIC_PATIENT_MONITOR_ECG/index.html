Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), Business Registration Online and CRA My Business Account; Alberta corporate income tax administration is integrated with the CRA for most corporations. The Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) administers import and export customs through the CARM Client Portal.

E-government filing portal

A corporation incorporated in Canada, federally or in Alberta, obtains a nine-digit Business Number (BN) from the CRA through Business Registration Online or the applicable incorporation and registration process. A non-resident corporation must obtain a BN where it carries on business in Canada, is required to file a Canadian corporate income tax return, registers for GST/HST, has payroll obligations, imports or exports commercial goods, or otherwise requires a CRA program account. The corporation must provide its legal name, incorporation jurisdiction and date, registered office, principal business activity, directors or authorized representatives, Canadian address or non-resident contact details, and supporting incorporation documents. A GST/HST program account is added to the BN through Business Registration Online, CRA forms, or CRA-approved registration procedures. Non-resident businesses may be required to appoint an authorized representative and provide proof of business activity, contracts, invoices, customs records, and identity documentation. Commercial importers and exporters must register with CBSA through the CARM Client Portal and obtain or use a valid BN15 import-export program account. A GST/HST registration is generally mandatory when taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, subject to special rules for non-residents and specified digital-economy supplies. A non-resident may register voluntarily before exceeding the threshold if it makes taxable supplies in Canada and satisfies CRA requirements. GST/HST registration does not itself determine corporate tax residence; a foreign corporation may also have Canadian permanent-establishment, carrying-on-business, branch-tax, filing, and treaty obligations.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no general United States-style check-the-box election for corporations. A Canadian corporation is ordinarily taxable as a separate legal entity, while a foreign corporation remains subject to Canadian classification under Canadian law and applicable tax treaties. Relevant statutory elections include section 85 Income Tax Act rollovers for qualifying transfers of property to a taxable Canadian corporation, section 86 reorganizations, section 51 conversions, capital dividend account elections, and specified GST/HST elections. Canadian hybrid mismatch rules may deny deductions, limit credits, or include amounts where an arrangement produces deduction or non-inclusion or duplicate deduction outcomes. Entity classification should be confirmed under Canadian domestic law and the applicable treaty rather than selected by an elective tax form.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, generally measured on a single calendar-quarter basis or over four consecutive calendar quarters, subject to statutory exclusions and special non-resident rules. Taxable supplies include zero-rated supplies, while exempt supplies are generally excluded from the threshold. A corporation carrying on business in Canada may be required to register even where particular exports are zero-rated. Voluntary registration is available where eligibility conditions are met.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada are generally zero-rated for GST where the statutory export conditions are met and the supplier retains evidence that the goods were exported. The supplier should not charge GST on qualifying exports but may claim input tax credits for GST incurred on related business inputs. Evidence commonly includes export declarations, carrier records, bills of lading, delivery records, customs documentation, purchase orders, commercial invoices, and proof of delivery outside Canada. Services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated only where the specific place-of-supply and non-resident-service rules are satisfied; zero-rating is not automatic merely because the customer is located outside Canada. Services relating to real property, tangible personal property situated in Canada, or performance in Canada may remain taxable. Exported medical-monitoring hardware, including ECG and bedside telemetry equipment, should be tested as goods exported from Canada and against any applicable special rules, including evidence of export and restrictions on use in Canada before export. GST registrants may recover eligible input tax through input tax credits, subject to documentary requirements, timing rules, and the normal limitation period.

  • CBSA export declaration through the applicable Canadian export reporting system, including the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) where required
  • Commercial invoice identifying exporter, consignee, goods, classification, value, origin, terms of sale, and destination
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, courier record, or other carrier transport document
  • Packing list and product description, including serial numbers where commercially relevant
  • Proof of export and proof of delivery outside Canada, such as carrier confirmation, customs exit record, delivery receipt, or foreign import record
  • Purchase order, sales contract, and payment records supporting the export transaction
  • GST records supporting zero-rating and input tax credit claims
  • Inward remittance certificate is not generally a Canadian statutory export-document requirement; bank transfer records or other payment evidence may be retained as commercial substantiation where relevant

Canada has no general nationwide real-time electronic-invoice clearance, government pre-approval, or digital fiscal-stamping mandate for ordinary domestic GST transactions. Registered suppliers must issue invoices or other prescribed documentary evidence containing required GST information, including supplier identity and BN, invoice date, recipient information where applicable, description, consideration, GST rate or amount, and relevant registration details. Electronic records and invoices are generally acceptable if they remain complete, reliable, accessible, and readable. Customs declarations and commercial data are submitted electronically through CBSA systems, including CARM where applicable, but this is not a general VAT-invoice clearance regime. GST registrants must retain supporting records for the statutory retention period, generally six years from the end of the relevant year, subject to specific exceptions.

Canada has no general statutory requirement to repatriate export proceeds within 180 days. CRA may reassess GST where export conditions are not met, evidence is insufficient, goods were used or delivered in Canada before export, services were incorrectly characterized as exported, or input tax credits were unsupported. The reassessment may include GST owing, denial or repayment of input tax credits, arrears interest, and applicable administrative penalties. A false statement or omission may attract a penalty generally based on the greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the understated tax or overstated credit, subject to statutory rules. Gross-negligence penalties may generally equal 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, and criminal prosecution may apply for fraud, evasion, or false records. Customs non-compliance may result in seizure, reassessment of duties and taxes, interest, and CBSA Administrative Monetary Penalty System penalties. There is no automatic refund entitlement merely because an export was reported as zero-rated; CRA may claw back improperly claimed refunds and input tax credits through audit or reassessment.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for both current and capital account transactions. There is no general foreign exchange control law prohibiting inbound or outbound capital movements. Dividends, royalty, and interest remittances are generally unrestricted, subject to documentary compliance under the Income Tax Act (e.g., withholding tax, NR4 reporting, treaty forms) and due-diligence requirements imposed by banks. In Alberta, no provincial capital controls apply. Practical hurdles are limited to anti-money-laundering, economic sanctions, and Know-Your-Client verification at the banking level rather than statutory convertibility restrictions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada maintains no statutory requirement to surrender export proceeds or to convert foreign currency receipts into Canadian dollars within a prescribed timeline. Exporters may retain foreign currency in authorized bank accounts, utilize proceeds for trade settlement, or convert freely. Reporting obligations apply solely in respect of transaction thresholds and records maintained for FINTRAC and income tax purposes; no mandatory conversion ratio exists.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary authority; foreign exchange administration is decentralized, with FINTRAC (proceeds of crime and terrorist financing reporting), the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (prudential regulation of banks), and the Minister of Finance (Exchange Control approval for regulated financial institutions) collectively governing cross-border payments and FX dealings.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium. Offshore digital EMI (Electronic Money Institution) accounts are operationally useful for cross-border receipts and disbursements where legacy SWIFT rails are inefficient. However, Canadian-regulated banking entities must treat digital EMIs as non-bank financial counterparties and apply enhanced due diligence. Legal constraints include: EMIs must not hold themselves out as deposit-taking institutions in Canada; funds held with an unregulated EMI are not CDIC-insured; FATF and local AML/CFT obligations require Canadian entities to verify the EMI's licensing status, jurisdiction, and compliance posture. EMIs holding Canadian customers' funds must be registered as money services businesses (MSB) in Canada if they provide remittance services; using an unregistered offshore EMI could generate FINTRAC reporting violations.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Alberta corporation generally pays 15% federal corporate income tax plus 8% Alberta corporate income tax, for a combined 23% general rate. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) may instead pay 9% federal and 2% Alberta tax, for a combined 11% rate on eligible active business income within the applicable small-business limit. Rates and eligibility must be confirmed for the tax year.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The Canadian domestic withholding rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is generally 25%, subject to reduction under an applicable tax treaty. Treaty rates depend on the recipient's residence, beneficial ownership, shareholding, and other treaty conditions; common treaty structures provide reduced portfolio and qualifying direct-investment rates, but no rate should be assumed without checking the specific treaty.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 and Alberta AT1 are generally due six months after the corporation's taxation-year end. Tax balances are generally payable two months after year-end; an eligible CCPC meeting the statutory conditions may have three months to pay. These payment extensions do not extend the return-filing deadline.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose an OECD-style universal Local File/Master File threshold for all corporations. Under Income Tax Act section 247, contemporaneous documentation is required for cross-border non-arm's-length transactions and must generally be provided within three months of a CRA request. Form T106 reporting generally applies when reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1 million in aggregate, subject to statutory rules and exceptions. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational enterprise groups meeting the CAD 1 billion consolidated-revenue threshold and the applicable Canadian filing conditions.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a general UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Canadian transfer-pricing rules, GAAR, beneficial-ownership principles, treaty anti-abuse rules, thin-capitalization rules, and factual residence or permanent-establishment tests can nevertheless challenge arrangements lacking commercial substance or an arm's-length basis. Maintain evidence of functions performed, assets used, risks controlled, decision-making, personnel, and commercial purpose.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Transfer pricing and false reporting

Statutory sanction: An adjustment may increase income and tax, with interest and a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the statutory threshold and reasonable-efforts conditions are met. False statements may attract gross-negligence penalties. Criminal prosecution may apply to wilful tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for corporate income tax or transfer-pricing adjustments. Personal exposure may arise under applicable director-liability statutes for specified unremitted source deductions, GST/HST, or other amounts, or for personal participation in an offence. Civil claims, prosecution, fines, and imprisonment depend on the conduct and statutory elements; travel bans are not an automatic tax consequence.
Failure to file or pay corporate tax

Statutory sanction: Federal late-filing and late-payment penalties and prescribed-rate interest may apply; Alberta may separately assess provincial penalties and interest. Repeated federal late filing can increase the late-filing penalty under the Income Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Corporate liability is primary. Director liability may arise only where a specific statutory basis applies, including certain unremitted amounts, and generally depends on statutory due-diligence and limitation rules. No automatic veil piercing, travel ban, or imprisonment follows from an ordinary late return.
Improper withholding on distributions or payments

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for unremitted withholding tax, penalties, and interest. The recipient may also remain liable for tax. Separate information-return penalties can apply.

Director personal liability: A director may face statutory liability for specified unremitted deductions or amounts if the applicable conditions are met. Personal criminal liability requires the elements of an offence; civil restitution, travel restrictions, and imprisonment are not automatic.
Tax evasion, fraud, or false statements

Statutory sanction: Tax, interest, civil penalties, and reassessments may apply. A person convicted of tax evasion under the Income Tax Act may face a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years; more serious fraud may be prosecuted under the Criminal Code.

Director personal liability: Individuals who authorize, participate in, or direct the conduct may be personally prosecuted and, on conviction, fined or imprisoned. Corporate status does not shield personal criminal conduct. Any travel restriction requires a separate legal basis or court process.
Shell entity, sham transaction, or treaty abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty benefits or deductions, recharacterize or adjust transactions under applicable provisions, assess tax and interest, and impose penalties where statutory conditions are met. GAAR or treaty anti-abuse provisions may apply on their facts.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for knowingly participating in fraud or offences, but incorporation alone does not create personal liability. Civil recovery, travel bans, and imprisonment require an applicable legal basis and, where required, adjudication.
Anti-money-laundering and proceeds-of-crime exposure

Statutory sanction: Criminal Code and applicable federal legislation may provide for prosecution, forfeiture, fines, and imprisonment. Reporting obligations depend on whether the corporation or activity is within the scope of the relevant regime; an ordinary manufacturing corporation is not automatically a reporting entity under every FINTRAC requirement.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly participate may be prosecuted personally. Corporate liability depends on statutory rules and attribution of conduct. Asset restraint, forfeiture, or travel restrictions require the relevant legal process; they are not automatic consequences of a tax irregularity.
