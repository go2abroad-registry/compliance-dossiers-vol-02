Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No minimum paid-up capital is required under Alberta law; a provincial business corporation may be incorporated with a nominal issued share capital of one common share (CAD 1.00). No escrow or paid-up capital deposit account is required by the incorporation procedure.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including the CRA Business Registration Online service and My Business Account portal. Alberta corporate registrations and provincial filings are administered separately by Alberta Corporate Registry through an authorized registry agent or applicable Alberta online services. Alberta has no general provincial sales tax.

E-government filing portal

A Canadian corporation generally obtains a nine-digit Business Number (BN) from the CRA upon incorporation or by registering through CRA Business Registration Online or Form RC1. The corporation must then open the applicable CRA program accounts, including GST/HST, payroll, import-export, and corporation income tax accounts as required. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada must obtain a BN and register for applicable CRA program accounts using the CRA non-resident registration process or Form RC1. GST/HST registration is required when the corporation is not a small supplier or voluntarily elects registration. Commercial importers and exporters must obtain an import-export program account and use the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) CARM Client Portal for commercial customs transactions. An Alberta corporation or extra-provincial corporation must also complete applicable Alberta Corporate Registry registration. Registration does not itself establish Canadian tax residence; residence depends principally on incorporation and central management and control, subject to applicable treaty rules.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canadian corporations are generally subject to entity-level federal and provincial corporate income tax. Canada does not have a United States-style check-the-box election for ordinary corporations. Available elections are transaction- or status-specific, including elections concerning capital dividends, eligible dividends, tax-deferred transfers under section 85 of the Income Tax Act, amalgamations, wind-ups, partnerships, and certain reorganizations. A corporation may qualify for the small business deduction if statutory conditions are met, including Canadian-controlled private corporation requirements where applicable. Alberta corporate tax status is coordinated through the CRA corporate tax filing system; there is no general Alberta pass-through election for a corporation. Hybrid mismatch outcomes are addressed through specific Canadian anti-avoidance, foreign affiliate, thin-capitalization, transfer-pricing, and hybrid arrangement rules rather than a general entity-classification election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, generally measured over the relevant preceding four consecutive calendar quarters or in a single calendar quarter, is the small-supplier threshold for most businesses. Taxable supplies made in Canada, including zero-rated supplies, are generally relevant to the threshold; supplies that are exempt and certain specified supplies are excluded. A person exceeding the threshold must register and begin charging GST under the applicable effective-date rules. Voluntary registration is permitted in many cases before the threshold is exceeded.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada may generally be zero-rated for GST where the statutory export conditions are satisfied, including delivery or export outside Canada in accordance with the Excise Tax Act and supporting evidence that the goods left Canada. Green coffee beans imported into Canada and subsequently exported may qualify for zero-rating on the Canadian export supply if the exporter retains evidence of export and the supply otherwise meets the statutory requirements. GST paid on qualifying imports and Canadian business inputs may generally be recovered through input tax credits, subject to registration, documentary, timing, and attribution rules. Services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated only where the specific exported-service rules apply; the status of the customer alone is insufficient, and exclusions apply for services connected with Canadian real property, tangible personal property, litigation, and certain services performed in Canada. Exporters must not charge GST on a qualifying zero-rated export but must retain objective evidence linking the transaction, goods, purchaser, carrier, export declaration, and destination outside Canada.

  • CBSA commercial export declaration or applicable Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) record, where export reporting is required
  • Commercial invoice identifying the exporter, purchaser, goods, value, origin, destination, and terms of sale
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, carrier manifest, or equivalent transport document
  • Packing list and container or shipment identification records
  • Customs release, import-entry, or delivery evidence in the destination country where available
  • Proof of delivery or other objective evidence confirming that the goods left Canada
  • Purchase and sales contracts, purchase orders, documentary letter of credit records, and payment records where relevant
  • GST import documentation, including CBSA accounting records and proof of GST paid, when claiming related input tax credits
  • Inward remittance certificate: not generally required under Canadian GST law; bank records or other payment evidence should nevertheless be retained where relevant to commercial, transfer-pricing, anti-money-laundering, or audit substantiation

Canada has no general nationwide real-time GST invoice-clearance, mandatory government e-invoicing, or digital tax-stamping regime for ordinary business-to-business transactions. A GST registrant must issue documentation containing prescribed information when required, maintain reliable electronic or paper books and records, and retain records for the statutory retention period, generally six years from the end of the relevant year. CRA electronic filing and payment obligations may apply by taxpayer type and filing period. Customs declarations for commercial imports and exports are processed through CBSA systems, including the CARM Client Portal; this is a customs reporting requirement, not a general GST invoice-clearance mandate. Electronic invoices and accounting records must be capable of substantiating tax charged, input tax credits, export treatment, and audit trails.

Canada has no general statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds and no general requirement to obtain an inward remittance certificate to support GST zero-rating. CRA and CBSA may audit export documentation, supply terms, transportation evidence, customs declarations, inventory records, payment flows, related-party pricing, and the identity and destination of the purchaser. If zero-rating is denied, GST may be assessed retroactively, together with applicable arrears interest and penalties. Incorrectly claimed input tax credits or refunds may be reassessed with interest and penalties. A gross-negligence penalty may generally equal 50% of the overstated tax benefit or understated net tax, while other statutory penalties may apply for late filing, late payment, false statements, repeated failures, or failure to maintain records. Deliberate tax fraud or false customs declarations may result in prosecution, fines, imprisonment, seizure, administrative monetary penalties, customs reassessment, loss of refund entitlement, and recovery of improperly obtained refunds. Export evidence must be retained for CRA audit and limitation-period purposes, generally at least six years, subject to extended periods for non-compliance, objection, appeal, or statutory exception.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is an IMF Article VIII jurisdiction with full current-account and capital-account convertibility. There is no exchange-control licensing, no import-cover requirement, no advance-payment restriction, and no repatriation moratorium affecting green-coffee import payments, profit remittances, or shareholder dividends. The only residual frictions are fiscal rather than FX-regulatory: (i) Part XIII withholding tax at the statutory 25% on dividends and royalties and, subject to the arm's-length-interest exemption, interest paid to non-residents, generally treaty-reduced to 5% to 15%; (ii) Part XIV branch-profit tax, which is relevant only if the specialty-coffee business were conducted through a non-resident branch rather than through the incorporated CA_CORP; and (iii) the thin-capitalization and back-to-back debt rules in Income Tax Act subsections 18(4) to 18(6), 90(6) to 90(13), and 212(3.1), which may recharacterize excessive non-resident shareholder debt as equity and disallow interest. In practice, a federally incorporated or Alberta-registered Business Corporation faces no convertibility obstacle: dividends may be paid at any frequency, subject to corporate solvency rules, the CRA T2 return, the Form T106 non-arm's-length reporting where applicable, and the Form NR4 withholding summary.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable to Canada. Since the repeal of the Foreign Exchange Control Act in 1951, Canada imposes no statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no central-bank escrow or surrender obligation on export receipts; the Canadian dollar is freely floating and fully convertible. For the inbound-import model in this dossier, the relevant obligations are: (i) electronic import declaration using HS code 0901.11 (green coffee, not roasted, not decaffeinated) filed through the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) platform; (ii) retention of all transactional books and records for six years under section 230 of the Income Tax Act; and (iii) FINTRAC reporting of any cross-border electronic funds transfer, or sequential series of transfers, that aggregates CAD 10,000 or more within a 24-hour period. Any origin-country export-proceeds surrender obligation is a matter of the supplier's local FX regime and is contractually neutralized through the confirmed documentary letter of credit.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). Canada has no dedicated foreign-exchange-control authority; the foreign-exchange administration is the shared remit of the Department of Finance Canada, the Bank of Canada (currency issuance and monetary policy), and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for cross-border movement reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Prudential supervision of the domestic commercial banks rests with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and trade data flows through the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) under the Customs Act.

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23.0% combined general corporate active business rate (15% federal general rate + 8% Alberta general rate); Canadian-controlled private corporations pay 11.0% combined (9% federal + 2% Alberta) on the first CA$500,000 of active business income.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on dividends paid to a non-resident shareholder; reduced to 5% under the Canada–US Treaty for a corporate beneficial owner holding at least 10% of voting shares, and to 15% for portfolio shareholders; other Canadian income tax treaties commonly provide rates from 5% to 15%.

Schedule 8 – Small Business Deduction Claims the CCPC small business deduction on the first CA$500,000 of active business income, subject to investment income and passive income clawback thresholds. Not available to a corporation not controlled by Canadian residents; mark the CCPC ownership declaration only when statutory Canadian control tests are met. Late filing penalty of 5% of the unpaid balance plus 1% per complete month, up to 12 months (maximum 17%); repeat failures double the penalty. Delinquent amounts bear prescribed CRA daily compound interest. Failure to file T106/NR4/T5 information returns carries additional per-day penalties and may result in arbitrary assessment and compliance enforcement.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CA$1,000,000 in aggregate annual non-arm's length cross-border transactions, triggering the T106 information return and contemporaneous Local File/Master File documentation. Multinational groups with consolidated annual group revenue above CA$750,000,000 must also file a Country-by-Country Report with the CRA.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate Economic Substance Regulation regime, but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR), statutory PE rules (ITA s.253), and substance-over-form doctrine require genuine board oversight, employees, inventory risk, title passage, and strategic decision-making in Alberta. A letter-box coffee importer may be deemed resident abroad or denied treaty benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.239 prosecution: fine of 50%–200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years on indictment, plus gross negligence civil penalties of 50% of the understated tax, interest, and revocation of compliance status.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who sign or direct false returns are jointly and severally liable for restitution of taxes and penalties; criminal conviction exposes individuals to incarceration, global asset freeze, and for non-Canadian directors, IRPA inadmissibility and removal from Canada.
Unremitted Withholding Taxes (Part XIII, Payroll, GST/HST)

Statutory sanction: CRA may assess the corporation for the full unremitted amount, compound daily prescribed interest, late penalties of up to 10% of the unremitted amount, and information-return penalties; repeated behaviour triggers criminal referral.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable under ITA s.227.1 and Excise Tax Act s.323 for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST; CRA may seize personal assets, issue director's assessment, and prohibit cross-border travel for senior officers while enforcement is outstanding.
Transfer Pricing and Profit-Shifting Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA audit adjustments to reassess taxable income at arm's length, 10% transfer pricing penalty under ITA s.247(3) on net adjustments, denial of treaty relief, and GAAR penalties of up to 50% of the tax benefit; failure to file T106 adds separate penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors are not shielded by the corporate veil where they knowingly sign false transfer pricing filings or cause GAAR avoidance transactions; personal exposure includes civil restitution of avoided taxes, fines for counselling false statements, and imprisonment for fraud.
AML/CFT and Corporate Transparency Violations

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties of up to CA$1,000,000 per violation; Criminal Code money-laundering offences carry imprisonment up to 10 years and unlimited fines, with proceeds subject to civil forfeiture.

Director personal liability: Senior officers who authorize AML breaches are personally criminally liable; convictions result in imprisonment, confiscation of personal and corporate assets, travel bans pending extradition or sentencing, and permanent inadmissibility for non-residents.
