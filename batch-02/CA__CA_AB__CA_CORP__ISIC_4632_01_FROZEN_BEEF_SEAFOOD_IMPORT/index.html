Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A registered office in Alberta must be a physical street address within the province, not solely a postal box or virtual mailbox. The registered office must be staffed and accessible during normal business hours for service of process. For wholesalers of frozen meat and seafood, having a registered office at a professional registered-agent address is permissible, but tax authorities require substantive commercial substance; a virtual office qualifies only for the statutory registered office if provided under contract by a licensed corporate services provider with a physical Alberta address. Such virtual arrangement does not, without more, satisfy the Canada Revenue Agency's general anti-avoidance or place of management tests; dependency on a virtual office alone elevates audit risk. The industry scope (ISIC 4632) requires either a licensed establishment address, or local distribution/cold-chain logistics arrangements to demonstrate commercial substance.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) for federal GST and national corporate income tax; provincial corporate income tax for Alberta is administered by the CRA under a tax collection agreement with Alberta Treasury Board and Finance. Primary portal: CRA 'My Business Account' (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html); registration via 'Business Registration Online' (BRO).

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity must obtain a Federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or online through BRO before commencing business in Canada. The BN registration concurrently establishes GST/HST, import/export (RM), and corporate income tax program accounts. Alberta does not issue a separate provincial tax number because the CRA administers Alberta corporate income tax. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Alberta must register and file a return; non-resident importers that are not 'small suppliers' must register for GST/HST regardless of the $30,000 threshold when importing goods for supply.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: A Canadian 'Business Corporation' (federal or provincial) is a separate legal and tax entity resident on the basis of incorporation and central management/control; it is fully taxable on worldwide income under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and Alberta Corporate Tax Act. No flow-through or consolidated attribute for Canadian tax purposes.

Available election forms

No 'check-the-box' election exists under Canadian domestic tax law; classification follows the entity's legal form. Hybrid mismatch rules (s. 12.8 and s. 18.4 of the Income Tax Act) deny deductions for certain dual-entity mismatches and require adjustments, but no proactive elective reclassification is available. For international treaties, foreign entity classification is determined by CRA administrative policy and Canadian treaty law (e.g., tie-breaker rules), not by a taxpayer election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide annual taxable supplies (small supplier threshold under ETA s. 148); however, a person who imports goods into Canada for the purpose of making taxable supplies and is not a small supplier must register, and non-residents making taxable supplies in Canada are generally required to register before the threshold if they are engaged in a commercial activity.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods may be zero-rated under ETA s. 21 (supply of goods exported by the supplier or a non-resident recipient) and Schedule VI, Part V, s. 1, provided the supplier obtains and retains documentary evidence of export within the statutory time frame. Services supplied to a non-resident are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, s. 9 when they are performed for a non-resident and are not consumed or performed in Canada. For offshore fish and meat trading, the supply of goods via international transport, including delivery to a more long-term storage outside Canada or to a carrier for immediate export, falls within the zero-rating provisions, subject to the CBSA/CRA evidence requirements.

  • Customs Export Declaration
  • Bill of Lading
  • Inward Remittance Certificate

Canada does not mandate real-time e-invoicing, fiscal clearance, or digital stamping. GST/HST invoices must meet the information requirements of ETA ss. 221 and 223 and be issued in a form acceptable to the CRA, including electronic with prior authorization. Customs filings (export/import) are declared electronically through the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) Single Window Initiative (SWI) and eManifest/message formats; no continuous transaction controls or government API real-time fiscal attestation is imposed.

Improper zero-rating triggers a reassessment of the uncollected GST at 5% plus interest and penalties. ETA s. 285 imposes a penalty equal to 50% of the tax for false statements or omissions attributable to negligence or willful permission; s. 280 penalizes failure to file or provide information; s. 327 provides for evading tax by up to 200% of the tax and criminal prosecution. The normal reassessment period is four years, extended to seven years for misrepresentation, and unlimited in cases of fraud. Practically, CRA may claw back zero-rating benefits if documentary evidence of export (customs declaration, bill of lading, and foreign exchange remittance certificate) is not retained and furnished within 180 days.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible and free-floating under the Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52). Both current-account and capital-account transactions are unrestricted; profit remittances, dividends, and non-resident shareholder capital withdrawals require no central-bank approval. The only practical frictions are PCMLTFA reporting obligations (FINTRAC large-value EFT and large-cash reporting) and bank-level due diligence, which do not constitute exchange controls. For ISIC 4632 cold-chain importers, import payments and LC settlements are lawful without prior regulatory authorization.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender requirement, no repatriation timeline, and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. Exporters and importers may freely hold foreign currency in Canadian bank accounts. The only related obligations are CBSA customs valuation and reporting for imports; any bank-imposed foreign-exchange cover requirements are contractual credit-condition covenants, not statutory surrender rules.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); prudential oversight by Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/CFT supervision by Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada maintains no separate foreign-exchange administration or exchange-control licensing authority.

24.0% combined effective headline rate for an Alberta-resident corporation (federal general corporate rate 16% for taxation years beginning after 2024 + Alberta general provincial rate 8%). For taxation years beginning in 2024, the combined rate is 23.0% (federal 15% + Alberta 8%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (s. 212(2)) on dividends paid to a non-resident beneficial owner, subject to treaty reduction. Alberta levies no separate provincial dividend withholding tax.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no statutory monetary filing threshold for Local File / Master File. Contemporaneous documentation is mandatory under IC 94-4 (comparable to OECD Chapter V documentation) whenever cross-border non-arm's length transactions have tax consequences. The Master File/Local File documentation is, in practice, required once the taxpayer has intercompany controlled transactions of CAD 1 million or more annually because such amounts trigger T106 reporting; no TP files are 'filed' with the CRA, rather they must be produced within 3 months upon request (IC 87-2R). Country-by-Country Report threshold: MNE groups with consolidated group revenue ≥ CAD 750,000,000 must file Form RC4649 (CbC) and 12-month notification. The T106 return is mandatory for all non-arm's length cross-border transactions with the aggregate value exceeding CAD 1,000,000 (ITA s. 233.1); the T1134 foreign affiliate information return is required where the cost amount of the controlled foreign affiliate's shares exceeds CAD 100,000.

Economic substance scope: The Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) of the UAE/Iraq, BVI, Cayman-type regimes are not directly replicated in Alberta. Canada polices economic substance through: (i) the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR, s. 245 ITA), which recharacterizes avoidance transactions lacking bona fide purpose; (ii) the transfer-pricing reallocation rule (s. 247(2)) which ignores transactions without substance if pricing is not arm's length; (iii) the agency or dependent-agent Permanent Establishment doctrine (s. 253 / treaty ATRs) and the MLI PEs for commissionaire structures; (iv) the Excessive Interest and Financing Expenses Limitation (EIFEL) rules (s. 18.2/18.3) capping net interest deductibility at 30% of EBITDA; (v) the anti-hybrid mismatch rules (s. 18.4 etc.) and the Canadian patent-box/net economic substance for treaty benefits. A cold-chain importer domiciled in Alberta must hold actual board meetings, employ a substantial Alberta team for sourcing, warehousing, freight-forwarding and sales, maintain local accounting, and hold title-to-risk through the Alberta entity; otherwise CRA will recharacterize the Alberta entity as a conduit and attribute profits to the foreign principal, with GAAR penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Reporting (ITA s. 239, s. 163(2))

Statutory sanction: Civil: gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understated federal/Provincial tax plus daily arrears interest at the prescribed rate (compound daily). Criminal: on conviction under s. 239(1), a fine of up to 200% of the tax evaded and/or imprisonment for a term not exceeding 5 years. CRA referral to the Public Prosecution Service of Canada and the RCMP CBCU for criminal tax prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable under s. 227.1 ITA for unremitted Part XIII withholdings and GST/HST, with the 2-year limitation on directors' liability tolled only where a diligent-ordinary-care defense is proven. Under s. 227.1(5), civil restitution of unpaid withholding; CRA may apply to court for extraordinary-remedy orders; directors may face travel bans (CRA's 'travel ban' list under s. 226.1), asset freezes, and passport revocation in criminal referral cases; sentencing in major meat/seafood off-book cases has included 3–5-year imprisonment and 100% restitution.
Transfer-Pricing and Documentation Failures (ITA s. 247)

Statutory sanction: Reassessment by CRA reallocating income to the arm's length profit level with a harmonized penalty of 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment (up to CAD 1 million per year for large groups), plus prescribed-rate interest compounded daily from the balance due date; under s. 247(3), the 10% penalty applies where the adjustment exceeds CAD 50,000; interest and penalties cannot be reduced by any offsetting adjustment outside the scope of s. 247.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable, but they are exposed once they sign a false certification of compliance (certification penalty under s. 235.1/163(2) for false statements), or if they consciously omitted T1134/T106 information, the CRA can assess Part I penalties against the corporation and pursue directors who acted as tax 'powerful masters' where the corporation was used as a sham; criminal s. 239 exposure arises for intentionally false documents; CRA issues Form T1311 identity interrogation and cross-border auditing under IC 87-2R with denial of treaty benefits for perceived 'Perplant agreements.'
Artificial P.E. / Shell Substance Avoidance (s. 245 GAAR and Treaty Abuse)

Statutory sanction: GAAR denial of the tax benefit, recharacterization of the structure under s. 245(2), 25% penalty of the avoided tax attributable to the avoidance transaction plus daily compound interest; PE reattribution of a full Canadian business profit under s. 253(b) and treaty Article X; the CRA may reassess under the Income Application Rules ('wrong source' basis) and deny treaty residence benefits, adding Part XIII 25% dividends deemed distribution.

Director personal liability: Courts will pierce the corporate veil where the corporation is a facade (Ontario v. Rothmans; US SFX; Canadian Snowbird cases). Directors knowingly administering the shell face personal liability under s. 160 ITA for property transferred to offshore trusts for less than FMV, 5-year travel bans, TP corporate tax debts plus garnishment of personal benefits, and threat of criminal charges for counselling tax fraud; the CRA will issue a 'warrant of director's liability' to levy personal bank accounts without prior judicial authorization.
Part XIII Withholding and Payroll-Sharing Deficits (s. 212, s. 227.1)

Statutory sanction: The payer must pay the full Part XIII amount to the Receiver General (s. 215); a 10% penalty under s. 227(9) for failure to deduct in the prescribed manner; 20% penalty for failure to remit, plus daily compound prescribed-rate interest; a deemed trust in the amount of the tax withheld (s. 227(4)) attaching to all corporate assets with a super-priority over secured creditors in bankruptcy of the Alberta import entity.

Director personal liability: Under s. 227.1(1), directors jointly and severally liable for the unremitted Part XIII/GST amounts as soon as the corporation fails to remit — a strict liability regime with a narrow due-diligence defense that cannot be met merely by relying on external accountants; personal exposure up to CAD 100,000s for a single payroll/withholding cycle; CRA immigration/travel enforcement measures; financial institution garnishment, and trust-preference remedies that override the balance sheet.
Customs and Trade-Based Money Laundering (Customs Act, s. 127 / PCMLTFA / Criminal Code)

Statutory sanction: Forfeiture of the imported goods or their value at time of seizure (s. 110 Customs Act) and a cumulative penalty of 2x the duty evasion; for a false declaration, a fine of up to CAD 500,000 plus imprisonment up to 5 years (s. 159); under the PCMLTFA, the 'Low_Risk_Standard' tier still requires an FTR/retail MSB registration if the importer handles physical currency in excess of CAD 10,000 (in any 24-hour period); an unregistered MSB conducting foreign remittances for the supply chain is subject to fines of up to CAD 500,000 and 5 years' imprisonment, with FINTRAC's administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000.

Director personal liability: Criminal Law: officers/controllers prosecuted as parties to the offence; revenue loss assessments can be collected personally, private foundations; Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) bans directors from fast-track clearance programs (FAST/C-TPAT/CBSA 'trusted trader'), places watch notices on individual passports, imposes 5-year denial of border clearance, and secures personal arrest warrants upon repeat quota misdeclarations in the meat trade; civil deception leading to projected profits returned via restitution orders under the Ontario/AB criminal restitution regime.
Loss Carve-Out and Tax-Loss Trafficking (s. 111(5) / s. 256.1 Adcontrolled)

Statutory sanction: Denial of loss utilization where there is a change-of-control (s. 249(4)) and the business was not continued with the same undertaking — a loss-affiliation test collapses; reassessment with full recapture, 50% gross-negligence penalty where the loss claim is unreasonable, and in cases of artificial loss manufacturing, GAAR penalty of 25% plus interest.

Director personal liability: No direct loss-trafficking liability for directors, but the acquisition agent and tax advisor who executes the loss-purchase scheme can be sued for breach of fiduciary duty/expert negligence in Alberta courts and charged as co-conspirators in the criminal offence of tax evasion if the loss claim is documented as part of a sham; transaction-level restitution against the vendor who sold the shell becomes mandatory under the Income Tax Act after CRA statutory 'Fraudulent Loss' assessments.
