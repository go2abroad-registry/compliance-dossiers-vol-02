Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — My Business Account portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/e-services-businesses/business-account.html)

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity must obtain a Canada Business Number (BN) to administer GST/HST, payroll, and import/export accounts. For a non-resident corporation, the standard procedure is to complete Form RC1, 'Request for a Business Number and Tax Accounts', and submit it by mail, fax, or through CRA's online 'Register and Open a Business Account' service. Alternatively, a corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or an Alberta provincial statute may request registration of tax accounts during the incorporation process. The foreign entity must provide proof of legal existence (certificate of incorporation from its home jurisdiction), a registered office address in its home jurisdiction, and the name and address of a Canadian contact (e.g., counsel, accountant, or bank branch) where applicable. If the entity has no permanent establishment, the CRA typically requires a non-resident tax representative or an authorization arrangement. GST/HST registration is mandatory when the non-resident makes taxable supplies in Canada and is not a small supplier; otherwise, the entity may still register voluntarily to claim input tax credits or to comply with export zero-rating documentation expectations.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not offer the U.S.-style 'check-the-box' election. For a Canadian business corporation, the relevant statutory elections include: (i) the closely related corporations election under subsection 128(1) of the Excise Tax Act, which permits related corporations to make certain otherwise-taxable supplies non-taxable for GST/HST; (ii) the non-resident corporation election under section 216 of the Income Tax Act, allowing a non-resident to file a Canadian income tax return and elect to compute tax on certain Canadian-source rental income as if the activity were a separate business; (iii) the branch tax election under section 219 of the Income Tax Act, deeming a non-resident corporation to be operating a Canadian branch; and (iv) provisions under sections 18.4 to 18.7 of the Income Tax Act that impose hybrid mismatch re-characterization, but no elective opt-out is available for hybrid mismatch rules. Provincial-level elections in Alberta are absent because Alberta levies no separate corporate capital tax or provincial sales tax, but Alberta corporate income tax registration is effected through the CRA corporate payments.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD $30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies (exclusive of zero-rated exports) over any single calendar quarter or over a trailing twelve-month period; non-resident persons who are not small suppliers and who carry on business in Canada must register. Importers and service providers with no physical presence also trigger mandatory GST/HST registration when the $30,000 threshold is exceeded, unless their only Canadian supplies are zero-rated.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

To claim 0% GST/HST on exported ophthalmic eyewear frames, the supplier must be a registrant who, in the course of a commercial activity, exports goods and meets the evidential requirements of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act. The goods must be delivered to a location outside Canada and leave Canada within a reasonable time after the sale; for a typical wholesale shipment, CRA expects export to occur within 60 to 90 days. Services supplied in connection with the goods (e.g., export booking services) are zero-rated only if the services are 'exported' to a non-resident. The supplier must prove that the goods were exported by retaining, among other records, the export declaration and a bill of lading showing a Canadian origin and a foreign destination.

  • Canadian Export Declaration (B13A) filed electronically via the Canadian Export Reporting System (CAES) or the annual export report in the retailer's database
  • Commercial invoice describing the acetate eyeglass frames, HS classification (ISIC 4646 corresponds to NAICS 423460), and shipment quantities
  • Bill of lading (original or electronic) or air waybill showing departure from Canada and delivery to the non-resident buyer
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank statement showing receipt of foreign-currency payment (commonly requested by CRA as supporting evidence, although not an explicit GST/HST statutory document)
  • Packing list evidencing the shipped contents
  • Purchase order or export contract with the non-resident customer identifying the final destination country

Canada has no real-time fiscal clearance, mandatory digital stamping, or pre-authorization API for invoices. Electronic invoicing is legally permitted and CRA's record-keeping requirements allow EDI or PDF invoices; no specific 'clearance' step is required. Alberta's administrative practice follows the federal e-invoicing regime, and there is no provincial mandate.

If CRA determines that goods were not in fact exported, the zero-rated treatment is reversed and the supply becomes taxable at 5% GST in Alberta. Assessments may be reopened retroactively within four years of the date of assessment, or seven years if a misrepresentation attributable to carelessness, negligence, or willful attempt to evade tax is established. Penalties include the false-statement penalty under section 285.1 of the Excise Tax Act, which is generally 25% of the net tax misstatement for knowingly or grossly negligent filings, plus interest and the potential denial of input tax credits previously claimed. Export refunds already received may be clawed back with interest, and further penalties under the Income Tax Act can apply if the export arrangement was mischaracterized to reduce income.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility. Canada has accepted IMF Article VIII obligations since 1952; the Canadian Dollar (CAD) floats freely, and both current-account and capital-account transactions are unrestricted. There are no statutory barriers to profit remittance: a solvent Alberta business corporation may pay dividends, repay shareholder loans, and pay interest/royalties without ministerial or central-bank consent. The only remittance costs are fiscal: Part XIII non-resident withholding tax at 25% (statutory), reduced under income tax treaties (e.g., dividends generally 5% where the beneficial owner is a treaty-resident company holding at least 10% of the voting shares; otherwise 15%; arm's-length interest is generally exempt from Part XIII; related-party interest and royalties attract treaty-reduced rates). Practical hurdles are compliance-driven, not exchange-control-driven: the paying bank requires board resolutions, source-of-funds proof, and a valid SWIFT purpose description; every EFT of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported to FINTRAC. Repatriation of capital and dividends is immediate and unrestricted once the bank's compliance conditions are satisfied.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None exist. Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender obligation, no statutory retention timeline, and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. An Alberta wholesale distributor may retain 100% of foreign-currency export proceeds indefinitely, hold USD/EUR/CNH in domestic or offshore accounts, and convert them in the spot market at any time. The only binding requirements are reporting-based: physical import/export of currency or monetary instruments of CAD 10,000 or more must be declared to the Canada Border Services Agency under the Cross-Border Currency and Monetary Instruments Reporting Regulations (PCMLTFA); financial institutions must record and report international EFTs of CAD 10,000 or more to FINTRAC; and the corporation must maintain export invoices, customs declarations, and settlement evidence to satisfy banking AML monitoring and CRA audit requirements.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) — central bank, sole issuer of Canadian bank notes, operator of the Lynx high-value settlement system, and supervisor of retail payment service providers under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA). The Department of Finance Canada (Minister of Finance) administers the Exchange Fund Account and foreign exchange reserve policy; OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions) licenses and supervises federally chartered Schedule I banks; FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada) enforces cross-border currency reporting and electronic funds transfer monitoring under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); ISED (Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada) administers the Investment Canada Act for inbound non-Canadian investment. Canada maintains no exchange-control authority and imposes no pre-approval, licence, quota, or central-bank permit for current-account or capital-account payments.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada imposes no statutory Local File/Master File threshold; however, transfer-pricing penalty under s.247(3) may apply when the aggregate value of specified cross-border transactions exceeds CAD 5 million and documentation is not produced within 90 days of a CRA request. Country-by-country reporting is mandatory for multinational groups with consolidated annual revenue of CAD 750 million or more.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain separate Economic Substance Regulations. The CRA applies the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) to deny tax benefits from transactions lacking bona fide economic substance, and the statutory transfer-pricing rules require the pricing to correspond to the substance of the transaction. A shell company or conduit may be re-characterized and treaty benefits denied.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion – False statements or willful misconduct

Statutory sanction: Civil penalty equal to 50% of the tax evaded (Income Tax Act s.163), plus interest and the unpaid tax. Criminal conviction under the Criminal Code s.380 may trigger a fine of up to 200% of the evaded amount and imprisonment for up to 5 years; provincial penalties also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable under s.227.1 for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST; if they participated in or consented to the evasion, they can be assessed the civil penalty and may be criminally prosecuted.
Shell Company / Conduit Abuse and Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: The CRA may disregard the transaction under GAAR, recharacterize the payments as dividends, and apply a 25% withholding tax. A gross-negligence penalty of 10% of the misreported amount (s.163.2) and interest may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false tax returns or authorize the conduit arrangement may be personally liable for penalties and the unremitted withholding tax; they are exposed to criminal prosecution for conspiracy to evade tax.
Anti-Money Laundering and Sanctions Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: False or incomplete reports under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act attract administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation; criminal offence convictions can result in fines of up to CAD 2,000,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly fail to implement required AML controls can be personally fined and imprisoned; the Crown can impose director liability for the corporation’s AML obligations if the director had operational authority.
