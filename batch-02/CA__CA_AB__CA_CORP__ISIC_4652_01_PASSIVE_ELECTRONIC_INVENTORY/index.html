Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA); primary digital channels are Business Registration Online (BRO) and CRA My Business Account. Corporate income tax, GST/HST, payroll, import/export and other program accounts are administered through the CRA. Import and export customs registration and reporting are administered by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) through the CARM client portal. Alberta provincial corporate registry matters are administered by Alberta Corporate Registry, while Alberta has no general provincial sales tax.

E-government filing portal

A corporation incorporated in Canada generally obtains a nine-digit Business Number (BN) from the CRA through Business Registration Online, telephone registration, or the applicable incorporation and registration process. The corporation then opens the required CRA program accounts, including an income tax account (RC), GST/HST account (RT), payroll account (RP), and import-export account (RM), as applicable. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada, making taxable supplies in Canada, importing commercial goods, or otherwise having a Canadian tax filing obligation must apply to the CRA for a BN and relevant program accounts. Non-residents generally use CRA Form RC1 or the CRA non-resident registration process where BRO eligibility is unavailable and may need to appoint an authorized representative or provide supporting incorporation, ownership, business-activity, Canadian-address, banking, and treaty-residency documentation. GST/HST registration requires disclosure of taxable supplies, expected turnover, effective date, place of business, and whether the registrant is resident or non-resident. Commercial importers and exporters should obtain an RM account and complete CARM enrollment before accounting for commercial imports. The BN and program-account numbers are statutory identifiers; a GST/HST number normally consists of the BN followed by the RT program identifier and four account digits.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A Canadian business corporation is generally a separate taxpayer and has no United States-style check-the-box election. It files a T2 Corporation Income Tax Return and is taxed on its own income, subject to the applicable federal and Alberta corporate tax rules, treaty provisions, and foreign-affiliate rules. Statutory elections may affect particular transactions or regimes, including GST/HST reporting periods, cash or electronic filing arrangements where available, section 85 rollover elections, capital dividend account elections, foreign-affiliate elections, and treaty-based positions. A corporation may also be incorporated as an unlimited liability corporation in certain Canadian jurisdictions, but that is a legal-form choice rather than a general tax check-the-box election. Canadian hybrid-mismatch rules can deny deductions, impose income inclusions, or otherwise neutralize deduction/non-inclusion, double-deduction, and imported mismatch outcomes; they do not provide a general entity-classification election. A corporation may seek treaty relief or competent-authority assistance, but treaty residence and permanent-establishment status are determined under applicable law and facts.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding supplies of financial services and generally excluding goodwill and sales of capital personal property, measured over a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters. A person exceeding the threshold in a single calendar quarter generally becomes a registrant from the effective date prescribed by the Excise Tax Act. A person exceeding the threshold over four consecutive calendar quarters generally must register by the applicable deadline. Voluntary registration is permitted before the threshold is exceeded, subject to statutory eligibility and compliance requirements. Non-residents may be required to register even where the ordinary small-supplier threshold does not apply, depending on Canadian taxable supplies, solicitation, delivery, inventory, agents, and other nexus facts.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods supplied in Canada may generally be zero-rated where the goods are exported from Canada in circumstances meeting the Excise Tax Act regulations, including export by or on behalf of the supplier or purchaser and documentary evidence that the goods were exported and were not consumed, used, or supplied in Canada before export, subject to specific statutory exceptions. The supplier must retain commercially reliable evidence such as export declarations, carrier records, bills of lading or air waybills, delivery records, customs records, purchase orders, invoices, and proof of destination. Goods shipped to a non-resident purchaser are not automatically zero-rated solely because the purchaser is non-resident. Services supplied to a non-resident may be zero-rated under the applicable place-of-supply rules, but exclusions apply, including services relating to real property situated in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada, certain personal services performed in Canada, and other prescribed or specifically excluded services. Exporters may generally recover GST paid on eligible commercial inputs through input tax credits if they are GST registrants, maintain prescribed records, and satisfy attribution and documentary requirements. Non-resident rebate relief is narrower and depends on the statutory rebate category and facts.

  • Canadian export declaration or applicable CBSA export reporting record, including required electronic export information and permit information where applicable
  • Commercial invoice identifying exporter, purchaser or consignee, description, quantity, value, currency, terms of sale, country of origin where relevant, and destination
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, courier record, or other carrier documentation evidencing movement from Canada to a foreign destination
  • Packing list and shipment or delivery records where necessary to substantiate the exported goods
  • Customs entry or import documentation, or other destination-country evidence, where reasonably available
  • Proof of payment or other commercial records linking the shipment to the sale
  • GST/HST invoice and accounting records showing zero-rated treatment and the statutory basis
  • Inward remittance certificate is not a general Canadian statutory requirement for GST zero-rating; payment and banking evidence may nevertheless be relevant audit support

Canada and Alberta do not generally impose a universal real-time electronic invoicing, electronic tax-clearance, or digital invoice-stamping mandate for ordinary domestic B2B sales. GST registrants must issue invoices, receipts, or other documentary evidence containing sufficient information to support the tax charged and recipient input tax credits, including the supplier identity, GST/HST registration number where required, transaction date, description, consideration, tax rate, and tax amount or calculation method. CRA electronic filing and online-account requirements apply to specified taxpayers and circumstances, but they are not a real-time invoice-clearance system. Records must generally be retained for the statutory period, commonly six years from the end of the relevant year, subject to longer-period rules and audit or objection extensions. Customs declarations and supporting commercial documents are submitted electronically through CBSA systems, including CARM where applicable. Electronic invoicing may be required contractually or under particular public-sector procurement arrangements, but no general Alberta clearance mandate applies to the stated distribution activity.

Canada generally has no statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds and does not impose a general requirement that export receipts be remitted to Canada within 180 days. CRA may reassess GST/HST where zero-rating is unsupported, the supply was not exported, the goods were consumed or used in Canada, the service was excluded from zero-rating, or records are inadequate. The reassessment may recover tax that should have been charged, deny related input tax credits or rebates, and impose interest. Administrative penalties may apply for false statements, omissions, late filing, late remittance, failure to register, failure to maintain records, or repeated non-compliance; gross-negligence penalties may apply where the statutory test is met. Deliberate fraud, tax evasion, false documentation, or participation in a false-invoice arrangement may result in civil penalties, prosecution, fines, and imprisonment. CBSA may impose customs penalties, seize or detain goods, reassess duties and taxes, and apply the Administrative Monetary Penalty System for inaccurate or unsupported export or customs declarations. There is no fixed universal export-VAT clawback amount; liability is determined by the reassessed GST/HST, interest, applicable penalties, and the facts of the contravention.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both the capital and current accounts under a liberalized OECD framework. Canada maintains no exchange-control legislation, no central-bank payment approvals, and no restrictions on profit or capital repatriation across its borders. Dividends, interest, royalties, and management fees may be freely remitted to non-resident shareholders once (i) non-resident withholding tax under Section 212 of the Canadian Income Tax Act and any applicable income tax treaty are correctly handled, (ii) FINTRAC reporting thresholds are respected, and (iii) the bank’s credit execution and sanctions screening have passed. No advance authorization is required from the Bank of Canada or the Minister of Finance for ordinary treasury flows, and no central bank approval is required to open transactional or escrow bank accounts for a Canadian business corporation. The only material currency-related compliance obligations are FINTRAC’s reporting rules for transactions of CAD 10,000 or more, the electronic funds transfer reporting regime, CRS/FATCA attestation and beneficial-ownership disclosure under the Canada Business Corporations Act and Alberta Business Corporations Act. The Investment Canada Act regulates inward foreign investment review, not foreign exchange conversion, and therefore does not impede the treasury function for a passive electronic inventory distribution entity.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory export proceeds surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion ratio. An exporter may retain 100% of all foreign currency receipts in a foreign-currency bank account in Canada, convert at a rate freely agreed with the banking counterparty, or remit the proceeds offshore. For goods exports, the commercial documents and underlying receivables must be retained in the entity’s records for at least six years under the Income Tax Act, and any receipt in cash, draught, transfer or equivalent of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported to FINTRAC as a large currency transaction or electronic funds transfer report. The bank will require a clear payment description in the SWIFT remittance field and may request the supporting invoice, bill of lading, customs declaration and INCOTERM evidence before releasing funds where the counterparty jurisdiction is classified as higher risk.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-High for payment-concentration and low-value disbursement use, but low-medium as a replacement for a regulated Canadian bank account. Global fintech EMIs operating from the United Kingdom, the EU, Singapore, or Hong Kong may legally service a Canadian entity by opening segregated master accounts with a Canadian Schedule I bank, provided that (i) the EMI is registered as a money services business with FINTRAC when transacting in Canadian dollars or servicing Canadian clients, or uses a registered Canadian MSB as its partner; (ii) the funds are held by the partner bank, not by the EMI as a deposit-taker; and (iii) the solution cannot offer commercial documentary letters of credit or guarantee facilities in the manner of a full-service Canadian bank. A Canadian passive-components distributor should treat an EMI as a secondary liquidity-clearing rail for distributor payments, cross-border payroll or vendor disbursement, while keeping primary operating and capital accounts at a Tier 1 Schedule I bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections into the Alberta-based entity should be executed using an international SWIFT MT103 wire transfer clearly identifying the ordering customer, the exact Canadian corporate beneficiary, and the beneficiary account IBAN or Canadian transit/account number. The remittance information field must carry a precise economic purpose, such as 'shareholder loan – recapitalisation', 'paid-up capital contribution', or 'subordinated debt – shareholder advance', and must reference the executed resolution or loan agreement. The bank will require a matching purpose-of-payment code under Statistics Canada's BPM6 balance-of-payments classification, generally 'Direct investment' or 'Other investment – loans', and all supporting corporate records must be uploaded to the bank’s KYC portal before credit. Any inbound wire exceeding CAD 10,000 is reported by the receiving bank to FINTRAC; payment must never be artificially split into smaller transfers. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium-High for payment-concentration and low-value disbursement use, but low-medium as a replacement for a regulated Canadian bank account. Global fintech EMIs operating from the United Kingdom, the EU, Singapore, or Hong Kong may legally service a Canadian entity by opening segregated master accounts with a Canadian Schedule I bank, provided that (i) the EMI is registered as a money services business with FINTRAC when transacting in Canadian dollars or servicing Canadian clients, or uses a registered Canadian MSB as its partner; (ii) the funds are held by the partner bank, not by the EMI as a deposit-taker; and (iii) the solution cannot offer commercial documentary letters of credit or guarantee facilities in the manner of a full-service Canadian bank. A Canadian passive-components distributor should treat an EMI as a secondary liquidity-clearing rail for distributor payments, cross-border payroll or vendor disbursement, while keeping primary operating and capital accounts at a Tier 1 Schedule I bank.
