Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); primary portals: Business Registration Online (BRO) for BN/program-account activation and My Business Account (MBA) for filings, assessments, and account administration.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Alberta Business Corporations Act (no minimum capital; 100% foreign ownership permitted; registered office and resident director required for CBCA incorporation). Step 2: Obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN) through CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1, activating in the same application the Corporation Income Tax (RC) and GST/HST (RT) program accounts; add Import/Export (RM) and Payroll (RP) accounts where applicable. Step 3: GST/HST registration is mandatory before making any taxable supply in Canada once worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; a marine-fuel wholesale broker exceeds this threshold at inception and must register prior to first invoicing. Step 4: The BN with RT suffix (e.g., 123456789 RT0001) is the sole GST/VAT identifier and must be displayed on all tax invoices and customs documentation. Non-resident corporations making taxable supplies in Canada may likewise register thorough BRO and may be required to post CRA-acceptable security.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — a federally or provincially incorporated business corporation is deemed resident in Canada under Income Tax Act s. 250(4)(a), taxed on worldwide income under Part I at the corporate level, and cannot elect pass-through treatment. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a federally or provincially incorporated business corporation is deemed resident in Canada under Income Tax Act s. 250(4)(a), taxed on worldwide income under Part I at the corporate level, and cannot elect pass-through treatment. Combined 2025 headline rate: federal 15% + Alberta 8% = 23%; the reduced federal 9% and Alberta 2% small-business rates on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income are available only to Canadian-controlled private corporations and are unavailable to a foreign-controlled commodity-trading subsidiary. A foreign parent operating through a permanent establishment in Alberta bears the same combined rate on Canadian-source business income plus 25% Part XIV branch tax on unremitted after-tax profits (subject to treaty relief).

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists; entity-level taxation is mandatory for every Canadian business corporation. Elections relevant to a fuel-trading group include: (i) ETA s. 279 GST/HST group registration election consolidating closely related resident corporations into one GST/HST filer; (ii) ETA s. 156 election deeming supplies between closely related corporations to be made for no consideration; (iii) ETA s. 150 election to treat otherwise-exempt financial services as taxable, enabling recovery of input tax credits on financing and guarantee fees; (iv) hybrid-mismatch rules under ITA s. 18.2–18.4 apply automatically (no elective opt-in) and disallow deductions to the extent that structured payments create double non-taxation or deduction-without-inclusion outcomes; (v) zero-rating of exports under ETA Schedule VI, Part V is mandatory once statutory conditions are met and requires no election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies over any trailing four-consecutive-quarter (12-month) window (the 'small supplier' threshold); registration is compulsory from the earlier of the day the threshold is exceeded or the day the first taxable supply is made in Canada. Nexus: GST/HST applies to taxable supplies made in Canada (ETA s. 165), to importations of goods, and to supplies solicited in Canada by non-residents (ETA s. 143); a fuel wholesale broker located in Alberta transacting with Canadian or non-resident counterparties crosses the threshold at inception and must register before any supply, while brokers supplying goods or services into HST provinces (e.g., Ontario 13%, Nova Scotia 15%) must collect the applicable provincial HST component under place-of-supply rules.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating at 0% GST is available under ETA Schedule VI, Part V on three principal bases: (i) s. 1 — supply of goods exported by the supplier as part of the supply, where the goods are exported as soon after the supply as is reasonable given the circumstances (CRA administrative practice accepts bunker fuel delivered to a vessel of a non-resident operator for use as international-voyage stores where the vessel departs a Canadian port for a foreign destination); (ii) s. 2 — supply of goods to a non-resident purchaser who is not a registrant and who exports the goods from Canada; (iii) s. 7 — supply of brokerage or freight-arrangement services to a non-resident who is not a registrant, provided the service is not performed in respect of real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time it is performed. Domestic supply of marine fuel to Canadian carriers or for purely domestic voyages remains subject to 5% GST; supplies into HST/PST provinces attract the applicable provincial component under place-of-supply rules. The broker must maintain contemporaneous evidencing export, non-resident status, and receipt of proceeds.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Form A8A/B13) filed through the CBSA Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) or G7 Customs, with expenditure codes, HS 2710 classification, destination country, and declared value.
  • Bill of Lading or Marine Bunker Delivery Note evidencing outbound carriage or vessel bunkering in international commercial service with a foreign port of discharge and the non-resident operator's name.
  • Inward Remittance Certificate, SWIFT MT103, or bank credit advice evidencing settlement of export proceeds in foreign currency from the non-resident counterparty.

No mandatory e-invoicing, real-time fiscal clearance, API tax clearance, or digital stamping regime exists in Canada. Electronic invoices are valid if they meet the record-keeping requirements of ETA s. 286 and are retained for six years. The CRA is progressively adopting the voluntary Pan-Canadian 'Pegasus' e-invoicing framework (PEPPOL standard), and mandatory participation applies only to government-as-buyer transactions. Since January 1, 2025, all GST/HST registrants must file returns electronically via GST/HST NETFILE, GST/HST TELEFILE, or CRA-certified software; no state-run real-time API clearance is required.

CRA export verifiers audit proof of physical export within a 'reasonable time,' non-resident status, supporting contracts, and matching of remittances. Normal reassessment window is four years from the filing due date (ETA s. 298(4)); the window is unlimited for fraud, misrepresentation, or wilful neglect. Denial of zero-rating triggers self-assessment of 5% GST on the full invoice value, retrospective clawback of all associated input tax credits, and prescribed-rate interest compounded daily. Additional penalties: false-statement penalty under ETA s. 285.1 equal to the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the tax sought to be evaded; failure-to-remit penalties under ETA s. 280 (6% of the unpaid amount plus 1% per month, up to 12 months); CBSA Administrative Monetary Penalty System (AMPS) assessments of up to CAD 25,000 per inaccurate export declaration; and criminal liability for wilful evasion under ETA s. 327 — fine up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment up to two years on summary conviction, or up to five years on indictment. Records supporting zero-rated export claims must be retained for six years (ETA s. 286).

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada observes IMF Article VIII. Current-account and capital-account transactions are freely convertible; profit, dividend, royalty, and interest remittances require no central bank approval, but are subject to Part XIII withholding tax (25% statutory, often reduced by treaty). Practical frictions arise from bank-level AML/KYC due diligence, FINTRAC reporting for transactions over CAD 10,000, and documentary evidence of bona fide trade.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export-proceeds surrender or repatriation timeline exists; the mandatory conversion ratio is effectively 0%. Exporters may retain, invest, or use foreign-currency export proceeds without conversion into Canadian dollars, provided they maintain proper tax-basis valuation, commercial records, and FINTRAC compliance for large cross-border movements.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central monetary authority; the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) regulates banks; FINTRAC administers cross-border transaction reporting. Canada does not maintain a dedicated Foreign Exchange Control Board or FX approval authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low feasibility as a primary treasury rail. Offshore EMIs cannot accept deposits, issue letters of credit, or provide commercial credit in Canada; they must be registered as money services businesses with FINTRAC and, once in force, comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA). Such platforms are suitable only for small ancillary cross-border payments and never for trade financing, escrow, or high-limit settlements.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Capital injections are routed by SWIFT MT103 using explicit purpose codes: 'INV' for equity subscription or contribution, 'LOAN' for intercompany shareholder loans, and 'GIFT' for non-refundable parent funding. Each transfer must be supported by a board resolution, share subscription agreement or notarized loan agreement, source-of-wealth records, and an Investment Canada Act analysis where thresholds are triggered. The receiving Canadian bank reports cross-border transfers over CAD 10,000 to FINTRAC; funds are credited to the Capital Escrow Account in CAD or USD with beneficial-owner tags and documentary trace.Settlement uses SWIFT MT103 (ISO 20022 remittance) via the Canadian bank's correspondent network, especially improved global payments innovation (gpi) for real-time tracking. Commodity trades require Commercial Letters of Credit, standby LCs, or documentary collections as agreed; CAD payments clear through LVTS in real time. Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined (15% federal general rate reduction + 8% Alberta provincial flat rate); 11% for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPC) on first CA$500,000 active business income

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII Income Tax Act (gross) on dividends paid to non-residents.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CA$1,000,000 aggregate fair market value of transactions with non-arm's-length non-residents during the taxation year triggers T106 reporting and contemporaneous documentation under IC 87-2R; preparer of Master/Local File may also be required on CRA request irrespective of size.

Economic substance scope: Not applicable as separate Economic Substance Regulations in Canada. Substance is evaluated through the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) and treaty-abuse rules; a corporate vehicle lacking employees, premises, or actual commodity trading functions may be disregarded for Canadian tax purposes or defeated for treaty relief.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.239(1): indictable offence – fine of 50% to 200% of tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years. Civil gross-negligence penalty under s.163(2): 50% of understated tax, plus interest and reassessment.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly authorized evasion are personally liable to civil reassessment penalties. In criminal cases, directors are prosecuted individually and face imprisonment. Travel bans are common pending plea/allocation hearings.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.247(3): penalty 10% of the net upward transfer-pricing adjustment; failure to file T106 carries an additional penalty of CA$1,000 and CA$100 per day for each day overdue (maximum CA$1,500).

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily liable for transfer-pricing penalties unless they signed false or reckless statements, in which case they may be assessed for negligence under s.163(2) and subject to criminal prosecution.
Shell Company / GAAR Abuse

Statutory sanction: General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) nullifies the tax benefit and imposes a 25% penalty under s.245(5) plus statutory interest; reassessment may reallocate income to the actual operator.

Director personal liability: Directors who are beneficiaries of the sham shell may have the corporate veil pierced under common law if the shell is used for fraud or unjust enrichment; CRA may jointly reassess directors for unpaid corporate taxes; serious cases trigger criminal tax evasion charges and travel restrictions.
AML / Sanctions Violations

Statutory sanction: Special Economic Measures Act (SEMA): criminal fine in the court's discretion and imprisonment up to 10 years. FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties for non-compliance with the PCMLTFA can reach CA$1,000,000 per violation for corporations.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally exposed to criminal prosecution for laundering proceeds of crime or breaching economic sanctions; civil asset forfeiture and (for non‑Canadians) foreign state travel bans are common. Ill‑gotten profits in commission accounts are subject to confiscation.
