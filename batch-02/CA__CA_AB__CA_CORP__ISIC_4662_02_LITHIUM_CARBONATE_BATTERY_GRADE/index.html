Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital or escrow obligation applies under Alberta law to a wholesale battery-metals arbitrage corporation. Articles may establish one or more classes of shares, including no-par-value common shares, and paid-up capital must not exceed the consideration actually received for the issued shares. A nominal subscription of one common share (CAD 1) is customary.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), administered primarily through Business Registration Online and My Business Account. Alberta corporate income tax matters are administered by Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration; Alberta has no general provincial sales tax.

E-government filing portal

A foreign corporation should establish its Canadian legal or taxable business presence and obtain a nine-digit Business Number (BN) from the CRA through Business Registration Online, telephone registration, or Form RC1 where applicable. It must provide legal name, incorporation jurisdiction, registered address, directors, business activities, accounting period, and authorized representative information. It should then open the applicable program accounts: RT for GST/HST, RM for import-export activities, RP for payroll if applicable, and RC for corporate income tax where required. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada, making taxable supplies in Canada, importing commercial goods, or otherwise meeting Canadian registration criteria may require a BN and GST/HST registration. CRA may require supporting incorporation documents, proof of identity, Canadian business information, and security for a non-resident GST/HST registrant. Importers must also obtain a Canada Border Services Agency importer account through the CARM Client Portal and maintain an RM import-export program account. GST/HST registration is generally effective from the date specified by the CRA or the date the statutory registration obligation arises. Provincial corporate registration with the Alberta Corporate Registry is separate from CRA tax registration.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no general United States-style check-the-box election for corporations. A Canadian corporation is generally a separate taxpayer subject to federal and Alberta corporate income tax on its taxable income. Relevant elections may include a section 85 Income Tax Act rollover for qualifying transfers of eligible property to a taxable Canadian corporation, section 86 share reorganizations, amalgamation and winding-up elections, and treaty-based entity characterization positions where applicable. Canadian hybrid-mismatch rules under sections 12(1)(l.1), 18.4 and related provisions can deny deductions or impose inclusion consequences for deduction/non-inclusion and imported mismatch arrangements. A foreign entity cannot generally elect to be treated as a Canadian flow-through entity merely by filing a classification election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of taxable supplies, generally measured over four consecutive calendar quarters or exceeded in a single calendar quarter. A person exceeding the small-supplier threshold must register and begin charging GST at the applicable statutory time. The threshold does not generally exempt a non-resident that is required to register under special rules, including certain non-resident suppliers and persons importing taxable supplies in the course of commercial activities.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods supplied in Canada and exported from Canada may generally qualify for zero-rating where the supplier ships or delivers the goods outside Canada, or the purchaser exports them in circumstances satisfying the Excise Tax Act rules. The supplier must retain objective evidence that the goods left Canada and must not treat a Canadian domestic delivery as an export merely because the purchaser is non-resident. Services supplied to a non-resident may qualify for zero-rating where the statutory non-resident and place-of-supply conditions are met, subject to exclusions for services relating to Canadian real property, tangible personal property, litigation, and other specified Canadian activities. Zero-rating does not eliminate customs, export-control, sanctions, dangerous-goods, or import requirements in the destination country. Input tax credits and GST refunds require valid tax documentation, commercial-purpose evidence, and records linking purchases to zero-rated or other commercial activities.

  • Commercial invoice identifying the exporter, purchaser or consignee, goods, value, currency, terms, and country of origin
  • Export declaration or electronic export report through the Canadian Export Reporting System where an export declaration is legally required; certain goods, destinations, values, and controlled goods may be subject to specific reporting rules
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, carrier manifest, or equivalent transport evidence showing movement from Canada to a destination outside Canada
  • Packing list and product description, including tariff classification and lithium-carbonate specifications where relevant
  • Proof of export or delivery outside Canada, such as carrier confirmation, customs exit evidence, or foreign import documentation
  • Import permits, export permits, certificates of origin, and dangerous-goods documentation where required by the goods, destination, trade agreement, or transport mode
  • GST/HST sales invoices, contracts, purchase orders, accounting records, and evidence supporting the zero-rated treatment
  • Evidence of consideration and settlement, such as bank records or payment confirmations; an Inward Remittance Certificate is not generally a Canadian statutory requirement

Canada has no general real-time GST invoice-clearance, electronic fiscal-stamping, or mandatory e-invoicing API regime for ordinary business-to-business transactions. Electronic invoices and electronic accounting records are generally acceptable if they contain prescribed information, support the recipient's input tax credit claim, and remain accessible and reliable for the statutory retention period. A GST registrant must generally issue documentary support showing supplier identity and GST/HST registration number, invoice date, transaction description, consideration, applicable GST/HST rate, and tax amount, with enhanced information required for higher-value supplies. CARM electronic customs processes apply to commercial imports, but they are not a GST invoice-clearance system.

Canada does not generally impose a mandatory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds. The CRA may reassess GST, deny zero-rating, recover improperly claimed input tax credits or rebates, and charge arrears interest where export conditions or substantiation requirements are not met. False statements or omissions may result in gross-negligence penalties, generally 50% of the related understated tax or improperly claimed amount, and other administrative penalties. Repeated or deliberate non-compliance may lead to criminal prosecution, fines, and imprisonment. Customs non-compliance may trigger CBSA Administrative Monetary Penalty System assessments, seizure or forfeiture, reassessment of duties and taxes, and sanctions for false export or import declarations. Records supporting zero-rated supplies and tax claims must be retained for the applicable Canadian statutory period, generally six years from the end of the relevant tax year.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current and capital account. Canada maintains no exchange-control law, no requirement for profit-remittance licensing, and no limitation on dividend, interest, royalty, or proceeds repatriation for Canadian business corporations. The only statutory gates are (i) Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on outbound remittances (25% statutory rate, treaty-reduced under specific tax treaties, e.g., Canada-U.S. treaty rates of 5-15% for dividends and generally 0-15% for interest/royalties), and (ii) mandatory FINTRAC reporting of international electronic fund transfers of CAD 10,000 or more. Inward and outward capital flows require no Bank of Canada authorization; formalities are limited to bank-level know-your-client checks and corporate-power validation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes zero export-proceeds surrender requirements and zero mandatory conversion ratios. Export receipts may be retained in any freely convertible currency in authorized foreign-currency accounts at Canadian chartered banks. Statutory obligations are confined to (i) customs documentation under the Customs Act and Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) processes, including B13A export declarations where applicable, (ii) FINTRAC prospective reporting of international funds transfers equal to or exceeding CAD 10,000, and (iii) standard corporate record-retention for transfer-pricing and audit evidence. No statutory repatriation timeline exists; commercial receivable terms govern the timing of collection and conversion.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary and settlement authority; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as prudential bank regulator; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as statutory transaction-reporting agency; Canada has no standalone foreign-exchange-control ministry or exchange-approval regime.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Conditional Medium. Federally regulated payment-service-provider fintechs registered under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) and restricted-purpose trust companies can be used as secondary pass-through collection accounts. Offshore non-Canadian digital bank and e-money-institution (EMI) accounts are legally constrained: they are not Schedule I/II Canadian banks, do not hold CDIC-insured deposit status, cannot directly access the Lynx (formerly LVTS) high-value clearing system or SWIFT settlement membership, and must themselves register as money services businesses (MSB) with FINTRAC when serving Canadian clients. Use of such EMIs for core treasury is not viable as the primary clearing rail; transactions are limited to small-value supplier onboarding, expense disbursements, and cross-border receivables collection. All funds sweeping from EMIs to the Schedule I primary bank account must be accompanied by source-of-funds and commercial-invoice documentation to avoid enhanced-due-diligence downgrades.

Federally regulated payment-service-provider fintechs registered under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) and restricted-purpose trust companies can be used as secondary pass-through collection accounts. Offshore non-Canadian digital bank and e-money-institution (EMI) accounts are legally constrained: they are not Schedule I/II Canadian banks, do not hold CDIC-insured deposit status, cannot directly access the Lynx (formerly LVTS) high-value clearing system or SWIFT settlement membership, and must themselves register as money services businesses (MSB) with FINTRAC when serving Canadian clients. Use of such EMIs for core treasury is not viable as the primary clearing rail; transactions are limited to small-value supplier onboarding, expense disbursements, and cross-border receivables collection. All funds sweeping from EMIs to the Schedule I primary bank account must be accompanied by source-of-funds and commercial-invoice documentation to avoid enhanced-due-diligence downgrades.
