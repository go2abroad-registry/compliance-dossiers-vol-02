Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) - federal competent authority for GST/HST, corporate income tax and Business Number registration; portals: canada.ca/en/revenue-agency and Business Registration Online (BRO); GST/HST returns are filed via GST/HST NETFILE. The Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) administers customs/export reporting, and Alberta Corporate Registry (alberta.ca/corporate-registry) administers corporate legal registration; Alberta Corporate Tax is administered by CRA on behalf of Alberta and requires no separate provincial tax ID.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Alberta Business Corporations Act via Corporations Canada or the Alberta Corporate Registry to obtain a corporation file number. Step 2: Obtain the 9-digit Business Number (BN) from CRA through Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts'. Step 3: Open program accounts under the same BN: 'RT' for GST/HST (mandatory when taxable supplies made in Canada exceed CAD 30,000 in a calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters; voluntary below; required to claim 0% export GST and input tax credits), and 'RM' for CBSA import/export reporting (required to file electronic export declarations in the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS)). Step 4: Add 'RC' payroll account if the corporation has Canadian employees. Step 5: No separate Alberta tax registration is needed; Alberta's 8% corporate income tax is coordinated by CRA under the Alberta Corporate Tax Act and the province's single-administration agreement. Step 6: A corporation incorporated outside Alberta that carries on business in Alberta must complete extra-provincial registration with the Alberta Corporate Registry and appoint an Alberta attorney for service; this is corporate capacity registration, not a tax ID. The BN is the statutory TIN for GST/HST and corporate income tax purposes (format: 123456789RT0001).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a US-style check-the-box regime; a Business Corporation is always an entity-level taxpayer of federal corporate income tax and Alberta corporate income tax, and no hybrid entity-characterization election exists. Relevant statutory elections: ETA s. 156(2) election between closely related corporations (registered recipient; supplies made for nil consideration to facilitate group-level GST neutrality); ETA s. 273 GST/HST joint venture election (participants elect a single operator to file and remit GST on the joint venture's supplies); simplified filing arrangement for eligible non-resident GST registrants with no Canadian commercial activity (CRA administrative program described in Guide RC4022); and Income Tax Act s. 115.1 election available to a non-resident corporation to compute Canadian tax on treaty principles (business profits attributable only to a Canadian permanent establishment). Anti-hybrid rules in ITA ss. 18.4-18.7 disallow deduction/non-inclusion outcomes arising from hybrid mismatches, including those involving foreign disregarded entities.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of taxable supplies made in Canada in either a single calendar quarter or the last four consecutive calendar quarters (the ETA's small-supplier exemption threshold); registration is mandatory once the threshold is exceeded and is voluntary below it. For the scrap copper wire export business, GST registration is commercially mandatory to support zero-rated export invoices and to recover input tax credits on Canadian acquisition, logistics and processing costs.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A GST/HST registrant's sale of shredded scrap copper wire qualifies for the 0% export rate under ETA s. 16(1) and Schedule VI, Part V, when: (a) the sale transfers ownership of the goods; (b) the goods are shipped from Canada to a foreign destination outside Canada as part of a continuous journey or delivered to a non-resident purchaser or its agent; and (c) the supplier obtains and retains contemporaneous documentary evidence that the goods were in fact exported within a reasonable period after the sale (CRA administrative practice generally expects export within 60-90 days of the invoice date). The goods must not be consumed or sold domestically before export. Domestic resale of scrap copper within Canada is taxable at 5% GST; the registrant must segregate export sales (0%) from domestic taxable sales (5%) on the same return. Sale terms such as FOB Alberta warehouse or Ex Works are acceptable provided the exporter maintains the transport/customs evidence of exportation to the foreign consignee.

  • CBSA Export Declaration (electronic B13A) filed via the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS), generating a Proof of Report Number; required for shipments valued over CAD 2,000 or for controlled goods
  • Commercial invoice and purchase/sales contract naming the non-resident buyer and stating the destination
  • Bill of Lading (ocean, air, or truck) or Cargo Control Document naming the non-resident consignee and evidencing departure from Canada
  • Inward remittance evidence - SWIFT MT103, documentary LC settlement advice, or bank credit confirmation proving receipt of foreign currency from the foreign buyer (Canada has no statutory Inward Remittance Certificate; bank remittance advice is the accepted proof)
  • Weight ticket and assay/analysis certificate for the copper content of the shredded scrap (commercial best practice for valuation and customs valuation support)
  • Supply-chain records (purchase invoices from Alberta/Canadian suppliers) demonstrating the recycled scrap origin and linking the domestic acquisition to the export sale

Canada has no real-time fiscal clearance, no mandatory e-invoicing clearinghouse, and no digital-stamping/API fiscal control regime. Invoices must contain the prescribed GST information under ETA ss. 169(1)(b) and 221-223 (supplier name, BN/GST registration number, date, description, consideration and tax status) and may be issued on paper, PDF, XML or EDI without CRA pre-approval. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically via GST/HST NETFILE, with mandatory electronic filing and electronic remittance for registrants whose annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 1,500,000; lower-volume registrants may file paper returns until the threshold is reached. Export declarations are subject to mandatory electronic clearance through the CBSA Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS), and the exporter must obtain a Proof of Report Number as the digital clearance record for each export shipment.

CRA targets scrap-metal exporters for audit, particularly where export evidence is photocopied, backdated, or incomplete. If the zero-rating is reversed, the exporter is assessed for the 5% GST that should have been charged on the full sale price, plus interest at the CRA quarterly prescribed rate and penalties; the normal reassessment period is four years for GST/HST, but the period is unlimited where the return contains neglect, carelessness, wilful default, or fraud. Civil penalties under the Excise Tax Act include: s. 285 false-statement penalty (greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the tax sought to be evaded) for knowingly false export documentation; s. 280 failure-to-remit penalty of 5% of the net GST owing plus 1% per month compounded. Criminal tax evasion under ETA s. 327 exposes the corporation and its officers to fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to five years on indictment. Separately, CBSA may impose administrative monetary penalties under the Customs Act (up to CAD 50,000 per false export declaration) and suspend the exporter's RM account. Although Canada imposes no statutory 180-day FX repatriation deadline, the 180-day benchmark is adopted here because CRA audit practice requires foreign payment within a reasonable commercial period and financial institutions will flag stale export receivables for anti-money-laundering review.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has maintained full and unrestricted convertibility of the Canadian dollar on both current and capital accounts since repeal of the Foreign Exchange Control Act in 1951. Non-residents may freely inject equity, repatriate capital, remit dividends, and settle trade obligations without central bank approval. The only regulatory encumbrances are: (i) FINTRAC reporting of all electronic funds transfers at or above CAD 10,000; (ii) Canadian sanctions screening under the Special Economic Measures Act and the United Nations Act; (iii) Part XIII Income Tax Act withholding on cross-border remittances (treaty-reduced where applicable); and (iv) CRS/FATCA self-certification obligations at account onboarding. Profit remittance is therefore a tax-compliance exercise, not a foreign exchange permission exercise.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None applicable. There is no statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no surrender requirement for export proceeds in Canada. A recycling-scrap exporter may retain 100% of foreign currency receipts in CAD or any freely convertible currency, hold funds offshore, or apply them directly to third-party trade payables. All that is required is accurate record-keeping for Canadian income tax and the standard bank/FINTRAC reporting threshold of CAD 10,000 per EFT.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) exercises monetary policy and financial system oversight; prudential supervision of banks is delegated to OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions); AML/CTF administration and cross-border EFT surveillance are vested in FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada). No exchange control authority or foreign exchange administration exists in Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low for primary treasury function; Medium as a supplementary disbursement rail. A Canadian-registered Money Services Business (PCMLTFA-registered MSB) may provide CAD wallets, FX conversion, and contingent-payment mass payout rails, but cannot open a Payments Canada clearing account, cannot issue documentary letters of credit, and cannot act as an OSFI-regulated deposit-taker. Offshore digital EMIs (UK FCA or EU EMD licence) lack access to Canadian clearing systems and are generally unacceptable for the receipt of export proceeds from central-bank-sanctioned trade flows. Use fintech EMIs strictly for minor contingency payouts after the Tier-1 banking nexus is established.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate reportable cross-border non-arm's length transactions triggering Form T106; CRA audits normally request OECD-style Local File and Master File where related-party turnover exceeds CAD 5,000,000 or the s. 247(3) penalty benchmark is the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of the taxpayer's gross revenue from Canadian operations.

Economic substance scope: Alberta does not operate a standalone ESR regime; however, GAAR under ITA s. 245 and the PE attribution rules (Part IV ITA Regulations) re-characterize transactions lacking economic substance. A scrap-copper trading corporation must maintain physical premises, staff capable of negotiating contracts, storage/shipping control, and board-level decision-making in Alberta; otherwise CRA may disregard the Canadian entity as a booking shell, reallocate profit to the offshore principal, apply the 10% transfer-pricing penalty, and, for a non-resident parent, assert 25% Part XIV branch tax on the reallocated Canadian earnings.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: Criminal sanction under ITA s. 239: fine from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded plus imprisonment up to 5 years (indictable) or 2 years (summary); civil gross-negligence penalty under ITA s. 163(2) of 50% of the understated tax and interest; CRA may also assess false-statement penalties under s. 163.2 up to CAD 100,000.

Director personal liability: Directors who directed, authorized, accepted, or acquiesced in the false reporting are jointly exposed to criminal prosecution as parties; conviction can result in imprisonment, restitution orders, seizure of personal assets, and passport surrender as a bail/travel condition; CRA can pursue a director under s. 160(2) for assets transferred out of the corporation to defeat tax liabilities.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance / Shell Intermediation

Statutory sanction: CRA reassessment to the arm's length price under ITA s. 247(2); 10% transfer-pricing penalty on the net upward adjustment (where the aggregate adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of gross revenue from Canadian operations) absent reasonable efforts; T106 penalties of CAD 100/day (max CAD 2,500 per return) and CAD 2,500 to CAD 25,000 for failure to comply with a formal documentation demand.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign the T2 return knowing of the engineered prices face fraud-based civil restitution; in insolvency, CRA's claims for recomputed taxes and penalties rank as unsecured, but directors can become personally liable for unpaid Part XIII withholding and GST/HST remittances under ITA s. 227.1 and ETA s. 323; travel restrictions may arise under Federal Court enforcement of a section 223 certificate if the director's personal assets are garnished.
Trade-Based Money Laundering (TBML) / AML Regime

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 500,000 for a first contravention (individuals) and CAD 1,000,000 for entities, with higher amounts for repeat violations; money laundering offense under Criminal Code s. 462.31 carries a maximum 10-year imprisonment and proceeds-of-crime forfeiture; cross-border trade understatements trigger customs penalties under the Customs Act for false declarations.

Director personal liability: Directors are liable as secondary participants if they knowingly facilitated circularity, and may be made subject to freeze orders, ne exeat regno writs barring travel while assets are at risk, and bail conditions requiring passport surrender; restitution orders can attach to director compensation paid when the trade has no legitimate economic substance.
