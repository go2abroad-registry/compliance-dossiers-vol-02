Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No check-the-box election exists in Canada; entity classification is statutory under the Income Tax Act. Certain hybrid-mismatch elections are available under the Multilateral Instrument (MLI) and domestic recharacterization rules; corporate status (e.g., CCPC vs. public corporation) is determined by statute, not elective.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable (non-zero-rated) supplies over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; a non-resident may also voluntarily register regardless of threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of goods exported from Canada may be zero-rated if tangible personal property is delivered to a common carrier or shipped by freight, insured, and exported by the supplier or customer, with records demonstrating export within a reasonable time. Services provided to non-residents may be zero-rated if performed wholly outside Canada, or in Canada but relating to real property outside Canada, or if ancillary to exported goods.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A or Canadian Automated Export Declaration (CAED))
  • Bill of Lading or other transportation document evidencing shipment
  • Commercial invoice and customs entry documents
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank settlement proof (if applicable)
  • Cargo control and carrier documents, e.g., airway bill, trucker's manifest

Canada currently imposes no real-time e-invoicing clearance, API filing, or digital stamping mandate. Registrants exceeding the small-supplier threshold must file GST/HST returns electronically (GST/HST NETFILE) on a monthly, quarterly, or annual basis; books and records, including electronic invoices, must be retained in an accessible digital or paper format.

False zero-rating claims expose a registrant to GST/HST reassessment, interest, and penalties under the Excise Tax Act, including up to 25% of the net tax misstatement for gross negligence plus a late-filing penalty. Fraudulent intent can trigger criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act and Excise Tax Act, seizure of goods, and revocation of registration. The CRA also conducts export audits requiring proof of shipment and due diligence.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for ordinary current-account and capital-account transactions. Canada generally permits foreign ownership, foreign-currency accounts, cross-border dividends, interest, royalties, loan repayments, capital reductions, and repatriation of lawful profits without prior central-bank approval. The corporation must satisfy its bank's source-of-funds, beneficial-ownership, sanctions, anti-money-laundering, tax, and commercial-purpose reviews. Dividends and other distributions remain subject to applicable corporate-law solvency tests, board approval, withholding-tax rules, treaty eligibility, transfer-pricing requirements, thin-capitalization limitations, shareholder-loan rules, and accurate financial records. Cross-border related-party financing may require arm's-length pricing, contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation, debt-capacity analysis, and withholding-tax review. Payments involving sanctioned persons, sanctioned jurisdictions, restricted goods, money laundering, terrorist financing, or unexplained third-party beneficiaries may be blocked, delayed, reported, or rejected. Canada has no general capital controls, but certain foreign investments and transactions may be reviewed under the Investment Canada Act, the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, Canadian sanctions legislation, export-control laws, competition laws, and tax legislation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No general statutory requirement exists to surrender export proceeds to a Canadian bank, convert export receipts into Canadian dollars, or repatriate export proceeds within a prescribed period. Export proceeds may generally be retained in Canadian-dollar or foreign-currency accounts, subject to contractual banking terms, tax accounting, sanctions screening, and accurate customs and financial reporting. Canada has no universal export-payment purpose-code regime equivalent to jurisdictions that mandate central-bank coding. Banks may nevertheless require transaction descriptions, invoices, bills of lading, customs records, contracts, certificates of origin, end-use information, and counterparty details. Monetary instruments and certain cross-border movements of currency or monetary instruments of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported under applicable border-reporting rules, and international electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more are reportable by financial institutions to FINTRAC in prescribed circumstances. Exporters must comply with Canadian export-control, sanctions, customs, tax, and trade-statistics requirements. Elemental sulfur is not ordinarily subject to a general currency-control restriction, but product classification, dangerous-goods status, destination, end use, and counterparty screening must be confirmed for each shipment.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no general foreign-exchange administration imposing exchange controls or mandatory currency conversion. Relevant compliance authorities also include the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), the Canada Revenue Agency, the Canada Border Services Agency, the Department of Finance Canada, Global Affairs Canada, and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions, depending on the transaction.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for payment operations and low-to-medium as a replacement for a Canadian operating bank. An offshore electronic-money institution or payment-service provider cannot automatically open a Canadian deposit account, provide deposit-taking, or conduct unrestricted payment services in Canada merely because the Canadian corporation is its customer. Canadian payment services may engage the Retail Payment Activities Act and Bank of Canada registration and risk-management requirements, while deposit-taking and banking activities may require authorization under Canadian banking legislation. Foreign providers remain subject to their home-jurisdiction licensing, Canadian sanctions and AML expectations, correspondent-bank screening, beneficial-owner verification, transaction monitoring, data and privacy controls, and possible Canadian registration or licensing requirements. Client funds may be held in safeguarded or segregated accounts rather than insured Canadian deposits. Use should be limited to documented collections, payments, and treasury diversification after confirming the provider's licensing status, Canadian reach, safeguarding model, settlement banks, insolvency protections, CAD availability, and sanctions policy.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% (federal corporate tax 15% + Alberta provincial corporate tax 8%; combined taxable income rate for general non-CCPC corporations)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% on dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder, imposed under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (s.212(2)). No separate Alberta provincial withholding tax exists on dividends; provincial collection is aligned with the federal regime.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 must be filed on or before the day that is six months after the end of the corporation’s fiscal year (e.g., for a December 31 year-end, the filing deadline is June 30 of the following year). The final payment of Part I tax, provincial Alberta tax, and instalments is due on the balance-due day, which is two months after the fiscal year-end (three months for qualified CCPCs; four months for certain agricultural corporations) unless instalment rules apply.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 (aggregate fair market value of non-arm’s-length cross-border transactions in a taxation year that triggers mandatory preparation, prior disclosure, and filing of Form T106 contemporaneous Transfer Pricing Documentation; a lower threshold of C$250,000 may apply for specified service transactions involving related non-residents in a low-tax jurisdiction). For larger multinational groups meeting the consolidated revenue threshold, Country-by-Country Reporting (CbCR) is also required.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a separate ‘Economic Substance Regulations’ law analogous to the UAE-ESR regime; however, CRA and the Canada Revenue Agency apply a substance-over-form doctrine through judicial legal interpretation, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under s.245, corporate-residence jurisprudence (central management and control), treaty Limitation on Benefits and Principal Purpose Test compliance under modern Canadian tax treaties, and the OECD Global Minimum Tax (Pillar Two) rules for multinational groups with consolidated revenue above €750 million. A Canadian entity used only as a mail-drop or invoicing conduit with no employees, premises, business decisions, and operational risk will generally be re-characterized, denied treaty benefits, or assessed as having a permanent establishment in Canada.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False/Deceptive T2 Filing

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act s.239(1), the corporation and its representatives are liable on summary conviction to a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and to imprisonment for up to 2 years; on indictment the imprisonment term increases to up to 5 years and fines up to 200% apply. CRA may also assess civil penalties under s.163(2) for gross negligence: the greater of C$100 and 50% of the understated tax, plus compounded daily interest on the unpaid tax.

Director personal liability: A director who knowingly approves or signs a false T2 return, destroys evidence, or directs an employee to lie to a CRA auditor can be prosecuted as a party to the offence (Criminal Code s.21 and ITA s.239), leading to imprisonment, criminal fines, and an order to make restitution. CRA may also use director assessments under s.227.1 for any unremitted Part XIII/Source withholdings that arose from the fraudulent arrangement; travel bans and asset-freezing orders are available ancillary sanctions in serious criminal tax prosecutions.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing and Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA can reassess the corporation’s taxable income to the arm’s-length amount under s.247(2). Where the net transfer-pricing adjustment exceeds the greater of C$5,000,000 and 10% of the gross revenue for the taxation year, a 10% Transfer Pricing Penalty under s.247(3) applies to the amount of the adjustment; an additional penalty may apply for missing documentation/reporting. Interest accrues on the unpaid tax and penalty from the balance-due day.

Director personal liability: Director civil liability is generally not automatic for transfer-pricing adjustments under s.247. However, directors who knowingly create or tolerate a false intercompany pricing structure that results in tax evasion may be personally prosecuted under the Criminal Code and ITA s.239; in a civil context, directors may be held liable for misfeasance or breach of fiduciary duty where they cause the company to engage in deliberate TP non-compliance, including a claim for restitution by CRA if the corporate entity is defunct or insolvent.
Failure to File T2/T106 and Non-Payment of Part XIII WHT

Statutory sanction: Failure to file the T2 triggers s.162(1) late-filing penalties (5% + 1% monthly, capped at 12 months; 10% + 2% monthly for repeat failures), plus s.161 interest on the unpaid tax. Failure to file T106 can lead to penalties of C$25 per day per return (minimum C$100 and maximum C$2,500 per return); for large corporations, penalties can be C$2,000 or more per return. Unremitted Part XIII tax is a trust-fund amount; CRA collects it from the corporation with statutory interest and may assess the full shortfall as tax arrears.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally and jointly and severally liable under s.227.1 for any unremitted Part XIII withholding tax or employee-source deductions that the corporation was required to remit, if the directors knew or ought to have known of the failure and did not exercise due diligence. A director assessment can be collected from personal assets without the corporate veil barring recovery; failure to pay criminal penalties may be prosecuted, and travel bans are possible only in criminal proceedings under the Income Tax Act or Criminal Code for fraudulent schemes.
Artificial Shell Company and Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: CRA may recharacterize the corporation as a pure conduit, disallow deductible payments and treaty withholding rates, apply the GAAR under s.245 to deny the tax benefit, and impose a GAAR penalty of 50% of the claimed tax advantage (subject to a minimum C$1,000). A deemed dividend can be assessed under s.212(3) if the arrangement funnels profits out of Canada in the form of loan repayments, interest substitutes, or corporation-controlled distributions. In severe cases, the non-resident parent is deemed to have a Canadian permanent establishment, and the company’s exemptions are denied retroactively.

Director personal liability: Directors of an artificial or shell Canadian company may be held personally liable for Part XIII withholding tax that the shell failed to remit (s.227.1), and they expose themselves to criminal fraud prosecutions if they sign false statutory declarations or share certificates. In a treaty-shopping audit, travel bans and asset freezes can be imposed by court order if CRA obtains a criminal investigation warrant; the corporate form is not respected where the director acts as a mere agent of the foreign principal.
Sanctions and Trade-Based Money Laundering

Statutory sanction: Criminal Code provisions on money laundering (ss.462.31 et seq.) carry a maximum prison sentence of five years and fines at the discretion of the court; the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act gives FINTRAC the power to issue administrative monetary penalties of up to C$1,000,000 for reporting violations, and the Canadian Asset Recovery Agency may seize and forfeit property connected to unlawful activity. Violating the Special Economic Measures Act (e.g., trading with designated Russian, Iranian, or North-Korean minerals) is a separate offence resulting in fines and freezing orders.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly authorise or facilitate suspicious cross-border mineral transactions face personal criminal liability, including imprisonment up to 5 years and personal confiscation orders. They can be made subject to travel bans and asset-freezing orders in connection with an AML or sanctions investigation. Even if a director merely failed to act knowingly, FINTRAC can impose monetary penalties against officers and directors who fail to oversee their compliance program, and civil courts may award restitution to CRA or affected third parties for reckless breach of anti-money-laundering duties.
