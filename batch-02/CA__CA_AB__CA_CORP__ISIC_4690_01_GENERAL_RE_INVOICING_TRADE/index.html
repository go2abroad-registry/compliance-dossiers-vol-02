Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital is required under the Alberta Business Corporations Act. At least one incorporator must subscribe to one or more shares; the initial allotment may be for nominal consideration (e.g., CAD $1 per common share). No escrow deposit or regulator-held capital is required; for an Alberta business corporation, solvency tests apply before dividend distributions or share repurchases.

Funding & unlock path: Office premises must satisfy the Alberta Fire Code under the Safety Codes Council, structural load and occupancy permits verified by municipal building inspectors, and workplace clearance under the Occupational Health and Safety (OHS) Code. No special civil defense certification is required for typical business premises; a fire inspection certificate may be requested by insurers or municipal licensing authorities.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — Business Registration Online and My Business Account portal (canada.ca/en/revenue-agency). Alberta's corporate income tax is administered by the CRA under the federal–provincial tax collection agreement.

E-government filing portal

A federal or Alberta business corporation obtains a federal business number (BN) automatically upon incorporation. For GST/HST and customs/import-export accounts, the entity must add program accounts (RT for GST/HST, RM for import/export) via CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1. A foreign corporation carrying on business in Alberta must first extra-provincially register with the Alberta Corporate Registry (Altaview) and then request a BN; the RM program account serves as both the customs identifier and the GST/HST registrant. Supporting identity evidence for non-resident applicants includes a certificate of corporate status, the foreign incorporation number, the Alberta registered-office address, and the name and authority of the in-Canada agent for service.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box election; every incorporated entity is per se a corporation subject to Part I of the Income Tax Act. Hybrid-relevant elections include: (i) ITA s. 88(2) deemed dividend election on vertical winding-up; (ii) ITA s. 93(1) election to re-characterize foreign affiliate accruing income as dividend for exempt-surplus treatment; (iii) ITA s. 113(1) deduction to render intercorporate exempt-surplus dividends tax-free; (iv) Excise Tax Act s. 156 election to treat supplies among closely related corporations as non-supplies (a functional VAT-grouping substitute); (v) ETA s. 150 election permitting financial institutions to stream financial-service supplies. No election exists to classify a Canadian corporation as transparent or as a partnership.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies (exclusive of zero-rated supplies) over any four consecutive calendar quarters; thereafter GST/HST registration is mandatory. Re-invoicing and trade-intermediation fees are taxable supplies of services unless zero-rated by reason of export to a non-resident.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods qualify for 0% GST/HST when they are (i) sold by a GST-registered supplier, (ii) physically exported from Canada by the supplier or by the non-resident purchaser to whom the supplier delivers the goods in Canada for immediate shipment abroad, and (iii) supported by satisfactory documentary proof of export. Services qualify for 0% GST/HST when supplied to a non-resident person, unless the service is in respect of real property situated in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance, or is rendered to an individual physically present in Canada. A commercial re-invoicing hub fee charged to an offshore trading counterparty for arranging, brokering, or re-invoicing goods that never enter Canada will ordinarily satisfy the zero-rating test; where an input tax refund is sought, the CRA applies an export-evidence completeness test.

  • Canadian Customs Export Declaration (CERS submission or customs broker summary)
  • Original or transport-quality Bill of Lading (master or house)
  • Commercial invoice and purchase order matching the exported goods
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or equivalent SWIFT/TT/Letter-of-Credit payment confirmation from the non-resident buyer
  • Transport records, including Incoterms contract, container tracking, and customs release documentation

Canada does not operate a mandatory real-time e-invoicing, government clearance, or digital stamping regime. CRA requires that all GST/HST invoices be retained in readable electronic or paper form and contain the supplier's BN, invoice date, description, consideration, and either the tax charged or a statement of zero-rating. Exporters must preserve electronic customs declarations, carrier records, and bank remittance evidence in kanban form for CRA audit. A voluntary Pan-Canadian B2B e-invoicing standard is under development but is not yet legally mandated.

The CRA may reopen a zero-rated otherwise assessable refund within four years (or longer for misrepresentation or neglect). If the export evidence is inadequate or the zero-rating claim is fraudulent, the CRA will reassess to collect the 5% GST/HST, charge daily interest, and apply penalties under ETA s. 280 (8% of tax assessed or reassessed) and s. 285.1 (25% of understated tax for careless, negligent, or wilful misrepresentation). Criminal evasion penalties under ETA s. 327 can reach 200% of the evaded tax plus imprisonment. A taxpayer who fails to repatriate export proceeds within 180 days in the absence of a demonstrably credible commercial reason may be deemed by the CRA to have issued the export invoice to a shell entity and will face export-zero-rating denial, subtractions from input tax credits, and possible anti-money-laundering referral.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current and capital account convertibility consistent with IMF Article VIII. There are no general exchange controls restricting conversion or repatriation of trade proceeds, capital injections, or branch profits. Dividends and capital remittances are subject to normal corporate and withholding tax rules under the Income Tax Act, not FX approval. Non-resident direct investment is generally unrestricted but may require review under the Investment Canada Act for share acquisitions above statutory thresholds.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion to CAD, and no prescribed timeline for holding export proceeds in foreign currency. Export receipts may be retained in any freely convertible currency, converted at market rates through authorized dealers, or used directly for trade settlement.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); no separate foreign exchange administration. The Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises federally regulated deposit-taking institutions for prudential conduct.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low-to-Medium feasibility. Canada does not operate a European-style EMI (Electronic Money Institution) licensing regime; payments and e-money-like activities are regulated at the federal level under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) and at provincial/securities levels for money services. Offshore digital EMIs generally cannot obtain direct clearing membership with Payments Canada and must rely on a Canadian Schedule I bank or an approved payment service provider for sponsor-based clearing. The EMI may operate as a digital wallet or FX intermediary but must be able to produce a clean Canadian AML/KYC record and legal opinion on its authority before any bank accepts it as a settlement counterparty.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound capital injection is routed through SWIFT MT103 or MT202 from the parent’s bank to the Canadian operating account, with the payment narrative clearly identifying the sender, ultimate parent, and reference to the board-approved capital injection. Canadian banks have no formal FX purpose code but require a documented economic purpose; banks will request the share subscription agreement or intercompany loan agreement, the Investment Canada Act notification (if applicable), and proof that the entry does not trigger FINTRAC large virtual currency/currency reporting thresholds. Settlement occurs across the Canadian Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) or via SWIFT correspondent banks; CAD/USD conversion may be executed at spot rates by the Canadian bank using an U.S. dollar clearing account. All supporting documentation must be pre-cleared by the bank’s onboarding team before the first wire is received.Trade settlements should use SWIFT MT103 for direct payment to suppliers, with invoices and purchase orders referenced in the narration. For documentary trade, the Canadian bank issues or advises letters of credit using SWIFT MT700 (LC issuance) and MT707 (amendment); negotiating banks present documents under UCP600/URDG rules. CAD payments are cleared via LVTS, while USD and other major currencies are cleared through the bank’s U.S. and overseas correspondent networks, including Fedwire/CHIPS for USD. For high-value batch disbursements, SWIFT MT101/MT102 may be used in addition to the underlying commercial contract evidence. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined general combined federal + Alberta corporate income tax rate (Federal Part I net rate 15% after the 10% provincial tax abatement and 13% general rate reduction; Alberta general corporate income tax rate 8%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder, absent treaty relief. No separate Alberta withholding tax applies.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed no later than six months after the end of the corporation's taxation year (e.g., a December 31 year-end is due June 30 the following calendar year).

BoxField Non-resident guidanceTax-evasion pitfall

For a non-resident-owned Canadian corporation, use a Canadian agent or service address; the corporation is treated as resident if incorporated in Canada or if its central management and control is exercised in Canada. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 - Form T106 (Information Return of Non-Arm's Length Transactions) is required for any reporting taxpayer with reportable non-arm's length cross-border transactions whose aggregate fair market value exceeds CAD 1,000,000 in a taxation year. CRA contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation expectations (Master File/Local File) attach to the same threshold. The Country-by-Country reporting threshold is CAD 750,000,000 in consolidated group revenue for multinational enterprise groups.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations, but the Income Tax Act and CRA doctrine require real economic substance for all cross-border arrangements. Relevant standards include the GAAR under s.245, the transfer pricing recharacterization power under s.247(2), the beneficial-ownership test for treaty relief, and the treaty-abuse / Principal Purpose Test under Canada's tax treaties and BEPS Action 6. An Alberta re-invoicing hub must demonstrate an actual office, employees, records, decision-making authority, bank accounts in its own name, and economic risk; otherwise CRA can disregard the hub, allocate profit to the true commercial actor, or apply penalty provisions.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Criminal Fraud

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.239(1): on summary conviction, a fine equal to 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to 2 years less a day; on indictment, the same fine and imprisonment of not less than 2 years and not more than 5 years. Civilly, the taxpayer is assessed the full tax, 50% gross negligence penalty under s.163(2), and awareness interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who sanction or participate in evasion face criminal liability, fines, imprisonment, restitution orders, and potential travel restrictions imposed by a court pending criminal proceedings. Courts may pierce the corporate veil when the corporation is an instrument of fraud, allowing collection against personal assets.
Shell Company / Sham Re-Invoicing Hub

Statutory sanction: CRA may treat the entity as a sham or conduit, reassess as if the transactions had been made by the entity with economic substance, reallocate profit under s.247(2), disallow treaty benefits under the general anti-avoidance rule / principal purpose test, and assess a 10% transfer-pricing penalty under s.247(3) for failure to make reasonable efforts to establish arm's length terms.

Director personal liability: Directors may be held personally liable for tax evasion if they knowingly implemented sham structures; civil liability can be extended through fraud or unlawful conduct. Individuals may face travel bans and extradition risk where criminal tax evasion charges are laid.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA can reassess arm's length income and apply the transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment where the taxpayer did not make reasonable efforts to establish transfer prices. Separate penalties apply for failure to file T106 information returns, and failure to maintain documentation may trigger the unreported tax plus interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly instruct or adopt unreasonable transfer-pricing positions without documentation can be held accountable as enablers of a false tax return; criminal charges and managerial penalties are possible under the Income Tax Act and Criminal Code.
Withholding Tax Failure on Profit Repatriation

Statutory sanction: The Canadian payer is assessed absolutely for the full amount of the tax required to be withheld, plus prescribed-rate interest compounded daily and penalties for failure to remit under s.227(9) (10% of the unremitted amount, or 20% on a repeat occurrence). The payer may also lose the deductibility of the underlying expense.

Director personal liability: Under Income Tax Act s.227.1, directors are jointly and severally liable for the corporation's unremitted withholding amounts if they were directors when the remittance was due and did not exercise the required standard of care. Courts may issue travel bans where criminal non-remittance is charged, and imprisonment is available upon conviction.
AML / Anti-Money Laundering Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, which can be substantial and escalate for repeated and systemic non-compliance; criminal laundering of proceeds under Criminal Code s.462.31 can result in imprisonment up to 10 years. Financial institution penalties for malicious or reckless facilitation can be even higher.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be prosecuted personally for knowingly engaging in or consenting to laundering, failing to implement AML procedures, or misrepresenting beneficial ownership. Travel bans and bail conditions may be imposed on charged individuals; civil restitution may follow for losses caused by the AML breach.
