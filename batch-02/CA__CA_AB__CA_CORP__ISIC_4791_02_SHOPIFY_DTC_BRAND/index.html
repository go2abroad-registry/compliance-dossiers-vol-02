Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

Alberta law imposes no minimum paid-up capital requirement. Shares may be issued without immediate payment, provided the consideration is lawful and described in corporate records. No escrow account is required for ordinary DTC-brand share subscriptions.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — Minister of National Revenue; online portal: CRA 'My Business Account' (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses.html); Alberta corporate income tax is administered by CRA under the federal-provincial Tax Collection Agreement requiring no separate Alberta corporate tax return; Alberta corporate registry is Service Alberta (Corporate Registry).

E-government filing portal

1) Incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or Alberta Business Corporations Act and obtain corporation number from Corporations Canada or Service Alberta. 2) Register on CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) or submit Form RC1 to obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN) with program accounts: RC (corporate income tax, mandatory T2 filing), RT (GST/HST registration), RP (payroll, if hiring employees), RM (CBSA import/export, only if clearing goods). 3) Non-resident corporations not carrying on business in Canada must register under the CRA Non-Resident GST/HST registration for digital-economy supplies and may be required to provide security and, under ETA s. 306, appoint a resident Canadian agent. 4) Receive BN/Acknowledgment letter (RT account confirmation) and commence charging 5% GST on Canadian taxable supplies. 5) Update Alberta corporate registry records and registered office information. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; Canadian Business Corporation is a separate taxpayer under Income Tax Act Part I (no pass-through / no S-corp analogue). Territorial_Scope: resident corporation taxed on worldwide income with foreign tax credit relief; Alberta provincial corporate tax is integrated with federal administration. Tax_Exempt_Zone: not applicable. Contextual combined headline corporate rates: federal 15% general / 9% small business + Alberta 8% general / 2% small business = 23% general / 11% small business for Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPC) within the CAD 500,000 annual small business limit.

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box classification regime. Available elections for this DTC entity include: (a) ITA s. 219 election by a non-resident corporation to file a branch return under Part I instead of Part XIV branch tax; (b) ITA s. 256(1) loss-transfer election between related Canadian taxable corporations; (c) ETA s. 156 election by closely related corporations to file a combined GST/HST return; (d) ETA s. 312 quick-method accounting election and simplified GST/HST accounting for e-commerce, if eligible; (e) ITA ss. 18.2–18.3 hybrid-mismatch and excessive interest and financing expenses limitation (EIFEL) rules, which limit deductions on cross-border hybrid arrangements and do not constitute elective mechanisms but must be assessed at registration. Non-resident corporations may also elect under ITA s. 216 for net-basis taxation of Canadian rental income, though this is generally not applicable to a pure DTC internet retail business.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (small-supplier threshold over the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; ETA s. 148(2)). For non-resident suppliers of digital economy or low-value (<CAD 150) goods under the 2021 e-commerce regime, registration becomes mandatory once Canadian consumer taxable sales exceed CAD 30,000 in any 12-month period; once registered, GST applies to all Canadian taxable supplies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods are zero-rated under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 1 when tangible personal property is exported from Canada pursuant to a contract of supply and is not taken into use in Canada before export, with physical export occurring as soon as reasonably possible after delivery. Services and digital goods supplied to non-residents are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, ss. 7 and 23 where the service is not in connection with Canadian real property and is not performed wholly for consumption in Canada. A Shopify DTC brand shipping inventory from Canadian fulfillment centers to foreign consumers may claim 0% GST/HST on such export sales, provided contemporaneous export evidence is obtained and preserved before filing; sales shipped directly from a third-country supplier to a foreign consumer are outside Canadian GST/HST scope.

  • Form B13A / Canadian Export Declaration filed electronically via CBSA Canadian Export Reporting System (CES) for shipments with declared value exceeding CAD 2,000, including CBSA Shipment and Control numbers
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / courier consignment note evidencing physical movement of goods out of Canada
  • Commercial sales invoice, packing list, and Shopify purchase order data identifying the foreign destination address, customer name, and arm's-length consideration
  • Proof of foreign-currency settlement and repatriation, including SWIFT MT103 or bank credit advice, PayPal/Stripe payout summaries, or an inward remittance certificate where local banking protocols require it
  • Outbound shipping manifest and delivery tracking status demonstrating final delivery to a foreign address (digital copy acceptable)
  • CBSA customs entry/exit records and broker-prepared export permits where applicable

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, CRA API-linked invoice stamping, or mandatory e-invoicing. GST/HST registrants must retain complete electronic books and records for six years (ETA s. 286; ITA s. 230). Export-related reporting must be filed electronically with the Canada Border Services Agency through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CES) for shipments exceeding CAD 2,000, with penalties up to CAD 25,000 per non-compliance occurrence. Voluntary Peppol-based e-invoicing interoperability is under advisory implementation but is not regulatory. Shopify Tax module and platform-generated invoices are acceptable for CRA purposes provided data is retained in a readable digital format.

CRA may reassess the exporter within the normal four-year limitation period under ETA s. 298 and ITA s. 152, extended to ten years where misrepresentation, false statements, or willful default is established. If export documentation is insufficient or the supply is deemed domestic, the export zero-rating is reversed, and the exporter must remit 5% GST plus prescribed daily interest, and input tax credits previously claimed are clawed back. Civil penalties include ETA ss. 280/281 false-statement penalties of 25% of net tax, ETA s. 285.1 penalties up to CAD 1,000 for record failures, and criminal prosecution under ETA s. 239 with fines up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to five years. Cross-border e-commerce export zero-rating is a targeted CRA audit priority, so the DTC brand must maintain a complete six-year documentary trail for every export claim.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for ordinary current-account and capital-account transactions. Canada generally permits non-resident ownership, foreign direct investment, dividend payments, interest payments, loan repayments, royalty payments, and repatriation of sale proceeds without prior foreign-exchange approval. Banks may require corporate authority, beneficial-ownership information, source-of-funds evidence, tax documentation, sanctions screening, and a credible payment purpose. Payments remain subject to Canadian tax withholding, transfer-pricing rules, thin-capitalization and interest-limitation rules, anti-money-laundering requirements, sanctions, reporting obligations, and contractual or lender restrictions. There is no general statutory profit-remittance approval process for an Alberta corporation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no general statutory requirement to surrender export proceeds to a Canadian bank, convert export receipts into Canadian dollars, or convert a prescribed percentage of export proceeds. Export revenue may generally be retained in foreign-currency accounts or converted at the corporation's discretion. Customs, tax, accounting, sanctions, anti-money-laundering, and statistical reporting obligations may still apply. International electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more, or multiple related transfers meeting the applicable threshold within the prescribed reporting period, may trigger reporting by the financial intermediary to FINTRAC; this is not an export-proceeds conversion requirement. Exporters should reconcile commercial invoices, customs records, payment receipts, and foreign-exchange contracts.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no general foreign-exchange administration imposing currency-conversion controls. Relevant financial-crime, sanctions, payment-reporting, and prudential authorities also include the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions, the Canada Revenue Agency, the Canada Border Services Agency, and Global Affairs Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium to High for payment collection and disbursement, but not a complete substitute for a Canadian operating bank. Shopify Payments, PayPal, Stripe, and other payment institutions may support merchant acquiring, settlement, and foreign-currency balances subject to eligibility, reserve, chargeback, prohibited-business, sanctions, and transaction-monitoring rules. An offshore electronic-money institution may not conduct unrestricted deposit-taking or banking in Canada merely because it serves Canadian customers remotely. Use of an offshore account does not eliminate Canadian corporate tax, books-and-records, transfer-pricing, sanctions, AML, payment-reporting, or beneficial-ownership obligations. Keep core payroll, tax, supplier, and liquidity functions with a regulated Canadian bank or appropriately licensed provider.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal plus 8% Alberta. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access an 11% combined small-business rate: 9% federal plus 2% Alberta, subject to the applicable business-limit rules and a CAD 500,000 Alberta federal-provincial business limit.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. Common treaty outcomes are 5% where the beneficial corporate owner satisfies the required ownership threshold and 15% in other qualifying cases. Under the Canada-United States treaty, the usual rates are 5% for a qualifying corporate parent meeting the ownership requirement and 15% otherwise.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is unnecessary. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires reasonable contemporaneous documentation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. Form T106 generally applies to reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents when the aggregate reportable transaction amount exceeds CAD 1,000,000 or the applicable reporting conditions are met. Country-by-country reporting and formal master-file or local-file documentation apply principally to qualifying multinational enterprise groups with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million, subject to Canadian implementation rules.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone regime titled Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is assessed through the arm's-length principle, the general anti-avoidance rule, beneficial-ownership rules, treaty anti-abuse provisions, thin-capitalization rules, foreign-affiliate rules, and common-law and statutory sham or avoidance doctrines. A Canadian corporation should maintain real Canadian commercial activity, appropriate decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational capability, records, contracts, inventory and fulfillment evidence, banking, and risk control proportionate to its business. A Canadian incorporation with nominee directors, no genuine Canadian activity, circular funds, or artificial related-party charges may be challenged.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, criminal tax evasion may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment of up to five years, or both. False statements or omissions caused by gross negligence may attract a penalty generally equal to 50% of the resulting understated tax or overstated credit, in addition to tax and interest.

Director personal liability: Directors may face criminal prosecution where personally involved, personal assessment for certain unremitted source deductions and GST/HST, civil penalties, and restitution. A routine tax debt does not automatically create a travel ban or corporate-veil piercing; imprisonment requires applicable criminal conduct and proof.
Failure to Remit Payroll Source Deductions

Statutory sanction: The corporation remains liable for the unremitted amount, arrears interest, and applicable failure-to-remit penalties. Criminal prosecution may apply in serious cases.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally under the Income Tax Act for unremitted source deductions if statutory due-diligence and limitation requirements are satisfied. Responsible officers may also face prosecution where conduct is deliberate.
GST/HST Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Assessment of unremitted GST/HST, interest, late-filing penalties, gross-negligence penalties, and potential prosecution. Knowingly obtaining or claiming false input tax credits may trigger substantial civil penalties and criminal sanctions.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for unremitted GST/HST under the Excise Tax Act, subject to statutory conditions and available due-diligence defenses. Personal exposure is distinct from ordinary corporate income-tax liability.
Transfer Pricing and Related-Party Pricing

Statutory sanction: The CRA may reassess income under section 247, deny or reduce deductions, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the adjustment in qualifying circumstances, assess withholding tax and interest, and apply the general anti-avoidance rule where appropriate.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable for a corporate transfer-pricing adjustment solely because the adjustment exists. Personal exposure may arise for knowing participation in fraud, false records, criminal evasion, or unremitted withholding amounts.
Part XIII Withholding Failure

Statutory sanction: The payer may be liable for the tax that should have been withheld, plus interest and failure-to-deduct penalties. The payer may also be liable for failure to remit and failure to file information returns. Treaty relief cannot generally be applied merely because the recipient is resident in a treaty jurisdiction.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal assessment for unremitted source amounts under statutory director-liability rules where the corporation failed to remit and the statutory conditions are met.
Shell Company and Beneficial Ownership Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Potential denial of treaty benefits, reassessment under the general anti-avoidance rule, tax, interest, penalties, corporate-law sanctions, and criminal liability for fraud, forgery, or tax evasion. Financial institutions may restrict or terminate accounts and file suspicious transaction reports.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face civil claims, regulatory enforcement, criminal prosecution, restitution, and personal tax assessments where they controlled or knowingly facilitated the conduct. Canada does not impose an automatic travel ban merely because a company lacks substance.
AML and Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties vary by violation and compliance history. Serious proceeds-of-crime or money-laundering offences may result in forfeiture, substantial fines, and imprisonment under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and Criminal Code.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be prosecuted where they authorize, direct, or knowingly facilitate the conduct. Corporate penalties do not eliminate individual criminal exposure, asset restraint, forfeiture, or restitution.
Shareholder Benefits and Personal Expense Diversion

Statutory sanction: The amount may be included in the shareholder's income or treated as a taxable benefit, with denied corporate deductions, shareholder-loan consequences, interest, and penalties. Deliberate concealment may constitute tax evasion.

Director personal liability: The shareholder or director may incur personal income tax, benefit tax, repayment obligations, interest, and penalties. Corporate limited liability does not protect a person from personal tax on benefits or from liability for fraudulent conduct.
Corporate Records and Director Governance Failure

Statutory sanction: Tax authorities may deny deductions or credits, impose record-keeping and information-return penalties, issue requirements for information, and reassess on the basis of available evidence. Corporate-law penalties may apply for inaccurate registers or annual filings.

Director personal liability: Directors may face statutory director liability, shareholder claims, creditor claims, and personal exposure for knowing misconduct or breach of fiduciary and statutory duties. Limited liability does not protect deliberate fraud or misuse of corporate assets.
Late Filing, Non-Filing, or Failure to File After Demand

Statutory sanction: Late-filing penalties, failure-to-file-after-demand penalties, arrears interest, installment interest, loss or delay of refunds, and potential prosecution may apply separately at the federal and Alberta levels.

Director personal liability: Directors generally do not become personally liable solely for a late corporate income-tax return, but may become liable for unremitted source deductions, GST/HST, or amounts connected with knowing participation in a criminal or fraudulent failure.
