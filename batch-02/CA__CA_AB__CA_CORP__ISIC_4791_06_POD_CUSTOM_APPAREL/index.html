Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is generally freely convertible for legitimate commercial and investment transactions. Canada has no general capital-account controls, mandatory repatriation rules, or restrictions requiring conversion of foreign-currency receipts into Canadian dollars. Current-account payments, capital injections, dividends, interest, royalties, and service fees may generally be remitted through Canadian financial institutions, subject to bank compliance, sanctions, anti-money-laundering controls, beneficial-owner verification, corporate authorization, solvency requirements, transfer-pricing rules, thin-capitalization rules, and applicable non-resident withholding tax. Payments involving sanctioned persons, sanctioned jurisdictions, unexplained third parties, high-risk payment intermediaries, or inconsistent transaction documentation may be delayed or rejected. Dividends and other distributions must be supported by applicable corporate-law approvals and must not render the corporation insolvent.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, mandatory conversion ratio, or general deadline requiring export receipts to be converted into Canadian dollars or deposited with a designated bank. Export proceeds may ordinarily remain in foreign currency or in foreign bank accounts, subject to accurate accounting, tax reporting, sanctions compliance, contractual obligations, and any applicable customs, trade-statistics, or payment-service reporting requirements. Foreign-currency balances should be reconciled and recorded using an appropriate accounting exchange-rate policy.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no general foreign-exchange administration imposing exchange controls; federal financial-intelligence, sanctions, banking-supervision, and tax authorities also affect cross-border treasury operations, including the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions, the Canada Border Services Agency, and the Canada Revenue Agency.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium for payment collection and foreign-currency transfers, but not a complete substitute for a Canadian operating bank account. A foreign electronic-money institution or payment platform may onboard a Canadian corporation only where its licensing perimeter, geographic availability, safeguarding model, correspondent-bank access, and terms permit Canadian commercial customers. It may not conduct deposit-taking or other regulated payment activities in Canada without the required authorization or reliance on a permitted structure. Stripe and PayPal may be used as payment processors subject to merchant underwriting, prohibited-business rules, reserve policies, chargeback controls, identity verification, tax information, and settlement-account requirements. Fintech balances should not be treated as insured Canadian bank deposits unless the applicable product expressly qualifies for deposit insurance.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route equity through a Canadian bank account by wire transfer from the disclosed shareholder or investor's account, preferably using the investor's legal name as remitter. Provide the bank with the subscription agreement, shareholder or board resolution, capitalization table, beneficial-owner information, source-of-funds evidence, and intended use of proceeds. Canada does not use a universal statutory foreign-exchange purpose-code system for ordinary corporate wires; the bank will instead require a clear payment narrative and may request an internal transaction-purpose classification. Identify the transfer as equity, shareholder loan, or other legally accurate funding and do not mix capital with customer receipts without accounting records. For shareholder loans, retain executed terms, interest and maturity provisions, transfer-pricing support, withholding-tax analysis, and repayment authority. Reconcile the received amount to the corporate ledger and retain SWIFT confirmations, bank statements, and foreign-exchange conversion records.Use domestic Canadian payment rails for CAD suppliers and bank wires or correspondent-bank transfers for foreign suppliers. Payments should identify the beneficiary, beneficiary bank, invoice number, goods or services description, currency, and commercial purpose. For higher-value or higher-risk transactions, use a commercial documentary letter of credit, standby letter of credit, documentary collection, or other bank-controlled trade-finance instrument. Match purchase orders, invoices, shipping or fulfillment evidence, customs records where applicable, and sanctions screening before release. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined general corporate rate (15% federal + 8% Alberta); 11% combined rate for qualifying CCPC active business income below the Canadian-controlled private corporation small business limit.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid by a resident Canadian corporation to a non-resident person; an applicable treaty may reduce the rate to 5% for a beneficial owner that is a company holding at least 10% of the voting shares, or to 15% in other portfolio cases.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed no later than six months after the corporation’s taxation year-end. Payment of the balance of federal and provincial tax is generally due within two or three months after year-end depending on the corporation’s CCPC status.

Deadline: · Frequency:

The AT1 must be filed within six months after the end of the corporation’s taxation year, matching the federal T2 deadline. Alberta corporate tax instalments and final balance are due in accordance with the Alberta Corporate Tax Act and the federal collection arrangements.

A non-resident corporation must mark its non-resident status, enter its Canadian business number and permanent establishment address in Alberta, and confirm that the return is being filed because it carries on a business in Canada. Late filing triggers a CRA penalty of 5% of the balance of tax due, plus 1% per full month of lateness up to 12 months. Information-return failures may attract daily penalties of CA$25 per day up to a maximum of CA$2,500. Interest accrues daily on unpaid federal tax at the prescribed rate.
Schedule 8 – Federal Tax Abatement Allocates taxable income earned in each province or territory of permanent establishment and computes the 10-percentage-point federal tax abatement. Complete this schedule only if the non-resident corporation has a permanent establishment in Alberta or another Canadian jurisdiction; without a Canadian permanent establishment, no abatement is available. Late filing triggers a CRA penalty of 5% of the balance of tax due, plus 1% per full month of lateness up to 12 months. AT1 – Identification Alberta enterprise number, federal business number, registered name, permanent establishment location, industry code, and fiscal period. A non-resident corporation with an Alberta permanent establishment must enter the Alberta permanent establishment address and the date business activity commenced in Alberta. Alberta imposes late-filing penalties equivalent to the federal structure: 5% of provincial tax payable plus 1% per full month of default up to 12 months, and prescribed interest on unpaid Alberta corporate tax. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Form T106 (Information Return of Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents) must be filed when the total fair market value of reportable non-arm's length cross-border transactions in a taxation year exceeds CA$1,000,000. Contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is expected for such transactions and must be available upon request. A Country-by-Country Report is required where the multinational group's consolidated revenue for the preceding fiscal year is at least CA$750,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a formal Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime similar to select offshore financial centres. Instead, the Income Tax Act general anti-avoidance rule (s.245) and common-law doctrines of sham and substance-over-form require that cross-border arrangements involving a print-on-demand retailer and related non-resident entities have a genuine business purpose, real economic substance, and arm's length compensation. A foreign shell with no employees, premises, or operational functions that purports to earn the profit of Canadian POD retailing will be recharacterized or disallowed.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: Gross-negligence penalty under subsection 163(2) of the Income Tax Act equal to 50% of the understated tax. Criminal tax evasion under subsection 239(1) may result in a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, and imprisonment of up to five years on indictment (or up to two years less a day on summary conviction). Comparable civil penalties apply under the Excise Tax Act for GST/HST evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly cause the corporation to evade tax may be assessed personally for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST under s.227.1. CRA collection powers extend to personal bank accounts, real property, and other assets; travel restrictions, asset freezes, and negotiated payment orders can be imposed, and a criminal conviction may lead to imprisonment.
Transfer Pricing / Profit Stripping Through a Shell Affiliate

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust the transaction to the arm's length amount under section 247(2) and disallow the excess deduction. The transfer-pricing penalty under subsection 247(3) is 10% of the net adjustment where reasonable efforts were not made. The general anti-avoidance rule may also apply to invalidate the entire scheme, with unpaid tax, interest, and penalties assessed from the date the transaction was completed.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorize sham transfer-pricing arrangements and sign fictitious documentation can be held jointly liable under joint and several assessment rules, subjected to asset seizure and garnishment, named in fraudulent conveyance actions, and in egregious cases barred from leaving Canada pending tax collection proceedings.
