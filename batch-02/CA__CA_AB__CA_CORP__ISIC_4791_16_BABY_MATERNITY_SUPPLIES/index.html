Competent corporate registry

Alberta Corporate Registry, Corporate Registry Division, Service Alberta (CORES online business registry)

Governing statute

Business Corporations Act (Alberta), RSA 2000, c B-9, as amended (ABCA) — incorporation by articles (ss. 9-10), certificate of incorporation (s. 14), name restrictions (s. 12), registered office within Alberta (s. 101), corporate records (s. 102), board of directors and officers (Part 6), and by-law adoption and shareholder confirmation (s. 123).

Formation / constitutional filings

Articles of Incorporation (ABCA Form 1) filed electronically via CORES; Certificate of Incorporation issued by the Alberta Registrar of Corporations. The standard online incorporation fee is C$150 (approx. US$110), plus a NUANS name search report fee of C$40 (approx. US$30) for a named company.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

Alberta imposes no statutory minimum paid-up capital for a private business corporation. Incorporators may subscribe common shares at nominal consideration (e.g., C$0.01 per share) without depositing funds into an escrow account; shares must be issued as fully paid and the share-consideration rules of the ABCA must be satisfied, but no escrow bank attestation, capital verification return, or paid-up deposit is required.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — federal tax administration; electronic services via My Business Account portal and Business Registration Online (BRO). Alberta corporate income tax is administered by CRA under the Alberta–Canada tax collection agreement; provincial incorporation, Corporate Access Number (CAN), and annual registry filings are handled by Alberta Corporate Registry (Service Alberta).

E-government filing portal

Step 1 – Incorporate: file Articles of Incorporation under the Canada Business Corporations Act (federal) or Alberta Business Corporations Act (provincial), obtain the Certificate of Incorporation and, for Alberta companies, the Corporate Access Number (CAN). Step 2 – Obtain Business Number (BN): apply to CRA online via Business Registration Online (BRO) or by filing Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts'; the 9-digit BN is the statutory corporate tax identifier and is used for all CRA program accounts. Step 3 – Activate program accounts: open the corporate income tax account (BN + RC) and GST/HST account (BN + RT) at incorporation; select the corporate fiscal year-end and GST/HST reporting period (monthly, quarterly, or annual). Registration for GST/HST is mandatory once worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD $30,000 in a calendar quarter or in a single transaction; voluntary registration is permitted where the corporation imports goods for commercial purposes so as to claim input tax credits. Step 4 – Non-resident registration nuances: if the corporation is non-resident, CRA requires completion of Form RC1 or RC431, appointment of a Canadian GST/HST representative, and may require security under subsection 266(1) of the Excise Tax Act; a non-resident corporation soliciting orders through an internet site is deemed to carry on business in Canada for GST/HST purposes. Step 5 – Alberta tax accounts: no Alberta provincial sales tax registration is required because Alberta levies no retail sales tax, and Alberta corporate income tax is reported on the federal T2 return administered by CRA.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a Canadian-resident Business Corporation is taxed at the entity level under Part I of the Income Tax Act on worldwide income; Canada has no check-the-box, S-corporation, or LLC pass-through classification for corporations. Alberta corporate income tax applies at the entity level at 8% (small-business limit) or 11% (general corporate rate) and is collected federally by CRA. Territorial scope is worldwide for resident corporations and Canadian-source only for non-resident corporations. No tax-exempt-zone regimes apply to Alberta e-commerce operations.

Available election forms

Canada does not administer a U.S.-style check-the-box election. Available statutory elections include: Income Tax Act s. 261 functional-currency election; s. 85(1) rollover election on transfer of property to a corporation; s. 83(2) capital-dividend account election; s. 111(7) loss-transfer election between related corporations; s. 216(1) election for non-resident rental income; s. 18.4–18.6 hybrid-mismatch rules and related elections implementing BEPS Action 2. Under the Excise Tax Act: s. 150 related-party election for exempt financial supplies, s. 273 charity election, and the Quick Method accounting election for qualifying small suppliers.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD $30,000 cumulative worldwide taxable supply consideration in a calendar quarter (or immediately upon a single taxable supply exceeding CAD $30,000) — the small-supplier threshold under subsection 148(1) of the Excise Tax Act. A non-resident e-commerce operator soliciting orders via an internet storefront is deemed to have nexus in Canada and must register once the threshold is met; voluntary registration is available for corporations importing goods for commercial purposes to claim input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available to GST/HST registrants under Part V of Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act: section 1 zero-rates tangible personal property (TPP) delivered by the supplier or on its behalf to a place outside Canada, provided the goods are exported as soon as practicable; section 7 zero-rates services performed for a non-resident; section 9 zero-rates services in respect of real property outside Canada. For e-commerce exports of baby and maternity supplies, the supplier must retain documentary proof that the goods physically left Canada, such as carrier waybills with foreign destinations, CBSA B13A export declarations, or foreign customs entry stamps. Registered exporters claim input tax credits on related inputs and obtain refunds net through the GST/HST return; domestic sales of taxable baby/maternity commodities within Alberta attract 5% GST.

  • CBSA B13A Export Declaration filed via the Canadian Automated Export Declaration / Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) for exports of CAD $2,000 or more or for controlled/regulated goods
  • Commercial invoice / Canada Customs Invoice
  • Ocean Bill of Lading or Air Waybill showing delivery destination outside Canada
  • Signed international sale contract or purchase order identifying the non-resident buyer
  • Freight forwarder or third-party logistics export certificate of shipment evidencing handover to an international carrier
  • Foreign customs import declaration or entry stamp from the destination country as proof of arrival
  • Bank credit or payment evidence such as SWIFT MT103, gateway settlement records from Stripe/PayPal, or corporate bank credit notes matching invoice amounts and foreign-currency settlement
  • Inward remittance reconciliation schedule linking each zero-rated export invoice to the corresponding foreign payment received within the 180-day repatriation window

Canada does not operate a mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, per-invoice digital stamping, or centralized government clearance API. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically via CRA GST/HST NETFILE (XML schema), My Business Account, or certified third-party software for virtually all registrants; corporate T2 returns are subject to mandatory electronic filing above CRA gross-revenue thresholds. Registrants must issue invoices containing supplier name, Business Number, date, description, and GST charged, and must retain records in CRA-accepted electronic formats under CRA Information Circular IC21-17R1 and GST/HST Memorandum 11-1. Canada is progressively adopting the PEPPOL e-invoicing standard through the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Initiative, but e-invoicing remains voluntary for B2B transactions and is not a precondition for input tax credits.

CRA audits export zero-rating under GST/HST Memorandum 17-3 and requires the full documentation bundle to be maintained for every zero-rated export. Missing or inconsistent evidence results in denial of zero-rating, reassessment at 5% GST on gross sale proceeds, reversal of claimed input tax credits and refunds, and arrears interest compounded daily at the CRA prescribed rate. Knowingly false or misleading statements attract penalties under s. 280 of the Excise Tax Act equal to 25% of the tax understated (minimum CAD $250 per false statement); fraudulent conduct triggers s. 285 evasion penalties of up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded plus imprisonment of up to 5 years on indictment (2 years on summary conviction). Under s. 298 of the Excise Tax Act, the CRA reassessment period is extended indefinitely where misrepresentation is attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default, enabling retrospective clawback of export refunds and input tax credits.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a floating exchange rate regime with unrestricted convertibility for current account transactions including trade settlements and profit remittances. Capital account transactions are generally permitted for Canadian corporations with mandatory reporting to the Bank of Canada under the Foreign Exchange Act; profit remittances to non-resident shareholders are freely convertible subject only to corporate tax filing and beneficial ownership verification.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Export proceeds constitute current account transactions and are not subject to mandatory surrender or conversion requirements. No statutory timeline or fixed ratios apply; funds may be retained offshore or surrendered voluntarily to the Bank of Canada for statistical purposes at market rates.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal plus 8% Alberta provincial tax. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) generally receives a combined small-business rate of approximately 11% on eligible active business income within the applicable federal and Alberta business limits.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax generally applies to taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident. Treaty rates may reduce the rate, commonly to 5% where the recipient company satisfies the ownership threshold and limitation requirements, and commonly to 15% for other qualifying corporate or individual recipients. The precise rate depends on the recipient's residence, beneficial ownership, ownership percentage, treaty entitlement, and anti-abuse rules.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 and AT1 returns are generally due six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Any balance of tax payable is generally due two months after year-end, or three months after year-end for certain eligible Canadian-controlled private corporations meeting the statutory conditions. Instalments are due monthly or quarterly under the applicable rules. A corporation must file even if no tax is payable.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate general-purpose Economic Substance Regulations regime comparable to certain offshore jurisdictions. Canadian tax analysis nevertheless requires genuine legal and commercial substance, accurate characterization of transactions, beneficial ownership, arm's-length pricing, and business-purpose support. The general anti-avoidance rule, sham doctrine, specific anti-avoidance provisions, transfer-pricing rules, principal-purpose treaty standards, and beneficial-ownership principles can deny artificial arrangements. Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Criminal tax evasion can result in a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years under the Income Tax Act. Civil penalties may include 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed amount for gross negligence, arrears interest, reassessment, and loss of tax attributes.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal prosecution where they authorized, acquiesced in, or participated in the conduct. The corporation remains primarily liable for corporate tax, but directors may face fines, imprisonment, civil recovery, and director-liability assessments for specified remittances. Canadian law does not impose an automatic general travel ban for corporate tax debt; border restrictions or bail conditions may arise in a criminal case.
Failure to Remit Payroll, GST/HST, or Part XIII Withholding

Statutory sanction: The CRA may assess the corporation for unremitted amounts, penalties, daily or prescribed-rate interest, and failure-to-remit penalties. GST/HST and source-deduction offences may also produce criminal fines and imprisonment. Directors can be assessed personally for eligible unremitted source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts, subject to statutory due-diligence defenses.

Director personal liability: Personal director assessments may apply despite separate corporate legal personality. Directors should maintain remittance controls, signing authority segregation, cash-flow monitoring, and documented due diligence. Personal bankruptcy does not automatically eliminate all trust-like remittance liabilities.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: The CRA may reassess under section 247, deny or reduce deductions, impute income, apply a 10% transfer-pricing penalty to the relevant adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, charge interest, and impose gross-negligence penalties. Contemporaneous documentation may be required and can be demanded by the CRA.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable for a corporate transfer-pricing reassessment solely because it exists. Personal exposure increases where directors participated in intentional evasion, fraudulent documentation, unlawful distributions, or breach of statutory remittance duties.
Treaty Abuse and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for under-withheld Part XIII tax, arrears interest, failure-to-withhold penalties, gross-negligence penalties, and information-reporting penalties. Treaty benefits may be denied under the applicable limitation-on-benefits, principal-purpose, beneficial-ownership, or general anti-avoidance rules.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal criminal exposure for knowingly false certifications or deliberate withholding failures. Civil liability may arise for directors where statutory director-assessment conditions are satisfied; a general automatic travel ban is not a standard civil tax remedy.
Shell Company, Sham Transactions, and Artificial Expense Structures

Statutory sanction: The CRA may deny deductions, recharacterize transactions, apply the general anti-avoidance rule, assess withholding tax, impose penalties and interest, and refer fraudulent conduct for prosecution. Corporate records, beneficial-ownership information, and electronic payment trails may be examined or obtained through audit and information requests.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is not automatically pierced, but directors may be personally exposed for fraud, knowing participation, unlawful distributions, statutory remittances, or other recognized grounds. Criminal conviction can result in imprisonment and fines; civil recovery and asset-preservation orders may also be available.
Customs, Import GST, Product Compliance, and Sales-Channel Misclassification

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include reassessed customs duties and import GST, penalties, interest, seizure, administrative monetary penalties, customs verification, and suspension or loss of import privileges. Product-safety violations may produce recalls, prohibition orders, fines, and prosecution under applicable Canadian consumer-product legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face prosecution for knowing or reckless contraventions. Personal liability is fact-dependent and is more likely where the individual directed false declarations, obstruction, or fraudulent import documentation.
AML, Sanctions, and Payment-Processor Misuse

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC reporting, record-keeping, registration, and compliance failures can lead to administrative monetary penalties and, for serious offences, criminal prosecution. Proceeds-of-crime and terrorist-financing offences can result in substantial fines, forfeiture, and imprisonment. Sanctions violations may attract penalties under Canadian sanctions legislation and can cause account closure or payment suspension.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct, authorize, facilitate, or knowingly benefit from illicit transactions may be prosecuted personally and may face imprisonment, forfeiture, fines, and asset restraints. A corporate structure does not shield knowing participants in money laundering or sanctions evasion.
Corporate Record, Beneficial Ownership, and Alberta Registry Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: The corporation may face registry penalties, loss of good standing, dissolution or administrative cancellation, inability to obtain certificates, and enforcement proceedings. Missing accounting and ownership records can aggravate tax penalties and audit outcomes.

Director personal liability: Directors can face statutory fines and civil claims for breach of corporate duties. Dissolution does not necessarily eliminate tax, remittance, fraud, or creditor liabilities. Travel bans are not a routine consequence of registry non-compliance but may arise in separate criminal proceedings.
Improper Dividends and Insolvent Distributions

Statutory sanction: Under the applicable federal or Alberta corporate statute, directors may be required to restore amounts improperly paid and may face statutory penalties. Tax consequences can include shareholder benefits, deemed dividends, withholding tax, shareholder-loan inclusions, and denial of deductions.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unlawful distributions or breach of statutory solvency duties, subject to available statutory defenses. The corporation's separate personality does not protect directors from direct statutory restitution obligations.
