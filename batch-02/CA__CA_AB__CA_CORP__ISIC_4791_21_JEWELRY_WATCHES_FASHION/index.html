Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — 'My Business Account' online portal (canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html). The CRA administers the federal GST/HST and Part I corporate income tax. The Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration (TRA) administers Alberta corporate income tax through the Corporate Tax & Admin portal. The Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) administers customs duties and import GST/HST at the border via the CARM (Assessment and Revenue Management) system.

E-government filing portal

Foreign entities must first obtain a Business Number (BN) from the CRA by completing Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number' or through the online 'Register a business' service, indicating non-resident status. If the entity is incorporated in Canada (federal or provincial), incorporation must be completed first through Corporations Canada (federal) or the Alberta Corporate Registry (NUANS name search), after which the CRA BN is established with program accounts: RT (GST/HST), RC (corporate income tax), RP (payroll), and RM (import/export). Non-resident suppliers may register under the simplified GST/HST non-resident registration using Form GST101, supported by non-resident address and permanent establishment details in Canada. Alberta corporate tax registration is automatically triggered when a corporation has a permanent establishment in Alberta. After registration, the entity receives a BN-format tax number (9 digits + 2-letter program identifier), used for returns, remittances, and customs.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation. A Canadian business corporation (Inc./Corp.) is a separate legal taxpayer resident in Canada and is subject to federal Part I corporate income tax and applicable provincial corporate tax (Alberta general corporate rate 8%; small business rate 2% on eligible CCPC income below the Alberta threshold). The corporation does not qualify as a pass-through entity; dividends are taxed separately in shareholders' hands under Part XIII withholding for non-residents. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. A Canadian business corporation (Inc./Corp.) is a separate legal taxpayer resident in Canada and is subject to federal Part I corporate income tax and applicable provincial corporate tax (Alberta general corporate rate 8%; small business rate 2% on eligible CCPC income below the Alberta threshold). The corporation does not qualify as a pass-through entity; dividends are taxed separately in shareholders' hands under Part XIII withholding for non-residents. Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) status may be claimed for the federal small business deduction and Alberta small business deduction, subject to the CRA's de facto control analysis and 25% non-resident shareholder limits.

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box regime. The CRA classifies foreign entities substantively for Canadian tax purposes, and hybrid mismatch deduction-denial rules apply under ITA ss.18.2-18.6 (BEPS Action 2) for payments made after 31 December 2022. Available elections include: ITA s.85(1) rollover election for tax-deferred transfers of property to a corporation; ITA s.216(4) election for non-residents to file Part I returns on net rental income; ETA s.156 election for closely related corporations (taxable supplies between them treated as made for nil consideration); ETA s.225 Quick Method election for small businesses; annual GST/HST filing election for qualifying registrants; and the small supplier threshold test election. Foreign LLCs and partnerships are individually assessed by the CRA to determine whether they are treated as opaque or transparent for Canadian tax purposes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

The statutory small-supplier threshold is C$30,000 in worldwide gross revenue (excluding GST/HST) from taxable supplies made in or exported from Canada, measured on the aggregate of the current and previous three calendar quarters. Once the threshold is exceeded, the supplier must register for GST/HST within 30 days. For non-resident online sellers and marketplace/fulfillment platform operators, the same C$30,000 threshold applies to worldwide taxable supplies, including sales fulfilled from Canadian warehouses; the platform may be deemed to make the supply for GST/HST purposes. Registration for GST/HST is mandatory in respect of tax charged at the border if the entity is a licensed importer of record, irrespective of the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of tangible personal property (goods), including jewelry, watches, and fashion accessories, sold to non-residents for delivery outside Canada are zero-rated under s.153 of the Excise Tax Act and Schedule VI, Part V, provided: (i) title and risk of loss pass to the non-resident purchaser; (ii) the goods are exported by the vendor or purchaser as soon as practicable and in the ordinary course of business; (iii) the vendor has documentary proof of export, including transport documentation showing physical departure from Canada; (iv) the supply is not part of a supply chain designed to return the goods to Canada; and (v) payment is received, though payment timing is not a precondition for zero-rating if all statutory conditions are satisfied. Services supplied to non-residents, such as offshore marketplace listing, logistics, or marketing services performed outside Canada, are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V where the recipient is a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST and the service benefit is realized outside Canada. Input tax credits (ITCs) may be claimed to recover GST/HST paid on inputs used in zero-rated export supplies; excess credits are refundable.

  • B13A / Canada Export Declaration filed through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) or CBSA authorized portal for any export of goods valued above C$2,000, or for controlled, regulated, or permitted goods (including precious metals, gemstones, fine jewelry and timepieces subject to trade controls)
  • Commercial invoice in English or French showing vendor, purchaser, complete description of goods, HS tariff classification (e.g., HS 71 for jewelry, HS 91 for watches), unit values, total invoice value, currency, and country of origin
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / courier waybill evidencing shipment to a destination outside Canada, with carrier name and destination port/city
  • Carrier or forwarder proof of export (e.g., CBSA cargo confirmation, terminal receipts, tracking scan showing international departure)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / foreign exchange settlement evidence (bank SWIFT/MT103, payment gateway settlement report from Stripe or PayPal, or FX conversion statement) demonstrating receipt of export proceeds in Canadian dollars or other convertible currency

No federal real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or mandatory e-invoicing clearance regime exists for GST/HST in Canada. The CRA permits electronic invoices and digital books and records provided their integrity is preserved (GST/HST Memorandum 17.1). Voluntary interoperable e-invoicing is supported through the Pan-Canadian Peppol trust framework; Quebec's mandatory B2B e-invoicing requirements are phased in from 2026-2027 but do not apply to Alberta. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically (GST/HST NETFILE) for registrants above the prescribed threshold, and large businesses generally must remit electronically. Import duties and import GST must be paid electronically through the CBSA CARM portal; no invoice stamping or government pre-approval of invoice data is required.

The CRA may reassess a zero-rated export supply within the normal reassessment period of 4 years from the date of the GST/HST return, and may reassess without time limit where misrepresentation, fraud, or neglect has occurred (ETA s.298). If the zero-rating is disallowed due to insufficient export documentation, the CRA will tax the supply at 5% GST (plus any provincial HST component where applicable), assess interest on the net tax owing, and apply penalties. False statements or omissions made knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence attract a penalty equal to the greater of C$2,000 and 25% of the net tax sought to be evaded (ETA s.285). Failure to file a required return attracts penalties under ETA s.280 (1% of the amount due plus 25%). Willful evasion or making false statements with intent to deceive is a criminal offence punishable, on indictment, by imprisonment up to 5 years and fines without limit (ETA s.327). The CRA and CBSA cross-reference export declarations, customs manifests, shipping data, and payment rails (Stripe/PayPal settlement records) under the e-commerce and jewellery sector compliance programs; export refunds may be clawed back with interest if documentation is fabricated, incomplete, or fails to demonstrate goods left Canada permanently.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible with IMF Article VIII status for both current-account and capital-account transactions. There are no exchange-control permits, no currency quotas, no differentiated exchange rates, no advance approval requirements, and no restrictions on the movement of capital. Profit remittance is legally unrestricted; however, dividends, interest, royalties, rents, and management fees paid to non-resident shareholders are subject to federal Part XIII withholding tax (statutory default 25%, treaty-reduced typically to 5%-15% on dividends and zero-to-10% on interest and royalties depending on treaty and arm's-length status). Before repatriation, corporate counsel must also manage thin-capitalization limits under Income Tax Act s.18(4)-(6), transfer-pricing rules under s.247, and anti-surplus-stripping rules under s.84(2), s.212.1, and s.212.1(1). Alberta imposes no separate foreign-exchange controls, no branch-profit tax overlay beyond the federal scheme, and no statutory minimum capital for incorporation; foreign-currency treasury operations may elect functional-currency reporting under s.261 of the Income Tax Act.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no surrender requirements, no mandatory conversion ratios, no repatriation timelines, and no external-account deposit obligations on export receipts. A Canadian exporter may retain USD, EUR, CHF, or other export proceeds in domestic or foreign bank accounts indefinitely without Bank of Canada approval. The applicable compliance duties are confined to: (i) Canadian-dollar reporting to the Canada Revenue Agency unless a s.261 functional-currency election is made; (ii) Form T1135 (Foreign Income Verification Statement) filing when the aggregate cost amount of specified foreign property, including foreign bank and brokerage accounts, exceeds C$100,000; (iii) FINTRAC electronic-funds-transfer reporting for every incoming or outgoing cross-border EFT of C$10,000 or more; and (iv) standard anti-money-laundering record keeping for wire origin and beneficiary documentation.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); exchange-rate and financial-sector policy coordination is exercised with the Department of Finance Canada; prudential regulation of federally chartered banks is delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); money-laundering and terrorist-financing supervision is delegated to FINTRAC; Canada has no standalone foreign-exchange administration or exchange-control ministry.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium overall. Wise (UK FCA-authorized EMI) and Revolut Business (Lithuania/ECB-authorized EMI) can provide CAD account details, multi-currency wallets, and fast cross-border FX for a Canadian e-commerce jewelry/watch operator, but they are not Canadian chartered banks, are not CDIC-insured deposit-taking institutions, and may not carry on a money-services business in Canada unless FINTRAC-registered. Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Root cause: Non-resident beneficial ownership, nominee directors, or multi-tier shareholding structures obscuring ultimate control

Defense evidence: Certificate of Incorporation (federal or Alberta); current Alberta corporate registry extract; corporate by-laws and unanimous shareholder agreement; register of directors and registered office; register of beneficial owners maintained under the Alberta Business Corporations Act; Hague-apostilled notarized corporate documents; passport and residential-address verification for each director and UBO; bank-reference letters from each foreign shareholder institution; and a corporate-org chart describing ultimate ownership.
E-commerce transaction velocity inconsistent with the stated scale of the jewelry/watch operation

Root cause: E-commerce transaction velocity inconsistent with the stated scale of the jewelry/watch operation

Defense evidence: Stripe and PayPal settlement statements; order-volume reports; refund and chargeback logs; supplier invoices for watch and jewelry inventory; logistics carrier shipment manifests with proof of delivery; marketplace and proprietary-website listings; and management-prepared or externally reviewed financial statements covering the first two operating quarters.
High-value movable goods (fine watches, diamonds, precious metals) presenting a recognized money-laundering typology

Root cause: High-value movable goods (fine watches, diamonds, precious metals) presenting a recognized money-laundering typology

Defense evidence: Independent valuation certificates from accredited gemological and horological authorities (GIA, IGI, COSC certification); hallmark registration records; manufacturer invoices evidencing cost basis; consignment or auction contracts; and a documented arm's-length pricing methodology for retail markups.
Cross-border wire flows involving high-risk, sanctioned, or tax-haven jurisdictions

Root cause: Cross-border wire flows involving high-risk, sanctioned, or tax-haven jurisdictions

Defense evidence: OFAC/EU/UN sanctioned-party screening results; export-control classification of any commodity inputs; end-user certificates; import and customs clearance records; supplier due-diligence questionnaires; and a counsel-prepared compliance memorandum explaining the commercial rationale for each high-risk corridor.
Inbound capital injections characterized as shareholder loans or equity without demonstrable economic substance

Root cause: Inbound capital injections characterized as shareholder loans or equity without demonstrable economic substance

Defense evidence: Notarized written loan or subscription agreement executed before funds arrival; arm's-length interest and repayment terms compliant with s.15(2) and Part XIII withholding requirements; bank statements from the originating institution; shareholder resolutions approving the funding; corporate minute-book entries; and a post-injection statement of account showing clear traceability of funds.
FATCA/CRS classification inconsistencies or failure to provide beneficial-ownership tax information

Root cause: FATCA/CRS classification inconsistencies or failure to provide beneficial-ownership tax information

Defense evidence: Form W-8BEN-E evidencing Canadian entity FATCA classification; CRS self-certification forms; confirmation that no U.S. significant owners or controlling persons exist; updated Alberta corporate registry filings; CRA program account registrations (corporate income tax, GST/HST, import-export account); and a tax-preparer letter confirming compliance with Canadian information-reporting obligations.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route capital contributions by SWIFT MT103 or MT202 from the shareholder's home-jurisdiction bank. For an equity subscription, remit after the board resolution and share issuance at par; use SWIFT field 70 remittance narrative EQUITY SUBSCRIPTION - ALBERTA BUSINESS CORPORATION and provide the Canadian bank with the executed subscription agreement, board minutes, and source-of-funds evidence. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate controlled non-arm's-length cross-border transactions with each non-resident in the corporation's taxation year triggers the contemporaneous documentation obligation (Local File / complete and accurate TP report). A Master File is not statutorily mandated but is recommended by CRA for large multinational enterprises to curtail penalties.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain discrete Economic Substance Regulations (ESR); however, CRA relies on the arm's length principle (s.247), the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245), judicial sham agency, and substance-over-form doctrine to disregard artificial Alberta intermediation. A jewellery-and-watch e-commerce trading company holding inventory must demonstrate genuine commercial functionality (logistics, pricing, risk assumption, personnel) to preserve treaty benefits and avoid permanent establishment reattribution.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Defective Filing

Statutory sanction: Under I.T.A. s.239(1): fine equal to 50%–200% of evaded federal tax and imprisonment up to 5 years; civil gross-negligence penalty 50% of the understated tax (s.163(2)); plus daily late-filing penalties and CRA prescribed recalculation interest.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable (s.227.1) for all unremitted withholdings (Part XIII/payroll) and unpaid provincial remittances; criminal proceedings may trigger travel bans and restitution orders; personal fraud convictions carry up to 5 years incarceration and post-conviction director disgorgement.
Shell Company & GAAR Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may invoke s.245 GAAR to deny the tax benefit, rescheduling the full reallocation to the beneficial owner, with gross negligence penalty 50% of resultant assessment; transfer-pricing penalty 10% of downward/upward adjustment net of TP threshold; interest accrues from the original year-end.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is pierced when the entity is a sham/façade; directors may be ordered to pay tax liabilities assessed under equitable trust (Crown priority), face contempt and travel restrictions for asset dissipation, and can be imprisoned for fraudulent concealment.
AML / Money Laundering (E-Commerce Payment Rail Risk)

Statutory sanction: PCMLTFA and Criminal Code: administrative penalty $2,000,000 per violation; criminal money-laundering offence punishable by up to 10 years imprisonment; repeat offenders face indefinite registration suspension of payment suppliers; Canadian Payments Council real-time rail can freeze funds.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally prosecuted on a 'willful blindness' basis, face penalties exceeding C$1 million, are barred from acting as directors of Alberta corporations for 5+ years, and can lose right to manage insurance/export bonds; travel bans may be ordered under Criminal Code restraint provisions.
