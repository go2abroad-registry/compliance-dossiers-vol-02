Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); Business Registration Online (BRO) at https://www.canada.ca/en/services/taxes/business-registration.html; CRA My Business Account portal at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses/business-account.html. Alberta does not operate a separate corporate income tax administration: CRA administers federal Part I tax and Alberta provincial corporate income tax (8%) under the Alberta Corporate Tax Act (RSA 2000, c A-15) and the Canada-Alberta Comprehensive Integrated Tax Coordination Agreement. Alberta Corporate Registry (Service Alberta) issues incorporation certificates; Alberta Treasury Board and Finance administers non-CRA taxes (e.g., fuel tax, tourism levy). Municipal business licensing in Calgary/Edmonton is separate from tax registration.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act (federal) or the Business Corporations Act (Alberta) via Service Alberta Corporate Registry (one-day filing, CAD 200-400 fee; no minimum capital requirement). Step 2: Apply to CRA for a single 9-digit Business Number (BN) using Business Registration Online or Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number', completing concurrently the GST/HST program account (RT), corporate income tax program account (RC), payroll account (RP) if employees are hired. Step 3: A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Alberta must register extra-provincially in Alberta (Form A) and designate a Canadian resident agent for service, providing the agent's address to CRA (CRA may require a non-resident tax account representative, Form RC139 or authorization via My Business Account). Step 4: Foreign-incorporated entities must obtain a BN for any GST/HST registration and income tax filing even if no Canadian incorporation is pursued, and must file a T2 return if a permanent establishment exists in Canada. Step 5: After incorporation, file the Alberta annual return and the CRA T2 corporate income tax return (Schedule 5 allocates Alberta income based on permanent establishment formula). The BN is the unique statutory tax identifier; no separate TIN/VAT-ID number exists.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation. A Canadian Business Corporation is a separate taxpayer from its shareholders under the Income Tax Act (RSC 1985, c 1 (5th Supp.)); there is no check-the-box pass-through. Federal general corporate rate is 15% (after the 13% general rate reduction); Alberta provincial rate is 8%, producing a combined general rate of 23%. A Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) is eligible for the federal small business deduction on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income (federal 11% effective plus Alberta small business rate 2%, combined 13%). Investment income is taxed at 28% federal (33.33% gross-up refundable mechanism). The hub (a freight/parcel consolidation business) will be a 'person' and 'registrant' for GST/HST. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. A Canadian Business Corporation is a separate taxpayer from its shareholders under the Income Tax Act (RSC 1985, c 1 (5th Supp.)); there is no check-the-box pass-through. Federal general corporate rate is 15% (after the 13% general rate reduction); Alberta provincial rate is 8%, producing a combined general rate of 23%. A Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) is eligible for the federal small business deduction on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income (federal 11% effective plus Alberta small business rate 2%, combined 13%). Investment income is taxed at 28% federal (33.33% gross-up refundable mechanism). The hub (a freight/parcel consolidation business) will be a 'person' and 'registrant' for GST/HST. Territorial scope: Canada taxes worldwide income of resident corporations; a non-resident corporation is taxable only on income from carrying on business in Canada or gains from dispositions of taxable Canadian property; the Canada-US Treaty tie-breaker applies for dual residents. Tax-exempt zones do not exist in Canada; export zero-rating is the only structural indirect tax relief.

Available election forms

Canada does not have a US-style check-the-box election; entity classification follows legal form, and a foreign entity with limited liability (e.g., a US LLC) is statutorily deemed a corporation under ITA s. 248(1). Available statutory elections relevant to this hub: (1) ETA s. 156 - election for closely related corporations to treat certain inter-corporate supplies as made for nil consideration, enabling GST/HST grouping; (2) ETA s. 22 - joint election to treat a non-resident registrant as the importer of an imported supply, facilitating ITC recovery; (3) ITA s. 216 - election for a non-resident to file a tax return for rental income from Canadian real property; (4) ITA s. 247(7.1) - transfer-pricing relieving election; (5) Income Tax Regulations ss. 1100(2.2) and 1103(2.1) - capital cost allowance elections; (6) ETA s. 211.1 and s. 252.1 - non-resident rebate and voluntary registration elections; (7) hybrid mismatch ordering rules (ITA ss. 12.7, 12.8, 18.4, 18.5, 20.3) implement BEPS Action 2 and are mandatory, not elective. Alberta has no distinct sub-federal election; CITCA allocation is formulaic.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000. A person must register for GST/HST if its taxable supplies made in Canada exceed CAD 30,000 in the current calendar quarter or in the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (ETA s. 148(1)(c), s. 240). The threshold applies to taxable supplies including zero-rated exports; a foreign parcel-consolidation hub selling freight/arranging services for cross-border movement will need to register once worldwide CAD revenue attributable to Canada crosses CAD 30,000. Voluntary registration is available before threshold, enabling ITC claims; a supply may be 'made in Canada' and thus in the threshold scope based on the place-of-supply rules in ETA ss. 142-143.2. Small suppliers who are not registered generally do not charge GST and cannot claim ITCs.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

The parcel consolidation hub may apply 0% GST/HST on: (1) a supply of a service of transporting goods to or from Canada (ETA Sch VI Pt V s. 7), including the domestic linehaul leg where the goods move as part of a continuous freight movement to an export destination; (2) a supply of a service of arranging transport of goods to or from Canada (ETA Sch VI Pt V s. 10), covering freight forwarding, booking, and carrier coordination; (3) incidental consolidation, sortation, and documentation services that are reasonably incidental to the zero-rated freight service (CRA policy GST/HST Memoranda 4.4.1 and 4.4.2; policy P-232 and P-239). Services performed wholly in Canada for a non-resident that are not in respect of real property in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada, passenger transportation in Canada, or a place of amusement/seminar in Canada may also be zero-rated under ETA Sch VI Pt V s. 7(a). Storage or warehousing physically performed in Canada is generally taxable unless the property is genuinely en route to export and the supply is a single continuous export movement; the hub should structure contracts to separate taxable Canadian storage services from zero-rated linehaul/arranging services. Documentary proof of export must be obtained contemporaneously, demonstrating that the goods were 'irrevocably destined' for export and that the supplier made the supply in the course of a commercial activity.

  • Canadian Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A) filed electronically through CBSA's Canadian International Trade Single Window / eManifest, showing the exporter of record and destination country
  • Clean bill of lading or air waybill (master/house) indicating the Alberta consolidator as shipper or forwarder and a consignee located outside Canada
  • Commercial invoice and parcel-level electronic manifest (shipping contract) proving sale or shipment on behalf of a non-resident customer
  • Freight payment evidence to the underlying linehaul carrier (truck/air/rail waybill and carrier settlement statement) demonstrating continuous movement from the hub to the port of export
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank/FinTech settlement report (e.g., Airwallex, SWIFT) showing receipt of foreign currency or CAD equivalent from the non-resident customer
  • Destination-country customs entry or arrival proof (e.g., US entry summary, UPS/FedEx delivery scans, EDIFACT CUSCAR, or GS1 logistics event) confirming the goods physically left Canada

Canada has no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance system, no government API invoice stamping, and no statutory e-invoicing clearance obligation for B2B GST/HST. Invoices may be issued on paper or electronically; the supplier must retain books and records sufficient to determine net tax (ETA s. 286, IC 05-1). CRA accepts EDI (ANSI X12, EDIFACT), XML, and PDF; the Pan-Canadian Peppol-based e-invoicing framework is being implemented on a voluntary basis and is expected to become standard for large registrants but is not yet a statutory mandate. GST/HST returns are required to be filed electronically (via GST/HST NETFILE or EDI/XML integration) on a monthly, quarterly, or annual cycle. The hub should implement automated invoice transmission from its parcel sortation system, maintain electronic manifests with HS codes, and reconcile to GST/HST return fields; real-time customs clearance applies only to import/export reporting through the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) eManifest/Canadian International Trade Single Window, not to fiscal invoice clearance.

CRA requires a 6-year retention of export documentation. Upon audit, CRA will test zero-rating with the full documentation bundle; if evidence is missing, CRA recharacterizes the supply as taxable and reassesses net tax, with interest from the original refund payment date. Penalties: (1) failure to collect/remit GST - ETA s. 280, up to CAD 500 plus 0.25% of unremitted amounts for up to 25%; (2) false statements or omissions - ETA s. 285.1, up to 25% of the assessed amount; (3) gross negligence, wilful default, or fraudulent zero-rating claim - ETA s. 285, penalty up to 50% of the tax evasion amount and interest under s. 280.1; (4) criminal evasion - ETA s. 327, fine up to CAD 250,000 for a corporation and imprisonment of up to 5 years for officers/directors; (5) parallel income tax reassessment under ITA s. 152(4) within 4 years for non-arm's length transactions, gross negligence penalty of 50% of understated tax under s. 163(2), director's liability under s. 227.1 for source deductions, and transfer-pricing adjustments under s. 247(2) if repatriation is delayed beyond 180 days or margins are below arm's length. CRA 'Export Zero' risk-screening flags missing B13A, bills of lading naming Canada as final destination, no foreign payment receipt, receipts from related non-residents, and HS code mismatches; a clawback also disallows previously claimed input tax credits for inputs used in the recharacterized taxable supply, and may revoke GST/HST registration where commercial activity is deemed to have ceased.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains unrestricted capital and current account convertibility under the Currency Act and the Exchange Control period ended under the Currency, Mint and Exchange Fund Act. No foreign exchange control approval is required for profit remittance, dividend distribution, or capital repatriation. However, Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (25% or treaty-reduced), Form T106 reporting for reportable transactions, and FINTRAC reporting for cross-border transfers above CAD 10,000 remain operational hurdles. CAD is freely convertible and clearing occurs through the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) and the Real-Time Rail (RTR).

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline, mandatory conversion ratio, or domestic escrow requirement on export receipts. Exporters may hold foreign currency proceeds freely in authorized bank accounts, use them in direct settlement, or convert through the spot market at negotiated commercial rates.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential oversight and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for anti-money laundering surveillance; no separate foreign exchange administration agency.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-High. Airwallex and similar non-bank financial technology providers are viable for payment gateway integration, sub-CAD 100,000 supplier batches, and cross-border settlement, provided the Alberta parcel consolidation entity registers as a money services business (MSB) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) and maintains a domestic settlement account. Offshore digital EMIs without a Canadian MSB registration cannot clear through Interac, ACSS, or LVTS and are restricted to SWIFT-compatible correspondent processing with higher compliance friction.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal general corporate income tax rate: 15%. Alberta general corporate income tax rate: 8%. Combined general rate: 23%. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) may generally access a combined 11% rate on up to CAD 500,000 of eligible active business income, subject to the federal business limit, Alberta rules, associated-corporation aggregation, and passive-income limitations.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. Common treaty rates include 5% where the beneficial corporate shareholder satisfies ownership and voting thresholds and 15% in other qualifying cases. The Canada-United States treaty commonly provides 5% for qualifying direct corporate ownership and 15% for other dividends. The Part XIII withholding tax return and remittance are generally due by the 15th day of the month following payment or credit.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 and Alberta corporate income tax return are generally due six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Corporate tax balances are generally payable two months after year-end. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally obtain a three-month balance-payment deadline if statutory conditions are satisfied, including the applicable taxable-income and associated-corporation limits. Instalments are generally due monthly; qualifying corporations may use quarterly instalments.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Generally due with the corporation's T2 return, six months after the fiscal year-end.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not prescribe a single CAD threshold that automatically triggers a separately named OECD-style Local File or Master File for every taxpayer. Under Income Tax Act section 247, contemporaneous documentation is required for non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents and must be prepared by the documentation due date. Form T106 reporting generally applies where the aggregate reportable non-arm's-length cross-border transactions with a non-resident exceed CAD 1,000,000, subject to the statutory rules. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated revenue of EUR 750 million or more in the preceding fiscal year. CRA may request detailed functional, economic, contractual, and benchmarking analyses below these thresholds.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone regime generally labelled Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is relevant through the arm's-length principle, transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse provisions, beneficial-ownership analysis, principal-purpose tests where applicable, thin-capitalization rules, interest limitation rules, foreign affiliate rules, and the distinction between a representative activity and a permanent establishment. A parcel-consolidation hub should maintain genuine Canadian personnel, premises, equipment, contracts, operational decision-making, risk control, books, banking, and commercially supportable pricing.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Conviction under the Income Tax Act may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years. Civil reassessment, gross-negligence or false-statement penalties, interest, and prosecution costs may apply separately.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal liability where they personally participate, authorize, acquiesce in, or direct the conduct. Corporate status does not protect an individual from personal criminal liability. There is no automatic tax-related travel ban, but courts may impose release or bail conditions and other orders in appropriate criminal proceedings.
Failure to Remit Payroll Withholdings or GST/HST

Statutory sanction: Assessments include the unremitted amount, prescribed interest, and statutory penalties. The CRA may impose director liability under Income Tax Act section 227.1 for source deductions and Excise Tax Act section 323 for GST/HST, subject to statutory due-diligence defences. Criminal prosecution may lead to fines and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST if statutory conditions are met. Liability can survive resignation where the statutory limitation and assessment requirements are satisfied.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under section 247, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the relevant adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, deny deductions, assess withholding tax, and charge interest. Recharacterization and general anti-avoidance rules may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable for a corporate transfer-pricing reassessment solely because it exists. Personal exposure may arise for intentional false statements, tax evasion, obstruction, knowing participation, or unpaid statutory trust amounts.
Failure to File T106, Foreign Affiliate, or Country-by-Country Information

Statutory sanction: Fixed daily penalties, transaction-based penalties, reassessment, denial of documentation protection, and interest may apply. Deliberate false information may trigger additional criminal and civil penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for knowing authorization or participation in false reporting. Ordinary filing errors generally create corporate rather than personal liability, subject to director-duty and tax-trust provisions.
Improper Treaty Relief and Withholding Failure

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed the shortfall, arrears interest, and applicable penalties. The payer may also be required to correct NR4 filings and support the withholding position during audit.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable where they knowingly direct non-compliance or fail to remit statutory trust amounts. A good-faith technical error normally does not create automatic personal liability.
Shell Company, Sham Arrangement, or Artificial Profit Diversion

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny deductions, recharacterize transactions, apply the general anti-avoidance rule, deny treaty benefits, assess withholding tax, and impose interest and penalties. Serious cases may be prosecuted as tax evasion or fraud.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly implement or approve sham arrangements may face civil and criminal exposure. Corporate limited liability does not shield fraud, deceit, fraudulent conveyances, or personal participation in unlawful conduct.
Canada Business Corporations Act and Corporate Record Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Corporate dissolution, compliance orders, administrative penalties, fines, or prosecution may result depending on the specific federal or Alberta corporate statute and violation.

Director personal liability: Directors may face statutory fines or prosecution for knowingly authorizing non-compliance. Corporate dissolution does not eliminate tax debts or liabilities arising from fraud or trust amounts.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Proceeds-of-Crime Violations

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties may reach up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation for a business in applicable cases. Criminal offences may result in substantial fines and imprisonment, including imprisonment of up to five years for certain proceeds-of-crime offences and up to ten years for certain laundering offences.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and employees may be prosecuted personally for knowing participation, direction, consent, or wilful blindness. Corporate indemnification cannot eliminate criminal responsibility.
Permanent Establishment and Unreported Canadian Activity

Statutory sanction: CRA may assess Canadian corporate income tax, branch tax where applicable, GST/HST, payroll obligations, interest, penalties, and withholding obligations. Treaty permanent-establishment protections depend on the specific treaty and factual activities.

Director personal liability: Corporate tax is generally assessed to the entity, but directors may incur personal liability for unremitted source deductions, GST/HST, knowingly false filings, or deliberate evasion.
