Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA / ARC) — federal tax administration portal at Canada.ca/taxes and Business Registration Online (BRO). Alberta corporate income tax is administered by the CRA on behalf of Alberta (Alberta Treasury Board and Finance) under the Alberta Corporate Tax Act; Alberta maintains no separate provincial sales tax administration.

E-government filing portal

1) Incorporate the master franchise entity under the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA) via Corporations Canada, or under the Business Corporations Act (Alberta) via the Alberta Corporate Registry, and obtain the certificate and Corporate Access Number (CAN). 2) File Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number and Program Accounts' through Business Registration Online (BRO) or by paper to the CRA to obtain the federal Business Number (BN) with program accounts: RC (corporate income tax), RT (GST/HST), RP (payroll), RM (import/export). 3) If the Chinese parent or master franchisor is a non-resident corporation, file Form RC1BR 'Request for a Business Number and Program Accounts - Non-Resident' and appoint a Canadian agent; a non-resident carrying on business in Canada must also file a Part I T2 branch return. 4) Register the RT program account within 30 days of ceasing to be a small supplier. 5) Enrol in the CRA My Business Account, register for GST/HST NETFILE, and authorize the Canadian tax agent through 'Represent a Client'. 6) For importation of ingredients, equipment or packaging, obtain an RM program account and a CBSA importer/exporter number under the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) portal.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a Canadian Business Corporation is a 'taxable Canadian corporation' subject to federal and Alberta Part I corporate income tax on worldwide income; dividends paid to non-resident shareholders are subject to Part XIII withholding tax at 25% statutory, reduced by the Canada-China Income Tax Agreement; the GST/HST registration is separate and does not alter entity classification; there is no pass-through or S-corporation analogue in Canadian law; the corporation files its own T2 corporate income tax return.

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists in Canada; entity classification is determined by the Income Tax Act and Excise Tax Act. Available elections for a bakery master franchise include: (a) ITA s. 85 rollover election to transfer existing franchise assets, inventory, goodwill and receivables into the corporation at tax-deferred amounts; (b) ETA s. 156 joint election for closely related corporations to treat intercompany supplies as nil-consideration; (c) ETA s. 211.1 branch registration election for a non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada; (d) Canada-China treaty-based permanent establishment allocation elections and the filing of Form T2207; (e) small supplier aggregation rule requiring the master franchise to aggregate taxable supplies with associated corporations for the CAD 30,000 GST/HST threshold; and (f) hybrid-mismatch documentation under ITA ss. 18.2/18.21 and 12 (effective for Canadian taxpayers from 1 July 2022) with administrative elections to adjust imported hybrid mismatches attributable to the Chinese franchise network.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — small supplier threshold. Registration is mandatory where total taxable supplies made in Canada (aggregated with associated corporations) exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or in the four preceding consecutive calendar quarters; voluntary registration is available for the central kitchen and retail chain to claim input tax credits on commercial leases, POS systems, equipment, freight and utility inputs; a non-resident franchisor may register under the simplified non-resident GST/HST registration procedure.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act to tangible personal property (Chinese pastries, frozen dough, mooncakes, dry bakery goods, packaging) sold by the GST/HST registrant to a non-resident customer where the goods are exported from Canada by the supplier, the customer or a common carrier acting on their behalf, generally within 30 days of the supply. Zero-rating also applies to exported services supplied to a non-resident franchisee (master franchise royalties, brand licensing, central kitchen consulting, menu development and marketing services) where the service is not performed in Canada and is not received by anyone in Canada, under ETA s. 7. No GST is charged on the qualifying export sale and the registrant may claim or retain input tax credits attributable to export inputs. Canada does not impose a codified 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline; the 180-day factor is used here as the dossier's conservative documentation benchmark, while CRA itself requires proof of export within a reasonable time (generally 30 days) and records retention for six years.

  • Canadian Export Declaration filed electronically to the CBSA through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) / CARM portal when the commercial value exceeds CAD 2,000, showing the Unique Carrier Reference (UCR), exporter's Business Number/RM account, consignee, destination country and CERS submission confirmation
  • Bill of Lading, Sea Waybill, Air Waybill or Canadian highway carrier manifest / border crossing report (ACI/eManifest) proving the goods physically left Canada
  • Commercial invoice or pro forma export invoice stating 'zero-rated export — no GST/HST charged', with the buyer's non-resident address
  • Franchise supply contract, purchase order or master franchise agreement evidencing the export sale to the non-resident buyer and the agreed Incoterms (FOB Canada / EXW / DAP)
  • Payment evidencing foreign settlement: SWIFT/wire confirmation from a non-resident bank, letter of credit, or inward remittance credit showing CAD or foreign-currency proceeds
  • Freight contract, forwarder certificate and insurance certificate showing the consignee's foreign destination and the export movement

Canada has no mandatory real-time B2B fiscal clearance, no API-based e-invoicing stamping mandate and no digital stamping regime as of 2025. CRA accepts paper or electronic invoices if they are complete, legible and retained in a retrievable electronic format; the store-level POS must preserve daily Z-reports, tax-inclusive pricing, sales journals and audit trails supporting the 5% GST collected. GST/HST returns must be remitted electronically via GST/HST NETFILE or approved CRA gateways, with payments by EFT/wire in Canadian dollars; CRA may request SAF-T-style digital extraction of the franchise's point-of-sale, chart of accounts and general ledger during an e-audit. A GST/HST invoice must show the supplier's name and Business Number, invoice date, description of supply, tax-exclusive amount, GST charged and consideration paid whenever the taxable supply exceeds CAD 30 or on request by a registrant purchaser.

CRA cross-references GST/HST returns, POS export sales and zero-rated invoices with CBSA border export records in CARM and e-manifest data; where proof of physical export or offshore service delivery is absent or inconsistent, CRA re-assesses the supply as taxable at 5% GST, disallows the input tax credits, denies any rebate paid and charges interest from the original due date. Civil penalties under ETA s. 285 reach 25% of the understated net tax for false statements or omissions attributable to neglect, rising to 50% for gross negligence; third-party civil penalties may apply to advisers who knowingly participate; fraudulent zero-rating schemes also carry Criminal Code exposure and potential suspension of the GST/HST program account. Clawback applies retrospectively where invoices are corrected or recharacterized as exports after the fact and no contemporaneous export documentation exists.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital accounts. No advance approvals, licenses, or restrictions apply to trade payments, dividend remittances, royalty payments, or proceeds from sale of Canadian assets. Part XIII of the Income Tax Act imposes a 25% non-resident withholding tax (treaty-reduced) on dividends/royalties, which is a tax matter rather than a convertibility engine. The Investment Canada Act only reviews acquisitions above CAD 1.3 billion enterprise value (for WTO-controlled investors) or where national security is triggered; capital contributions to an Alberta corporation from a non-resident shareholder are otherwise unrestricted.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no surrender or repatriation period for export proceeds. A bakery exporting product or receiving royalty/franchise income from abroad may keep such proceeds in any currency and in any foreign or domestic account. Only normal accounting and tax records must be retained for a CRA audit.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central bank for monetary policy and the ultimate operator of the Large Value Transfer System (Lynx). There is no dedicated foreign-exchange control authority because Canada abolished exchange controls in 1950; OSFI regulates banks and ensures solvency, while FINTRAC administers AML/CTF wire-reporting obligations.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Wise, Revolut Business) are not chartered banks in Alberta and cannot provide deposit-taking privileges or CDIC insurance. They may operate as registered Money Service Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC and are viable only as ancillary clearing/payout rails (e.g., employee expenses, low-value supplier payments, or cross-border float management). Primary corporate treasury banking must occur through federally chartered Schedule I or II banks to access Lynx, cheque clearing, and trade finance. Feasibility rating: Medium for auxiliary accounts; Low as a standalone treasury bank.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal non-resident corporate income tax headline of 23% (15% federal general corporate rate after abatement plus 8% Alberta general corporate rate) for active business income of a non-CCPC; Canadian-controlled private corporations may access small business deduction rates reducing the effective combined rate on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory Part XIII withholding tax on gross dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder. The rate is imposed on the actual amount of dividend paid unless reduced by an applicable tax treaty.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed no later than 6 months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Final tax balance due date is generally 2 months after year-end for non-CCPCs and 3 months after year-end for CCPCs; instalment payments are required throughout the year.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 in aggregate specified cross-border non-arm's-length transactions with a non-resident in a taxation year triggers mandatory contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation under ITA s.247(4). Schedule G / File documentation must include a Local File and, for MNE groups with consolidated revenue of at least CAD 1 billion, a Master File. The threshold applies separately for each non-resident non-arm's-length person.

Economic substance scope: Alberta and Canada do not maintain a separate standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime resembling offshore financial centres. However, the CRA and courts apply the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under ITA s.245 to disallow arrangements lacking real economic substance, the foreign accrual property income (FAPI) rules, and the treaty shopping abuse provisions. A bakery franchise structure without active conduct, substantial local employees, and real decision-making for the Canadian PE will not qualify for treaty benefits and may be recharacterized as a conduit arrangement.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Under ITA s.239(1), tax evasion is an indictable offense punishable by a fine of up to 200% of the tax evaded plus 100% to 200% civil gross negligence penalty, and imprisonment for a term not exceeding 5 years. Under Alberta's Cities and Municipalities Act, non-filing or false business licensing declarations may trigger administrative penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors who participate in or direct the evasion may be jointly and severally liable under ITA s.227.1 for unremitted source deductions, GST/HST, and third-party amounts. In addition to civil restitution, a court can impose a compliance order requiring the director to pay all outstanding amounts and, on conviction, a travel ban may be imposed as part of the criminal sentence, with potential immigration inadmissibility to Canada while payment is outstanding.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Non-Resident Part XIII Tax

Statutory sanction: The Canadian payer is assessed 25% of the gross amount as Part XIII tax plus daily compound interest from the date the amount should have been withheld; failure to withhold carries an independent penalty equal to 10% of the amount not withheld, and if negligence is proven, a 20% penalty under ITA s.227(8), plus interest and fines up to CAD 25,000 for a summary conviction.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are personally liable for the unremitted Part XIII withholding, payment over to the Receiver General, and related penalties; CRA may issue a personal director liability assessment and recover the amount by civil suit, garnishment of wages, or restriction on international travel if a departure order is obtained.
Shell Company with No Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA can deny treaty benefits and apply s.245(2) GAAR to reattribute income to the substantive Canadian or foreign operating corporation. Transfer pricing adjustments under s.247(2) can result in an upward taxable income adjustment plus a penalty of 5% of the net adjustment if the reported amount was not properly reviewed and documented. Interest is charged on all reassessed tax; gross negligence penalties can reach 50% of the amount avoided. In Alberta, a non-active corporation may also be administratively dissolved for non-filing under the Business Corporations Act, losing standing to enforce franchise agreements.

Director personal liability: Where the shell company is used for a fraudulent purpose, courts may pierce the corporate veil and hold shareholders or directors personally liable for unpaid corporate tax, franchise creditor claims, and penalties. Criminal prosecution for fraudulent manipulation of corporate structures can result in imprisonment for directors, civil restitution to the tax authority, and enforcement of foreign travel bans through Interpol if criminal charges are active.
Transfer Pricing Misstatements

Statutory sanction: A transfer pricing assessment under s.247(2) adjusts the taxable income to arm's length; the same amount is reduced for basis but cannot result in a refund. A penalty of 10% of the net adjustment applies where the total value of transactions exceeds CAD 1,000,000 and the taxpayer did not file the required T106 with Schedule G documentation, and the transfer price is significantly below or above arm's length. Failure to file T106 also carries the monthly penalty maximum CAD 2,500 per form.

Director personal liability: Directors who are aware of transfer pricing misstatements but do not ensure accurate T106 disclosure and reasonable documentation may be held liable as participants in the misrepresentation; CRA can require director signature on the T2 and impose trust-payment duties. Under s.247(4), the Canadian taxpayer remains primarily liable; however, for a non-resident corporation with a director resident in Canada, personal liability can be asserted for failure to act if the director also serves as a trustee or fiduciary of the corporate operations, and a court can issue an order freezing personal assets pending civil restitution.
