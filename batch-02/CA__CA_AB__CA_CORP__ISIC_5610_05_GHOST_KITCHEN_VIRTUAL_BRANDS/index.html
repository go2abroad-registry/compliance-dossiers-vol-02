Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada. Primary registration portal: CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/registering-your-business.html. Ongoing compliance and filed-return access are managed through My Business Account at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services/business-account.html; Alberta provincial corporate tax filings are submitted through the CRA portal for the combined T2/AT1 return.

E-government filing portal

The operator must first incorporate under the Alberta Business Corporations Act RSA 2000 c B-9 (or federally under the Canada Business Corporations Act and extra-provincially register in Alberta). The corporation then obtains a nine-digit CRA Business Number (BN) with program accounts in a single BRO application; the GST/HST program account carries the 'RT' prefix, and payroll (RC), corporate income tax (RC), or import/export (RM) accounts can be activated concurrently. Registration is mandatory within 30 days after worldwide taxable supplies exceed the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold (Excise Tax Act s. 148); a ghost-kitchen enterprise with delivery revenue should register immediately since actual turnover normally exceeds the threshold from the first quarter. CRA identity verification requires the Certificate of Incorporation, Alberta registered office information, director/officer details, and a resident agent if applicable; non-resident ownership is permitted, while Alberta director-residency requirements apply. The BN is issued within 1–5 business days, and the effective registration date can be backdated or prospective as elected on Form RC1.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. An Alberta business corporation is a separate taxpayer under subsection 2(2)(a) of the Income Tax Act and files a federal T2 return together with the Alberta Corporate Tax and Economic Diversification return. Territorial scope: resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income; a corporation incorporated in Canada is deemed resident under subsection 250(4), so a Canadian parent of a ghost-kitchen brand cannot be treated as a tax-transparent entity. For a foreign-owned non-CCPC, the combined headline corporate rate is federal general 15% plus Alberta general 8% = 23% on active business income (no small-business deduction is available because CCPC status requires Canadian-resident control). For an Alberta-controlled CCPC, the combined small-business rate is federal 9% plus Alberta 8% = 17% on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income, subject to the Alberta small-business limit. Alberta has no provincial sales tax; only the 5% federal GST applies to taxable supplies in Alberta.

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a US-style check-the-box classification election. Available statutory elections relevant to this structure include: (1) ITA s. 85(1) rollover election (Form T2057) to transfer capital assets, goodwill, or IP into the corporation at agreed tax values; (2) ITA s. 89(4) election to treat a capital dividend account balance as a tax-free capital dividend (Form T2054); (3) ITA s. 93(1)(a) election on foreign affiliate surplus distributions; (4) Excise Tax Act s. 156 election between closely related corporations to deem intercompany taxable supplies to be for nil consideration; (5) Excise Tax Act s. 167 election for the supply of a going concern to be GST-free; (6) ETA s. 191 elections available to financial institutions only, not relevant here; and (7) hybrid-mismatch adjustments under ITA ss. 18.6–18.7, where a taxpayer may elect the manner in which a domestic deduction is reduced or matched with an inclusion in respect of an imported hybrid mismatch arrangement.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000. The small-supplier registration threshold under ETA s. 148 is CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies (excluding financial services and capital property) measured in the current calendar quarter or in the four preceding consecutive calendar quarters. A ghost-kitchen operator that simultaneously sells through UberEats and DoorDash must aggregate revenue across all sales channels, including marketplace commissions and delivery fees, in applying the threshold. Once exceeded, the corporation must register within 30 days and file from the earlier effective date normally the date on which revenue exceeded the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under Schedule VI Part V of the Excise Tax Act to (a) goods supplied to a non-resident for export, where the supplier exports the goods or delivers them to a common carrier for direct export (s. 5); (b) services supplied to a non-resident, unless the service relates to real property in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance (s. 7); and (c) intangible personal property such as royalties, trademarks, or licensing rights supplied to a non-resident (s. 10). For this ghost-kitchen/virtual-brand model, prepared-food sales delivered to Canadian consumers are never zero-rated merely because the delivery platform is US-owned. Export zero-rating may be available only for genuine flows, for example: a US master-franchisee paying royalty/licensing fees for the virtual brand, recipes, and management software (zero-rated under s. 10); or food produced in Canada and exported to a US buyer where title and possession pass outside Canada. Services performed partly in Canada for a non-resident that is not registered for GST can be zero-rated where the supply is not connected to Canadian real property or Canadian tangible property, and the operator obtains written proof of the non-resident status and the foreign destination.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A) filed through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS), or digital proof of export where a mandatory declaration applies to the food category
  • Commercial invoice with export certification terms (Incoterms, FOB/CIF values, CAD equivalent, consignee identification)
  • Through Bill of Lading, Air Waybill, or over-the-road waybill evidencing movement out of Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank FX settlement statement confirming receipt of USD/CAD export proceeds into the Canadian corporate bank account
  • US customs entry documentation (CBP Form 7501) or ISF 10+2 filing where exports cross via US land border
  • CRA Form GST171C 'Proof of Export' or an equivalent CRA-accepted third-party certificate (customs broker statement, port authority certificate, independent carrier attestation)
  • Proof of delivery dated within 60 days of the original supply confirming export completion
  • CUSMA certificate of origin and any CFIA export permit applicable to food products destined for foreign markets

Canada has no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, no digital stamping regime, and no government-side invoice validation. The CRA operates a voluntary Pan-Canadian e-invoicing initiative based on the PEPPOL network (pilot launched 2023; GSF 1.0 profile); participation is optional. A GST registrant must issue tax invoices satisfying ETA s. 169(2): supplier name and BN/RT account, recipient name, invoice date, description, taxable amount, and GST amount at each rate; electronic invoices in PDF/XML are accepted without prior CRA authorization. For marketplace sales, the platform is not a settlement authority for tax, but CRA's marketplace operator rules (ETA s. 233.01, effective 1 July 2021) deem certain platform operators to be the supplier of goods in relation to non-resident vendors. For a domestic Alberta ghost kitchen selling through UberEats and DoorDash, the operator remains the supplier of the prepared food and must collect 5% GST on the full selling price, while the platform's commission or delivery fee is a separate taxable supply to the operator. GST returns are filed electronically; reporting frequency is monthly where annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 6,000,000, quarterly where annual supplies exceed CAD 500,000, and annual otherwise. Net tax is remitted via CRA My Payment; input tax credits and any zero-rating refunds are claimed on the same return.

The CRA treats zero-rated export claims as high-risk, especially for food products and IP royalties. If documentation is not retained for the statutory period (two years for proof of export and six years for books and records), the CRA reverses the zero-rating, reassesses GST at 5%, disallows associated input tax credits, and charges prescribed interest compounded daily from the original due date. Civil penalties include: a 50% gross-negligence penalty under ETA s. 285; a 25% repeated-failure penalty under ETA s. 293; and false-statement offenses under ETA s. 327(1) with fines up to CAD 25,000 and/or 12 months imprisonment on summary conviction, with higher indictable penalties for deliberate tax evasion. Income Tax Act exposure also applies under s. 239 for fraudulent export claims. On the FX repatriation side, failure to bring export proceeds back to Canada within 180 days can create an 'overdue amount' under amended ITA s. 17(1), triggering deemed interest income at prescribed rates; if the receivable is owed by a non-resident shareholder or a related party, the 180-day outstanding balance may also be recharacterized as a shareholder loan or benefit under ITA s. 15(2) and exposed to 25% Part XIII withholding tax or Part I taxable-benefit clawbacks. The operator should align export invoicing, freight documents, customs entries, and FX receipts in a single auditable file to withstand CRA international and GST audit programs.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible. Both current and capital account transactions are unrestricted; there is no inward or outward remittance approval regime, no conversion quota, and no repatriation licensing requirement. The only friction for profit remittance is the non-resident withholding tax on dividends under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (statutory 25%, treaty-reduced depending on residency), which is a tax compliance mechanism rather than an FX control. Capital accounts are fully open for non-resident direct investment, subject to Investment Canada Act net benefit review for large acquisitions and the national security review regime. For a small ghost kitchen operating corporation, dividend and shareholder-loan repayments to a non-resident parent are permitted without Bank of Canada approval, provided the solvency tests under the Business Corporations Act (Alberta) and the withholding tax obligations are satisfied.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender ratio, no holding period, and no mandatory conversion timeline for export proceeds or foreign currency receipts, including marketplace payouts from UberEats and DoorDash. A Canadian operator may maintain foreign currency accounts at any Schedule I chartered bank and retain USD proceeds indefinitely. The operative compliance requirements are: (i) FINTRAC record-keeping and reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) for electronic funds transfers of C$10,000 or more, including wire originator and beneficiary records; (ii) Statistics Canada reporting for capital transactions; and (iii) CRA functional currency reporting if the corporation elects a US-dollar functional currency.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central monetary authority; bank prudential supervision is administered by OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions); FINTRAC administers Canadian AML/CFT transaction reporting; Statistics Canada collects cross-border direct investment data. Canada abolished general exchange controls in 1951 and no government body retains discretionary FX approval powers.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM-HIGH, but only as a secondary collection rail for delivery-platform payouts; never as the primary treasury account. Legal constraints: (a) foreign EMIs are not licensed deposit-taking institutions in Canada and cannot accept deposits; they operate as registration-only money service businesses through a partner Schedule I bank; (b) funds held with an EMI are not CDIC-insured; (c) under the Retail Payment Activities Act, any payment service provider serving Canadian users must be registered with the Bank of Canada, so the EMI's registration status must be verified in advance; (d) CRA payroll, source-deduction, and GST/HST remittances should be funded from a Schedule I bank account to preserve the prescribed financial institution audit trail and CRA payment integrity; (e) EMI multi-currency rails (e.g., Wise Platform, Airwallex, Payoneer) are viable for receiving USD-denominated UberEats/DoorDash payouts and for low-value international supplier payments, but the treasury policy must require a daily zero-balance sweeper into the primary Schedule I operating account.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 50 million in gross revenue or CAD 250 million in total assets (for the group) triggers mandatory Local File and Master File preparation under CRA's TP documentation requirements; smaller entities should maintain contemporaneous documentation to avoid penalties.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a separate Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) like UAE, but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and treaty abuse provisions (e.g., OECD BEPS Action 6) require substantive business presence and economic activity to claim treaty benefits. A ghost kitchen with only a mailbox and no operational staff in Canada may be deemed a shell company and denied treaty relief.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under Income Tax Act s.239: fine up to 200% of tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years; civil penalties of 50% of tax evaded plus interest.

Director personal liability: Directors jointly and severally liable for unpaid corporate tax under s.227.1 if they failed to withhold or remit; travel bans and asset freezes possible under CRA's collection powers.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits under GAAR; reassessment of tax with penalties up to 25% of tax avoided; potential criminal charges for fraudulent misrepresentation.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for penalties if they knowingly participated in the sham; CRA can pierce corporate veil to recover taxes from personal assets.
AML/CFT Violations

Statutory sanction: Under Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: fines up to CAD 2 million for corporations; criminal liability for directors and officers.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally fined up to CAD 500,000 and imprisoned up to 5 years for knowingly failing to comply with AML obligations.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust prices and impose penalties under s.247(3): 10% of the net adjustment (if gross negligence) plus interest; double taxation risk.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for penalties if they signed false or misleading TP documentation; criminal liability for tax evasion if intentional.
