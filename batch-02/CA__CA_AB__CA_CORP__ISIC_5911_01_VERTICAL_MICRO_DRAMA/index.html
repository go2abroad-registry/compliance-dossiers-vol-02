Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canadian corporations are generally taxable entities and do not have a United States-style check-the-box election to become disregarded entities or partnerships. A corporation may qualify as a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC), public corporation, or other corporate category based on statutory facts rather than a general elective classification. Available elections are transaction-specific and may include subsection 85(1) or 85(2) rollover elections, section 86 share-reorganization elections, capital-cost allowance elections, GST/HST reporting-period and accounting-method elections, and treaty-based elections or relief claims. Canadian anti-hybrid mismatch rules can deny deductions or impose income inclusions where specified hybrid arrangements produce deduction or inclusion mismatches; they do not provide a general elective hybrid entity classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding zero-rated supplies, measured under the GST/HST small-supplier rules. Registration is generally required when the threshold is exceeded in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters. Voluntary registration is permitted before the threshold is reached, subject to the applicable conditions and ongoing compliance obligations. Separate rules may apply to non-resident suppliers of digital products or services to Canadian consumers.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada are generally zero-rated where the statutory GST export conditions are satisfied, including delivery or making the goods available outside Canada and retention of evidence establishing export. Services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated only where a specific Excise Tax Act provision applies; non-resident status alone is insufficient. Relevant rules may cover services performed wholly outside Canada, certain advisory or professional services supplied to non-residents, qualifying services in respect of goods situated outside Canada, and other prescribed categories. Electronically supplied streaming, application, and digital-content services must be analyzed under the applicable place-of-supply and digital-economy rules; customer location, billing address, payment information, IP or device indicators, contractual rights, and the nature of the supply should be documented. Input tax credits or GST refunds require valid supporting documentation, commercial-use nexus, and compliance with the applicable limitation and documentary rules.

  • Canadian export declaration or applicable electronic export-reporting record, including the relevant Canada Border Services Agency or Statistics Canada filing evidence where required
  • Commercial invoice and purchase or distribution agreement identifying the recipient, goods or rights supplied, consideration, and destination
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, courier record, freight forwarder record, or equivalent transport document
  • Proof of delivery or proof that the goods were made available outside Canada
  • Customs import record, destination-country import clearance, or other independent evidence of entry outside Canada where available
  • For services and digital supplies: contract, customer non-resident evidence, customer location and billing records, platform logs, delivery records, usage records, and evidence supporting the applicable zero-rating provision
  • GST/HST invoices, general-ledger records, bank statements, payment records, and input-tax-credit documentation
  • Evidence of refunds, chargebacks, platform settlements, and intermediary or marketplace reporting where Apple, Google, Stripe, or another payment intermediary is involved

Canada and Alberta do not generally require real-time electronic invoice clearance, government-issued digital invoice stamping, or universal tax-invoice API transmission for ordinary business-to-business GST transactions. Registered suppliers must issue compliant GST/HST invoices or receipts containing prescribed information, including supplier identity and GST/HST registration number where applicable, consideration, tax amount or tax calculation, tax rate, invoice date, and sufficient transaction detail. Electronic invoices and electronic records are generally acceptable if they remain accessible, reliable, readable, and capable of supporting audit verification. Electronic filing through CRA online services is required or strongly encouraged for specified registrants and returns, but it is not a real-time invoice-clearance regime.

Canada has no general statutory requirement that export proceeds be repatriated within 180 days. A Canadian exporter may generally retain or receive proceeds through foreign accounts, subject to ordinary income-tax, transfer-pricing, foreign-reporting, anti-money-laundering, sanctions, and exchange-control rules; Canada generally has no comprehensive foreign-exchange control regime imposing a universal repatriation deadline. CRA may deny zero-rating, assess unremitted GST, deny input tax credits, assess interest, and apply gross-negligence or other statutory penalties where export evidence is inadequate or the zero-rating claim is incorrect. False statements, wilful evasion, fraud, or deliberately altered records may result in substantial civil penalties, prosecution, fines, and imprisonment under the Excise Tax Act and Income Tax Act. Where a refund or input tax credit was improperly obtained, the amount may be reassessed and recovered with applicable interest and penalties. Records should generally be retained for at least six years from the end of the relevant reporting period, subject to longer periods for unresolved objections, appeals, or specified circumstances.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility on both current account and capital account. The Canadian dollar (CAD) floats freely and is internationally deliverable; there are no restrictions on profit remittance, dividend distribution, or capital repatriation by non-resident shareholders. The only legal frictions are fiscal and reporting in nature: Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid to non-resident investors is 25% statutory, reducible to 5–15% under applicable income tax treaties, and FINTRAC cross-border reporting applies to currency transactions and wire transfers above CAD 10,000. For a vertical micro-drama distributor monetizing through Apple IAP and Google Play Billing, foreign subscription revenue settled by app-store aggregators is freely credited to CAD accounts and convertible into USD, EUR, KRW, or any other market currency without central-bank approval.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. Canada imposes no statutory obligation to surrender export proceeds, no mandatory timeline for repatriation, and no required conversion ratio for export receipts of digital content or any other goods or services. The Alberta corporation may lawfully maintain foreign-currency accounts (USD, EUR, CNY, KRW) at Canadian banks, or with approved foreign dealers, and may retain export earnings offshore. Only FINTRAC wire-reporting and general corporate tax filing obligations attach to such proceeds; no foreign-exchange surrender certificate or central-bank approval is required.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), acting under the Bank of Canada Act as the central monetary authority; Canada maintains no statutory exchange-control regime, and foreign-exchange policy is administered by the Department of Finance Canada with prudential supervision of authorized banks by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); cross-border currency and monetary-instrument reporting is enforced by FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility: Medium (conditional). Canada does not recognize the European EMI (Electronic Money Institution) licence class, and an offshore EMI holding funds for an Alberta company cannot lawfully operate as a deposit-taking bank under the Bank Act. Foreign digital providers such as Wise, Payoneer, and Airwallex are permissible only as low-value payout and disbursement conduits behind a primary OSFI-regulated Schedule I bank account; funds must not be permanently held at the EMI. New registration obligations under the Retail Payment Activities Act (administered by the Bank of Canada, in force 2024–2025) apply to payment service providers touching Canadian retail users. Therefore, the primary treasury wallet must remain at a Canadian regulated bank, with EMIs restricted to cross-border creator/production-studio disbursements.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant capital injections are routed by SWIFT MT103 (or ISO 20022 pain.001) from the investor's account directly to the Alberta corporation's Canadian bank account. The wire must clearly identify the beneficiary and reference the purpose code for equity investment: under the OECD BPM6/IMF classification, the payment narrative should state 'Direct Investment – Equity Capital Injection into [Company Name]', for tracking under Statistics Canada's direct-investment survey processes. For inbound remittances exceeding CAD 10,000, the bank will capture standard FINTRAC information from the remitter; injections above CAD 75,000 may trigger Statistics Canada reporting, and the relationship manager should be notified in advance to code the entry as foreign direct investment in Canada rather than as trade revenue. Source-of-funds verification requires recent tax returns or financial statements of the investor, a board resolution approving the share issuance, and the executed subscription agreement; funds must move from the investor's own bank account and not from an unrelated third-party wallet.Outbound trade and production settlements are executed through: (a) SWIFT MT103 bank transfers for creator royalties, studio production payments, and equipment purchases, cleared in USD via CHIPS or Fedwire, in EUR via TARGET2, in CAD via Lynx (Payments Canada's high-value system), and in KRW/HKD via local clearing systems; (b) irrevocable commercial documentary letters of credit (UCP 600) issued by a Canadian Schedule I bank for larger equipment or licensing acquisitions; and (c) multi-currency or foreign-currency accounts (USD, EUR, KRW) to concentrate receipts from Apple and Google and disburse to overseas producers without double FX conversion. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility: Medium (conditional). Canada does not recognize the European EMI (Electronic Money Institution) licence class, and an offshore EMI holding funds for an Alberta company cannot lawfully operate as a deposit-taking bank under the Bank Act. Foreign digital providers such as Wise, Payoneer, and Airwallex are permissible only as low-value payout and disbursement conduits behind a primary OSFI-regulated Schedule I bank account; funds must not be permanently held at the EMI. New registration obligations under the Retail Payment Activities Act (administered by the Bank of Canada, in force 2024–2025) apply to payment service providers touching Canadian retail users. Therefore, the primary treasury wallet must remain at a Canadian regulated bank, with EMIs restricted to cross-border creator/production-studio disbursements.
