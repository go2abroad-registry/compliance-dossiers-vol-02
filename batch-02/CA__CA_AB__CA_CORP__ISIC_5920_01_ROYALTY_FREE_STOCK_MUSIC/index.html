Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; operational portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html); provincial counterpart in Alberta: Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration (TRA) for Alberta Corporate Income Tax, and Alberta Corporate Registry for extra-provincial registration of a corporation; CRA is the sole administrator of the Business Number, GST/HST (Part IX of the Excise Tax Act), payroll, and Part I corporate income tax.

E-government filing portal

Step 1 - Incorporate or extra-provincially register the Business Corporation: incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA) or the Alberta Business Corporations Act (ABCA) and, if CBCA-incorporated, file an extra-provincial registration with Alberta Corporate Registry before carrying on business in Alberta. Step 2 - Obtain the federal Business Number (BN): file Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) with CRA online or by mail; the BN is the single statutory identifier (9-digit plus 2-letter program account code) used for all CRA programs. Step 3 - Open GST/HST account (RT program account): registration under the same BN is mandatory once worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000, or earlier where the corporation supplies intangible personal property (audio licences) in Canada and wishes to claim input tax credits; non-resident shareholders who need to file Canadian returns should also obtain an Individual Tax Number (ITN) via Form T1261. Step 4 - Open payroll (RP) and corporate income tax (T2) accounts: the T2 account is created automatically when the first corporate return is filed; payroll account is required if employees are hired in Alberta. Step 5 - Open an RM (import/export) account with CRA only if physical media is shipped across the border; digital music licence delivery does not require customs registration.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: a CBCA/ABCA Business Corporation is a separate taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act; no pass-through or check-the-box look-through regime exists in Canada; resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income, and the Alberta jurisdiction does not create a tax-exempt zone; because non-resident ownership exceeds 50%, the corporation is not a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) and therefore loses the small business deduction, resulting in the general combined rate (15% federal + 8% Alberta = 23%) instead of the CCPC combined rate (9% federal + 2% Alberta = 11%).

Available election forms

No check-the-box or elective entity classification exists for Canadian federal or provincial tax purposes - incorporation is conclusive. Relevant available elections for a non-resident-owned licensing entity: (i) ITA s. 216 election to file a net-basis Part I return for Canadian rental/royalty income instead of the flat 25% Part XIII withholding tax; (ii) ITA s. 216.1 election allowing a treaty-protected non-resident to compute Canadian business income on a net basis; (iii) GST/HST elections: annual or quarterly filing election, the Quick Method (Form GST74), and voluntary small-supplier registration to claim input tax credits; (iv) hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.4-18.6) are mandatory, non-elective, and may restrict deductions for payments under hybrid instruments or to hybrid entities.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies (small-supplier threshold). For a non-resident corporation not carrying on business in Canada, taxable supplies to Canadian customers of 'specified supplies' (intangible personal property such as royalty-free music licences) are deemed to be made in Canada for registration purposes once CAD 30,000 of such supplies is exceeded (ETA s. 240(1.1)); registration may also be made voluntarily to recover input tax credits on Canadian production costs.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

The supply of a royalty-free music licence (intangible personal property) qualifies for 0% GST/HST under Schedule VI, Part V, ss. 7(1) and 10(1) of the Excise Tax Act when: (i) the recipient is a non-resident person who is not registered for GST/HST; and (ii) the licence is acquired for use exclusively outside Canada. The place of supply of intangible personal property is Canada where the recipient is in Canada or the licence rights are exercisable in whole or in part in Canada; Canadian resident customers are always charged 5% GST. The zero rate is claimed on the invoice by stating 'Zero-rated supply - Schedule VI, Part V'; if the seller invoices GST in error, the tax must nonetheless be remitted to CRA.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A or G13 EDI) - required solely for physical media shipments; not applicable to digital licence delivery
  • Commercial invoice and signed or subscription-based licensing agreement identifying the licensee, jurisdiction of residence, and exclusive foreign use of the music
  • Evidence of the licensee's non-resident status (foreign business registry extract, certificate of status, or a completed CRA zero-rating certificate)
  • Outward payment evidence demonstrating foreign-source payment (SWIFT MT103, Stripe/PayPal settlement report showing foreign merchant account and currency)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill where physical media carriers are used

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, government API invoicing, or digital stamping. E-invoicing is voluntary under the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Service (Peppol BIS 3.0), launched by the CRA in 2024 and adopted for business-to-government transactions; there is no continuous transaction control. Mandatory compliance points: GST/HST registrants with annual taxable supplies above CAD 1 million must file electronically (GST/HST NETFILE); remittances above CAD 10,000 must be made electronically; all registrants must retain invoices and supporting records in English or French and produce them to CRA in electronic format upon audit.

CRA may reassess within the normal reassessment period (4 years for a corporation that is not a CCPC, a non-resident person, or a partnership; 3 years for individuals, trusts and CCPCs; unlimited for misrepresentation, fraud or wilful default). For GST/HST, the assessment window is 4 years (ETA s. 298). Where export zero-rating is denied on audit (e.g., licence use traced to Canada, customer found to be a GST registrant, or insufficient documentation), CRA assesses 5% GST plus compound daily arrears interest and clawbacks the input tax credits claimed on related inputs. Gross negligence and false statements attract a civil penalty of 50% of the tax understated (ITA s. 163(2); ETA s. 285). Wilful tax evasion is a criminal offence under ITA s. 239 and the Criminal Code, punishable by fines of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to 5 years on indictment. CRA transfer pricing review under ITA s. 247 applies if export receivables remain unpaid beyond a reasonable commercial period; CRA's administrative benchmark for intercompany receivables is 180 days, and exceeding that benchmark without interest supports non-arm's-length reallocation. Fabricated zero-rating certificates or fictitious foreign customers trigger full clawback, gross negligence penalties and referral to the Public Prosecution Service of Canada.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains unrestricted current-account and capital-account convertibility and has accepted IMF Article VIII obligations. There are no exchange-control permits, non-resident deposit restrictions, or central-bank licensing requirements for capital inflows, current licensing fees, royalties, or dividend remittances. The operative hurdle is tax, not FX: payments to non-residents are subject to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (statutory 25% on dividends, royalties, rents, management fees, and certain interest; treaty-reduced, e.g., under the Canada-U.S. tax treaty, generally 5% for direct dividends, 15% for portfolio dividends, and 10% for royalties). The Canadian payor must withhold and remit to the CRA, issue NR4 tax information returns, and follow the CRA treaty-relief documentation process before making reduced-withholding remittances. No Bank of Canada or CRA prior clearance is required simply to convert or remit profits.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender rule, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no statutory repatriation window. Export receipts and royalty/licensing revenues may be retained in a foreign-currency account, such as an American-dollar account at a Canadian bank, or converted at a commercial rate. The only substantive obligations are record-keeping and reporting: financial institutions must file an Electronic Funds Transfer Report with FINTRAC for cross-border EFTs of CAD 10,000 or more, and a currency or monetary-instrument declaration threshold of CAD 10,000 applies to funds physically carried across the border. None of these measures operates as an FX surrender or pricing control.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (central bank); foreign-exchange control function is administered under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act by FINTRAC, while prudential bank supervision is overseen by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility: Medium as a supplementary collection and disbursement rail, but not as the primary treasury account. A Canadian entity may use an offshore digital EMI or payment service provider, such as a U.K., E.U., or Hong Kong licensed PSP, provided the EMI is not marketed in Canada as a deposit-taking institution and any Canadian solicitation activity is avoided unless the entity is registered with FINTRAC as a money services business. The EMI should evidence its home-country authorization, maintain settlement through a regulated sponsor bank, push and pull funds through a Schedule I Canadian bank account, and supply complete transaction-level data. Offshore EMI funds are not CDIC insured, may be subject to foreign insolvency regimes, and are frequently treated by Canadian banks and auditors as higher-risk intermediaries requiring enhanced due diligence.

Before or immediately after crediting, the Canadian recipient must provide the bank with a board resolution approving the issuance, the share-subscription or contribution agreement, UBO declaration, source-of-funds evidence, and, if the shareholder is a legal entity, certified corporate registry documents. Funds may be credited in CAD or USD without Bank of Canada approval; however, if the investment exceeds the screening threshold under the Investment Canada Act, a notification or application may be required.
