Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency / Agence du revenu du Canada (CRA/ARC), the federal tax authority, operating the Business Registration Online (BRO) service and the 'My Business Account' (MyBA) portal, which administer GST/HST, corporate income tax, payroll, and import/export program accounts. Alberta corporate income tax is administered by the CRA on behalf of the Province of Alberta (Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration, under the Alberta Corporate Tax Act and the federal-provincial tax collection agreement); no separate provincial corporate income tax filing to the Alberta authority is required for a corporation, as the T2 return and provincial schedules are filed with the CRA.

E-government filing portal

A Business Corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Alberta Business Corporations Act obtains its statutory tax identifier through the corporate registry and OneStop Business Registry data transmission to the CRA, which issues the 9-digit Business Number (BN) with program account identifiers: RC for corporate income tax (T2), RT for GST/HST, RP for payroll, and RD for import/export. Foreign entities without Canadian incorporation may apply directly to the CRA via Form RC1 or the BRO portal, and a simplified non-resident GST/HST registration channel exists for non-resident suppliers of specified digital services. Standard BN issuance takes 2 to 10 business days; the BN must be quoted on all invoices, GST/HST returns, T2 corporate tax returns, and customs documentation. Annual T2 filing is mandatory for every active corporation irrespective of taxable income, due no later than six months after fiscal year-end, and the Alberta corporate tax return is integrated within the federal T2 package.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation (a Canadian Business Corporation is a separate taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act; the federal general corporate rate is 15%, the Alberta general corporate rate is 8%, producing a combined headline rate of 23%; an eligible Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) benefits from the federal 9% small business rate on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income plus the Alberta 2% small business rate, a combined 11%; non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada are taxable on Canadian-source permanent establishment income with an additional branch tax on after-tax profits; Alberta has no municipal corporate income tax and no general tax-exempt zone, although certain Indigenous reserve and development zone activities may attract limited GST concessions and tax incentive carve-outs). — A Business Corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Alberta Business Corporations Act obtains its statutory tax identifier through the corporate registry and OneStop Business Registry data transmission to the CRA, which issues the 9-digit Business Number (BN) with program account identifiers: RC for corporate income tax (T2), RT for GST/HST, RP for payroll, and RD for import/export. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (a Canadian Business Corporation is a separate taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act; the federal general corporate rate is 15%, the Alberta general corporate rate is 8%, producing a combined headline rate of 23%; an eligible Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) benefits from the federal 9% small business rate on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income plus the Alberta 2% small business rate, a combined 11%; non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada are taxable on Canadian-source permanent establishment income with an additional branch tax on after-tax profits; Alberta has no municipal corporate income tax and no general tax-exempt zone, although certain Indigenous reserve and development zone activities may attract limited GST concessions and tax incentive carve-outs).

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box classification regime; a Business Corporation cannot elect to be treated as a pass-through entity. The relevant statutory elections include: (i) the Excise Tax Act section 156 election for closely related corporations to account for GST/HST at nil or reduced net tax, including corporate groups holding zero-rated export businesses; (ii) the Income Tax Act section 216 election for non-residents to compute tax on Canadian rental and royalty income; (iii) the Income Tax Act section 219(2) branch tax election and filing for non-resident corporations; (iv) GST/HST filing-period elections (annual, quarterly, or monthly) subject to CRA registration and revenue thresholds; (v) the simplified non-resident digital services registration and reporting election for suppliers of specified digital services; (vi) the Excess Interest and Financing Expenses Limitation (EIFEL) regime, applicable to large corporate groups for taxation years beginning on or after October 1, 2024, which limits net interest expense to 30% of tax EBITDA and reflects OECD hybrid and interest limitation principles; and (vii) CCPC status determination and associated small business deduction allocation elections among associated corporations. No election is available to convert a corporation into a partnership or to treat it as a transparent entity for federal or Alberta income tax purposes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in total taxable supplies (including taxable supplies of associated persons, as measured in Canada and abroad) over any single calendar quarter and the four preceding consecutive calendar quarters is the small supplier threshold; exceeding CAD 30,000 triggers mandatory GST/HST registration. Non-resident suppliers of specified digital services to Canadian recipients also become subject to the simplified registration regime once the same CAD 30,000 threshold is exceeded. A cybersecurity SaaS provider selling to Canadian business customers will typically cross this threshold quickly and should obtain an RT program account before issuing taxable invoices; voluntary registration is available below the threshold to claim input tax credits, and registration is retroactive into the quarter in which the threshold was exceeded.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act for supplies of services made to a non-resident recipient where the service is not in relation to real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance, and for supplies of intangible personal property (such as SaaS access rights) made for use exclusively outside Canada. Under CRA's cloud computing and digital services positions, a cybersecurity SaaS supply (cloud vulnerability scanning and penetration testing) provided to non-resident enterprise clients is zero-rated when the customer is a non-resident and the service is not utilized for Canadian-situated assets or Canadian-risk infrastructure. The supplier must obtain and retain documentary evidence establishing the non-resident status of the recipient and the non-Canadian place of use, including contracts, billing and registered-office addresses, foreign tax identifiers, IP/domain data, and user-location telemetry; input tax credits attributable to the zero-rated export remain fully recoverable, enabling net GST/HST refunds to the exporter.

  • CBSA Export Declaration (B13A) submitted electronically through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) — required only for tangible goods or mixed goods/services export shipments; not triggered for a pure software-as-a-service export
  • Signed SaaS subscription or master services agreement, zero-rated commercial invoices, and service delivery logs establishing the non-resident recipient, the agreed foreign-use scope, and the absence of Canadian-situated risk assets
  • Bank wire remittance advices (SWIFT MT103 confirmations), Stripe settlement records, and foreign bank statements as the functional equivalent of an inward remittance certificate, evidencing receipt and settlement of foreign consideration in the corporate bank account

Canada does not currently operate a federal real-time continuous transaction control (CTC), fiscal clearance, or digital stamping mandate. The CRA requires electronic filing of GST/HST returns by registrants with annual taxable supplies above CAD 1,000,000 and electronic T2 filing by corporations with gross revenue above CAD 1,000,000; no cryptographic invoice stamp or government clearance API applies. The Pan-Canadian PEPPOL e-invoicing interoperability framework is being adopted voluntarily for B2G and B2B transactions, and the federal government is migrating toward PEPPOL-based electronic invoicing for procurement invoices; Alberta does not impose the Quebec-style QST electronic invoice and transactional data reporting regime, which is limited to Quebec. A SaaS exporter should nonetheless maintain machine-readable invoices and full audit trails to support GST/HST zero-rating and CRA electronic records requirements.

The CRA may reassess a purportedly zero-rated export supply as taxable at 5% GST where the export documentation fails to substantiate the non-resident recipient and non-Canadian use; the uncollected or refunded GST is clawed back together with interest, and input tax credit refunds may be denied. Penalties apply under sections 280 to 285 of the Excise Tax Act, including gross negligence penalties of up to 25% of the tax amount sought to be evaded, per-day late-filing penalties, and administrative interest; knowingly false or fraudulent export claims can lead to criminal prosecution and an unlimited limitation period for CRA reassessment in cases of misrepresentation. CBSA penalties may additionally apply where goods-based exports are misdeclared. Canada imposes no statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline; the 180-day benchmark is maintained as a conservative internal documentation, collectibility, and audit-readiness control, and prolonged offshore receivable balances beyond that period invite CRA scrutiny of whether the supply genuinely qualifies as a zero-rated export.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully liberalized and freely convertible currency regime with no exchange controls (controls lifted December 1951; IMF Article VIII obligations accepted). No statutory restriction applies to current-account or capital-account convertibility of CAD; non-residents may repatriate dividends, interest, royalties, intercompany loan repayments, and sale-of-business proceeds without central bank approval. Practical conversion constraints: (i) financial institutions apply sanctions screening under the Special Economic Measures Act and the United Nations Act before settling wires; (ii) Part XIII Income Tax Act (Canada) withholding applies to outbound payments to non-residents (dividends 25%/15%/5% treaty-reduced; interest 25%/0% under most treaties; royalties 25%/10%/0% treaty-reduced); (iii) inbound investment is screened under the Investment Canada Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. 28 (1st Supp.)) - a new Canadian cybersecurity SaaS business is notifiable (s. 11) where gross assets exceed the prescribed threshold (C$5 million), and acquisition of control of a Canadian business by a WTO investor is reviewable only above the annually indexed enterprise-value threshold (C$2.326 billion in 2024 per ISED notices); (iv) a Canadian corporation holding specified foreign property with cost exceeding C$100,000 must file Form T1135; controlled foreign affiliate and non-arm's length transactions require Forms T1134 and T106 respectively. Profit remittance hurdles are limited to corporate solvency tests under the Canada Business Corporations Act and Alberta Business Corporations Act, director resolutions, and documentation for the bank's FINTRAC cross-border wire reporting.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender, mandatory conversion ratio, repatriation timeline, or retention quota on export receipts. A Canadian exporter, including a cybersecurity SaaS vendor, may retain foreign currency in domestic or offshore accounts without Bank of Canada approval. Applicable compliance duties: (a) financial institutions file FINTRAC Electronic Funds Transfer Reports for cross-border wires of C$10,000 or more; (b) physical currency and negotiable instruments crossing the border must be declared on CBSA Form E311/R311 when valued at C$10,000 or more; (c) Statistics Canada and ISED trade reporting applies to goods under the Canadian International Merchandise Trade program, while SaaS services are evidenced through corporate records rather than customs declarations; (d) GST/HST on exported services supplied to non-residents is zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. E-15), requiring the company to retain export evidence in its books; (e) no withholding tax arises on zero-rated SaaS exports, but Part XIII withholding applies to separately charged Canadian-source royalties or licensing fees; (f) foreign exchange gains or losses on retranslation of USD/EUR operating accounts are recognized in income under IAS 21 and taxed under Part I of the Income Tax Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) - central monetary authority under the Bank of Canada Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. B-2); prudential bank supervision delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) under the Bank Act (S.C. 1991, c. 46) and the OSFI Act; payments system oversight by the Bank of Canada under the Payment Clearing and Settlement Act (S.C. 1996, c. 6, as amended) and by Payments Canada under the Canadian Payments Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-21); AML/CFT enforcement by FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (S.C. 2000, c. 17) and the Criminal Code; retail payments regulation by the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (S.C. 2021, c. 23); official international reserves held in the Exchange Fund Account managed by the Minister of Finance and operated by the Bank of Canada, imposing no constraint on private-sector FX flows.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 for cross-border goods transactions; CAD 500,000 for cross-border services, interest, and royalty transactions (per fiscal year).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a formal Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) like the UAE, but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under Section 245 of the Income Tax Act and the 'substance over form' doctrine apply. A corporation must demonstrate real economic substance (e.g., employees, office premises, business activities) to avoid GAAR challenges and to qualify for treaty benefits. The CRA may disregard transactions lacking commercial substance and recharacterize them for tax purposes.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act (Section 239) and Criminal Code (Section 380): fines up to 200% of the tax evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years, and civil penalties up to 50% of the tax avoided.

Director personal liability: Directors can be held personally liable for corporate tax debts if they participated in or consented to the evasion; may face civil restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR may recharacterize transactions, deny treaty benefits, and impose penalties up to 50% of the tax avoided (Section 245); CRA may also assess gross negligence penalties of 50% of the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for negligence if they approved or facilitated the sham structure; courts may pierce the corporate veil for fraud or sham, leading to personal liability for unpaid taxes and penalties.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) / Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 2 million for corporations; criminal fines up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years for individuals; for directors, fines up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable if they knowingly failed to implement AML policies or were negligent in oversight; may face criminal charges and travel restrictions.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust prices to arm's length, impose penalties up to 10% of the net adjustment (if gross negligence), and charge interest on reassessments; failure to file T106 incurs a penalty of CAD 2,500 per month (up to 12 months).

Director personal liability: Directors are generally not personally liable for transfer pricing penalties, but may be liable for unremitted source deductions or GST/HST if the corporation fails to remit; in cases of fraud, personal liability may arise.
