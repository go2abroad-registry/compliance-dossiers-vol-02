Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); primary portal: My Business Account at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html and Business Registration Online (BRO). Alberta corporate income tax is collected by the CRA on behalf of Alberta Treasury Board and Finance under the federal-provincial Tax Collection Agreement; the Alberta Corporate Registry handles extra-provincial registration and provincial statute filings.

E-government filing portal

A Canadian business corporation receives its 9-digit Business Number (BN) automatically upon incorporation under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Alberta Business Corporations Act. For foreign entities: (1) determine whether the non-resident corporation carries on business in Canada or makes taxable supplies in Canada; (2) register online through Business Registration Online (BRO) or by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) and Form RC1A (GST/HST account application); (3) appoint an authorized Canadian representative/agent if the non-resident has no physical Canadian presence; (4) obtain the mandatory BN program accounts, including the RT account for GST/HST and the RC account for Part I corporate tax; (5) the BN is the functional TIN/VAT ID for all CRA programs and must appear on invoices, GST/HST returns, and T2 corporate returns; no separate national TIN exists in Canada.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: A Canadian Business Corporation is a separate person under Part I of the Income Tax Act and pays federal and Alberta corporate income tax in a single integrated T2 return; Canada has no pass-through or S-corp analogue and no federal check-the-box regime. The corporation is a separate GST/HST registrant. Non-resident hybrid entities such as US LLCs are recharacterized under the LLC conduit rules (ss. 94.1-94.3 of the Income Tax Act) and the Canada-US Treaty fiscal transparency protocol. Territorial scope: Canadian-resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income; non-resident corporations are taxed on Canadian-source business income under Part I and passive income under Part XIII withholding. Alberta imposes no provincial sales tax.

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a check-the-box election. Elective and structured mechanisms include: (1) section 216 Income Tax Act election for a non-resident to report net Canadian rental income instead of gross Part XIII withholding; (2) section 217 Income Tax Act election for a non-resident employee to file net-income returns for Canadian employment income; (3) section 273 Excise Tax Act election for closely related GST/HST registrants to treat qualifying intra-group supplies as zero-rated; (4) Quick Method of Accounting election under section 229 ETA for small registrants; (5) mandatory hybrid-mismatch countermeasures under sections 18.2 and 18.3 of the Income Tax Act (no opt-out, aligned with BEPS Action 2); and (6) treaty-based relief mechanisms, including Form NR7 waiver applications, competent-authority mutual agreement procedures, and LLC fiscal-transparency characterization under the Canada-US Tax Convention. The Alberta Small Business Deduction (2% provincial rate on up to CAD 500,000 of active business income) is automatic and requires no affirmative election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: a person making taxable supplies in Canada (including zero-rated supplies) must register for GST/HST once total worldwide taxable revenues, other than financial services, exceed CAD 30,000 per calendar year. Registration is voluntary below the threshold and is required for non-resident suppliers of specified digital services to Canadian consumers under the 2021 simplified non-resident registration regime once their Canadian taxable supplies exceed the same threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating eligibility derives from Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act. (1) Goods: a sale is zero-rated where the supplier exports the goods as soon as is reasonable after title passes or delivers them to a common carrier for export; CBSA export reporting and shipping records must evidence foreign delivery. (2) Services: a service supplied to a non-resident person is zero-rated unless the service is rendered to an individual present in Canada, is in respect of real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada, or is acquired for consumption, use, or enjoyment in Canada. (3) Intangible personal property: software, code libraries, digital templates, and online platform access supplied to a non-resident for use exclusively outside Canada are zero-rated. For a low-code/no-code website builder offering subscriptions through Stripe or Paddle to foreign B2B customers, export zero-rating applies if the recipient is non-resident and the digital service is not consumed in Canada; the supplier must retain evidence of the recipient's non-resident status (foreign incorporation, registered address, payment bank jurisdiction, IP geolocation, and service contract).

  • CBSA Export Declaration (Form B13A) filed electronically with the Canada Border Services Agency or an approved service provider, or the equivalent carrier export manifest
  • Bill of Lading (ocean/air/rail/truck) or courier airway bill evidencing export from Canada and delivery to the foreign consignee
  • Inward payment / foreign exchange remittance evidence: bank transfer, SWIFT/ISO 20022 message, or payment service provider settlement report. Canada does not operate an Inward Remittance Certificate regime; CRA accepts interbank payment records as export substantiation.

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls, API stamping, or digital fiscal invoicing. The CRA accepts paper or electronic invoices; a registered supplier's invoice must show the supplier's name and Business Number, the recipient's identity, the transaction description, and the GST/HST charged or a zero-rating indicator. GST/HST returns are filed periodically via GST/HST NETFILE; registrants with annual taxable supplies above CAD 1.5 million must file electronically. The CRA participates voluntarily in the Pan-Canadian Peppol-based e-invoice interoperability framework, but issuance is optional and is not a fiscal clearance requirement. Books and records must be retained for six years.

The CRA audits export zero-rating claims by matching GST/HST returns with CBSA trade data; where documentary proof is absent, the supply is recharacterized as taxable and the 5% GST is assessed with compound daily interest. Civil penalties under section 285 of the Excise Tax Act: knowingly or with gross negligence making a false statement or omission in an invoice, return, refund, or claim attracts a penalty of the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the tax or rebate concerned. Offences under section 286 ETA for fraudulent or deceptive export claims are punishable on summary conviction by a fine of CAD 1,000 to CAD 25,000 and/or imprisonment up to 12 months, and on indictment by a fine up to CAD 100,000 and/or imprisonment up to 5 years. Retrospective clawback: the CRA may reverse input tax credits allocated to zero-rated exports, recover refunds paid, and extend the normal 4-year reassessment period indefinitely where misrepresentation, fraud, or neglect is shown. Non-resident suppliers may be required to post security. Good-faith errors may be mitigated through a Voluntary Disclosure Program application or treaty mutual agreement procedure, but deliberate export fraud disqualifies relief. Although no express statutory FX-repatriation deadline applies in Canada, an 180-day benchmark is commonly used as payment-and-remittance substantiation in CRA export reviews; failure to demonstrate foreign-currency repatriation within that window may trigger denial of zero-rating.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current and capital account convertibility. There are no exchange controls and no restrictions on profit remittance, dividend distributions, or capital repatriation for Canadian-resident or non-resident-owned corporations. Payments to non-residents do not require central bank approval, provided that applicable Part XIII withholding taxes are satisfied and cross-border transaction reports are filed through FINTRAC for transfers exceeding CAD 10,000 (or foreign-currency equivalent). The Canadian dollar floats freely; intervention by the Bank of Canada occurs only exceptionally. The Department of Finance governs foreign-exchange policy, while the Bank of Canada acts as fiscal agent for the Government of Canada.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada does not impose mandatory surrender, conversion timelines, or repatriation quotas for export proceeds. Exporters may hold receipts in any freely convertible world currency, and there is no obligation to convert to CAD within any statutory period. Cross-border movements of currency or monetary instruments of CAD 10,000 or more require CBSA reporting; banking transactions may trigger FINTRAC large-transaction and cross-border wire reports. Export documentation (e.g., B13A Export Report) is required under the Customs Act for goods, but solely for customs statistics, not for foreign-exchange disposal.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

High viability as an ancillary settlement rail for Stripe Billing and Paddle payouts, particularly for USD/EUR receipt conversion and small-value global disbursements. However, offshore EMI balances are not CDIC-insured and cannot serve as the primary treasury depository. EMIs must comply with PCMLTFA customer identification and AML requirements, but major Canadian Schedule I banks generally require that final settlement of third-party merchant payouts occur into a CDIC-insured Canadian corporate bank account to minimise counterparty risk.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: High viability as an ancillary settlement rail for Stripe Billing and Paddle payouts, particularly for USD/EUR receipt conversion and small-value global disbursements. However, offshore EMI balances are not CDIC-insured and cannot serve as the primary treasury depository. EMIs must comply with PCMLTFA customer identification and AML requirements, but major Canadian Schedule I banks generally require that final settlement of third-party merchant payouts occur into a CDIC-insured Canadian corporate bank account to minimise counterparty risk.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No de minimis statutory threshold for transfer pricing documentation; CRA expects contemporaneous TP documentation for all non-arm's length cross-border transactions. Form T106 filing threshold is C$1,000,000 aggregate fair-market value of specified non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents. Country-by-Country Report and Master File / Local File obligations apply to multinational groups with consolidated group revenue equal to or exceeding C$880 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have offshore-tribunal Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) akin to the UAE, but economic substance is central to corporate residence, permanent establishment, treaty eligibility, and Part I taxation. Under the GAAR (ITA s.245), arrangements lacking genuine commercial substance and used solely for tax-motivated purposes can be recharacterized. DTAs contain limitation-of-benefits-type rules and general anti-abuse principles that require real business activity, employees, premises, and risk management in Alberta and in any foreign intermediate entity.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.239(1): fine of 50% to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment for up to 5 years; civil gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax under s.163(2); compound daily interest at the prescribed rate; CRA may freeze accounts and certificate liabilities as judgments.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly committed evasion are criminally liable and subject to imprisonment; courts may impose restitution, asset freezes, travel restrictions or bans, and lift the corporate veil to hold directors jointly and severally liable for the evaded taxes.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: ITA s.247(3) penalty of 10% of the net upward adjustment where documentation is insufficient; CRA may also reallocate income, deny tax deduction erosion, and apply interest. Failure to file T106 attracts additional penalties, and GAAR may be applied in egregious cross-border schemes.

Director personal liability: Directors involved in approving false transfer pricing statements may be held grossly negligent, personally liable upon corporate insolvency, and vulnerable to criminal fraud charges if the transfer pricing structure is used as a sham.
Unremitted Payroll / GST-HST

Statutory sanction: ITA s.227 and Excise Tax Act s.316 impose the full unremitted amount, a 10% additional penalty on amounts unpaid after 3 months, and prescribed daily compound interest. CRA may register liens on corporate assets and send third-party demand letters to processor accounts.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable under ITA s.227.1 and Excise Tax Act s.323 for up to 100% of the unremitted source deductions and GST/HST without fault, if they knew or reasonably should have known of the failure; the corporate veil is pierced by statute.
Artificial Shell / AML Failure

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, failure to implement KYC/CDD and report suspicious transactions attracts significant administrative monetary penalties for corporations and criminal prosecution; Alberta's corporate registry may dissolve a non-compliant corporation; CRA may treat the structure as an avoidance arrangement under GAAR and impose all applicable taxes.

Director personal liability: Nominees and directors of a sham corporation may be charged with aiding unlawful activity, face civil restitution, asset confiscation proceedings, travel bans, and imprisonment for up to 5 years where the enterprise is used for money laundering or tax evasion.
