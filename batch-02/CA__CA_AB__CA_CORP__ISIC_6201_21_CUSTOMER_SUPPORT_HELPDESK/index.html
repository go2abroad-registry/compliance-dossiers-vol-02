Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); primary portals: CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/registering-your-business/register.html and CRA My Business Account at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses/business-account.html; federal corporate registry: Corporations Canada, https://www.ic.gc.ca/eic/site/061.nsf/eng/home; Alberta corporate registry: Service Alberta Corporate Registry, https://www.alberta.ca/corporate-registry.

E-government filing portal

For a Canadian Business Corporation (CA_CORP): (1) incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or Alberta Business Corporations Act and obtain Articles of Incorporation; (2) register with CRA for a 9-digit Business Number (BN) and program accounts - RT (GST/HST), RP (federal/Alberta corporate income tax), RC (payroll if employees are in Canada), RM (customs) as applicable - using Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts' or CRA BRO for simultaneous federal-provincial registration; (3) for a non-resident vendor/parent supplying into Canada, register extra-provincially in Alberta and file Form RC1 as a non-resident GST/HST registrant (or under the simplified foreign-supplier digital-services regime) with the foreign head-office address; (4) the BN functions as the statutory TIN for GST/HST, corporate tax, payroll, and customs purposes; CRA issues the GST/HST registration number instantly upon online registration, and the number must appear on all invoices.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: a Canadian business corporation is a resident corporation taxed under Part I, Income Tax Act (ITA) on worldwide income; federal general corporate rate 15%, Alberta provincial corporate rate 8%, combined 23%; the Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) small-business deduction (federal 9% / Alberta 2% on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income) is unavailable where the corporation is foreign-controlled, so CA_CORP is generally taxed at the general rate; no pass-through or check-the-box regime exists; dividends to non-resident shareholders are subject to Part XIII withholding tax (25% statutory, treaty-reduced), and a non-resident branch is additionally subject to Part XIV branch tax (25%, treaty-reduced).

Available election forms

No U.S.-style check-the-box election: corporate tax identity flows from incorporation law. Relevant elections for a helpdesk/SaaS entity: (i) voluntary GST/HST registration where total taxable supplies do not exceed CAD 30,000 (small supplier) to recover input tax credits; (ii) closely related corporations GST/HST election under s. 156, Excise Tax Act, treating inter-corporate taxable supplies as for nil consideration; (iii) branch or division GST/HST election under s. 150, Excise Tax Act; (iv) joint venture election under s. 273, Excise Tax Act; (v) Canada's OECD hybrid-mismatch rules (BEPS Action 2) disallow deductions on certain hybrid arrangements and contain limited electivity applicable mainly to financing structures, not routine service flows; (vi) Advance Pricing Arrangements with CRA under s. 247, ITA may be elected to stabilize transfer pricing on cross-border service/software fees.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: the small-supplier threshold is based on total worldwide taxable supplies (including those of associated persons) in the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; once exceeded, GST/HST registration is mandatory for a Canadian resident corporation. For a non-resident digital-services supplier (including helpdesk/SaaS), the 2021 digital-economy regime makes registration mandatory when taxable supplies to Canadian consumers (in-Canada recipients) exceed CAD 30,000 per 12-month period; voluntary registration is permitted below these thresholds.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

The helpdesk/SaaS cloud supply qualifies as a zero-rated export of service under Part V, Schedule VI, Excise Tax Act, s. 7, where: (i) the customer is a non-resident of Canada for GST/HST purposes; (ii) the service is not rendered to the non-resident while physically in Canada; and (iii) the service does not relate to real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance. AI-driven conversational bot/helpdesk services also fall within the call-centre deeming rule in s. 7.1, which zero-rates call-centre services supplied to non-residents regardless of where the calls originate. Additional conditions: the non-resident customer must not be registered for GST/HST in respect of the supply (or, if registered, the supply must not be part of a Canadian taxable activity), and contemporaneous evidence of the customer's non-resident status, address, and settlement currency must be maintained. No CRA pre-approval is required; zero-rating is claimed on the invoice with a statement of export.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A / electronic export reporting under Canada's Export Reporting regime) - applicable only where physical media or hardware is exported; not required for pure cloud helpdesk services
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill - required only for accompanying physical goods; for pure SaaS deliverables the equivalent evidence is the executed service agreement and server/delivery access logs
  • Inward remittance evidence (bank credit advices, SWIFT confirmations, or Stripe/PayPal settlement records) - Canada does not issue formal 'inward remittance certificates'; bank records evidencing foreign currency receipt are the accepted substantive equivalent
  • Non-resident Customer Status Declaration (name, address, country of residence, GST/HST registration number if registered, and a statement that the supply is for consumption outside Canada)
  • Commercial invoice with the vendor's CRA BN/GST registration number, a 'Zero-Rated Export - Schedule VI, Part V, ETA' notation, and the export currency and settlement terms

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government invoice pre-approval exists at the federal or Alberta level; the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Network (Peppol/EN 16931 standard) is an optional B2B interoperability framework and does not involve CRA clearance. Invoices must include the vendor's BN/GST registration number and the consideration or, for zero-rated exports, a statement of zero-rating; electronic invoices are accepted as valid books and records, and all supporting documentation must be retained for six years under s. 286, Excise Tax Act. Self-assessment filing is done via GST/HST NETFILE and the My GST/HST Business Account; no real-time API transmission of issued invoices to CRA is required.

CRA audits zero-rated export claims by testing the customer's non-resident status, the place-of-supply analysis, and the genuineness of the contract; unsupported claims are reassessed within the normal 4-year period for corporations (10 years for neglect, carelessness, or wilful default, and without time limit for fraud). Clawback consequences: CRA assesses GST/HST at 5% on the full consideration, compounded daily prescribed-rate interest, denies associated input tax credits and refunds, and may impose a 25% gross-negligence penalty on GST/HST amounts under s. 285, Excise Tax Act, and a 50% false-statement penalty under s. 163(2), ITA. Criminal prosecution is available under s. 327, Excise Tax Act and s. 239, ITA, with fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years on indictment. The due-diligence defence is narrow: generic customer self-certificates without commercial substance and round-tripping settlement arrangements are routinely rejected, and the CRA publishes taxpayer names in egregious evasion cases.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully convertible currency under a floating exchange-rate regime. There are no statutory restrictions on current-account convertibility, capital-account convertibility, or the repatriation of profits, dividends, loan repayments, or capital proceeds. The Canadian dollar is freely tradeable in spot and derivatives markets. No foreign-exchange licensing, prior approval, or central bank permission is required for inward capital injections or outward remittances. Residual constraints arise only through sanctions and anti-money-laundering (AML) reporting obligations, not through exchange-control approvals. Non-resident entities must ensure their inbound funding and outbound distributions do not trigger FINTRAC reporting thresholds (e.g., 24-hour $10,000 CAD large virtual currency transaction reporting, $10,000 CAD cash transaction reporting) and must comply with tax withholding obligations, particularly Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on dividends, interest, rents, and royalties.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no prescribed timeline for repatriation of export receipts in Canada. Exporters may retain foreign currency, convert it at any time, or use it directly for offshore financial obligations. Invoicing, settlement currency, and the timing of repatriation are commercial decisions. However, all export-related remittances above FINTRAC thresholds are subject to transaction reporting to FINTRAC, and the business must maintain records sufficient to evidence the underlying export transaction and the disposition of the funds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (central bank); Foreign Exchange Administration function effectively resides with the Minister of Finance and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for countering money laundering and with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential regulation of financial institutions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium for ancillary treasury operations, but Low as a primary clearing rail for CAD-denominated liquidity. Canada does not use the European EMI licensing framework; there is no passportable EU EMI. A foreign electronic-money institution can operate in Canada only as a money services business (MSB) registered with FINTRAC, or as an agent/payee of a FINTRAC-registered MSB, and must comply with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Offshore digital EMIs cannot legally hold Canadian-dollar settlement accounts with Canadian clearing systems unless they are licensed/registered under Canadian banking or payment-service rules. They may be used as supplementary cross-border payment conduits, but all CAD settlement and payroll/trade disbursement flows should be routed through a Schedule I or Schedule II bank that participates in the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) or the Real-Time Rail (RTR) once available. EMI balances must never be treated as bank deposits for regulatory or treasury-risk purposes; they carry uninsured counterparty risk.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections into the Alberta corporation should be routed via wire transfer using the SWIFT MT103 message standard from the originating bank to the beneficiary account in Canada. The corporation must provide its bank with the purpose of the inbound transfer using standard Canadian settlement narratives, including 'Capital injection for business operations', 'Shareholder loan', or 'Equity subscription'. There is no Canadian statutory FX registration or capital-control approval code equivalent to emerging-market regimes. The bank will rely on the end-to-end payment instructions and the corporate bank's compliance screening. To avoid rejection, the remitter must include the full corporate legal name, the account number, and the Alberta corporate registry number; the beneficiary bank in Canada must be provided with a copy of the board resolution authorizing receipt of the funds. For amounts above CAD 100,000, the bank may request the underlying capital agreement and tax withholding confirmation. The inbound wire should be tagged with the ISO 20022 purpose code CREDT (credit transfer) or the supplementary purpose code EQUI for equity injection, as supported by the intermediary bank; where the Originating Bank lacks ISO 20022, the narrative field should state: 'SHAREHOLDER CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION, NON-RESIDENT, NO PART XIII WITHHOLDING, ARM'S LENGTH'.For trade settlements and operational disbursements from Canada, the preferred clearing rails are SWIFT MT103/202 messages for direct wire transfers and documentary commercial letters of credit (LC) for goods-related cross-border trade. For the helpdesk SaaS business (ISIC 6201), primary outbound disbursements should be corporate operating payments, software licensing fees, subcontractor invoices, tax payments, and profit distributions. When paying foreign subcontractors or software vendors, use the SWIFT MT103 rail with the correct ISO 20022 business purpose code (e.g., SUPP for supplier payments, LICF for IT licensing fees, or ROYL for royalties). For recurring low-value outbound flows, an alternative is to use the banks' API-hosted payment gateway or automated clearing house (ACH) for US-dollar flows via the Canadian clearing house. For payroll to non-resident contractors, issue the settlement through the SWIFT network, withhold any required Canadian non-resident service withholding tax under Part XIII or Part I rules if services are rendered in Canada, and evidence the tax deposit. For profit repatriation, use SWIFT MT103 with narrative 'DIVIDEND DISTRIBUTION, PART XIII WITHHELD' and provide the bank with the dividend declaration and the certificate of withholding. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium for ancillary treasury operations, but Low as a primary clearing rail for CAD-denominated liquidity. Canada does not use the European EMI licensing framework; there is no passportable EU EMI. A foreign electronic-money institution can operate in Canada only as a money services business (MSB) registered with FINTRAC, or as an agent/payee of a FINTRAC-registered MSB, and must comply with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Offshore digital EMIs cannot legally hold Canadian-dollar settlement accounts with Canadian clearing systems unless they are licensed/registered under Canadian banking or payment-service rules. They may be used as supplementary cross-border payment conduits, but all CAD settlement and payroll/trade disbursement flows should be routed through a Schedule I or Schedule II bank that participates in the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) or the Real-Time Rail (RTR) once available. EMI balances must never be treated as bank deposits for regulatory or treasury-risk purposes; they carry uninsured counterparty risk.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 for goods; CAD 500,000 for services, intangibles, and other transactions with non-residents

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a specific ESR, but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and the 'economic substance' doctrine apply; a transaction must have a bona fide business purpose and economic substance; shell companies with no real activity may be challenged

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under Income Tax Act s.239; fines up to 200% of tax evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for corporate taxes under s.227.1; civil restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment for fraud
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may disregard the entity under GAAR; penalties up to 50% of tax benefit; criminal charges for fraud

Director personal liability: Directors can be held personally liable for taxes and penalties; travel bans and asset freezes
AML / Money Laundering

Statutory sanction: Under Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, fines up to CAD 2 million for corporations; criminal charges

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally fined and imprisoned up to 5 years; civil liability for restitution
