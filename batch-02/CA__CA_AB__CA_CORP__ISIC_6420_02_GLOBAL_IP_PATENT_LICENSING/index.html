Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – My Business Account portal, GST/HST NETFILE, and Business Registration Online (BRO); Alberta corporate income tax is administered by the CRA under a tax collection agreement with the Government of Alberta (Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration).

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate under the Alberta Business Corporations Act or the Canada Business Corporations Act. Step 2: Apply to the CRA for a 9-digit Business Number (BN) and open program account RC (T2 corporate income tax) via Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1. Step 3: Register the GST/HST program account (RT) on the same BN. Step 4: Elect a GST/HST reporting period (monthly, quarterly, or annual). Step 5: Activate CRA My Business Account for electronic filing and correspondence. Step 6: If a foreign parent or non-resident shareholders exist, file Form NR301 or a Section 216/219 election to manage Part XIII withholding on cross-border royalty payments. Step 7: Obtain a certificate of compliance under the Income Tax Act before fiscal year-end if capital is to be repatriated. Foreign-owned corporations must disclose director and shareholder residency; a non-resident director requires either a residence waiver under Section 105 of the Alberta Business Corporations Act or an undertaking to file Form 105.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation – Canadian business corporations are not pass-through vehicles; they are separate taxable entities subject to Part I corporate income tax on worldwide income and Part IV tax on certain dividends. Alberta imposes a flat 8% provincial corporate income tax administered by the CRA, and Alberta does not levy a provincial sales tax.

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a US-style check-the-box election; entity classification follows the Income Tax Act (Canada) (ITA). Elections available to an IP HoldCo include (i) section 85 ITA rollover for tax-deferred transfer of patents and trademarks; (ii) section 21 ITA optional capitalization of interest; (iii) sections 88 and 93 elections for cross-border dividend and amalgamation planning; (iv) sections 216 and 115.2 elections for non-resident taxpayers; (v) treaty-based royalty withholding relief (e.g., Canada-US treaty Article XII rate reduction) through Form NR301; (vi) the GST/HST closely-related corporation election under section 22 of the Excise Tax Act; and (vii) compliance with hybrid mismatch rules under ITA sections 18.2 and 18.3 that deny excessive deductions on hybrid royalty or loan arrangements.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies (small supplier threshold, Excise Tax Act section 240); registration becomes mandatory once the threshold is exceeded, with a mandatory effective date for non-resident suppliers carrying on business in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to exported goods (Schedule VI, Part V, section 1), to services supplied to non-residents (section 7), and to intangible personal property such as patents or trademarks supplied to a non-resident where the supply is not made in Canada (section 10). A patent or trademark license granted by the Canadian HoldCo to a foreign licensee is zero-rated only if the licensee is a non-resident and the licensed IP is not used in Canada. A license to a GST-registered Canadian licensee is taxable. The supplier must obtain and retain a foreign-status certificate and underlying export evidence before the reporting period.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA B13A Canada Export Declaration or proof of permitted exception)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical export from Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / SWIFT wire or bank confirmation of foreign-currency receipt
  • Fully executed patent/trademark license agreement identifying grantor, licensee, royalty, and territory
  • GST/HST zero-rated or exempt invoice containing CRA-required particulars
  • Non-resident status evidence of the licensee (foreign tax identification number or certificate of incorporation)
  • Place-of-supply documentation showing the licensed IP is not used in Canada

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, API clearance, or digital stamping regime exists in Canada. Pan-Canadian e-invoicing is voluntary and built on the Peppol network. GST/HST invoices must contain the supplier's BN, invoice date, description, and tax amount for taxable supplies above CAD 30. Returns are transmitted electronically via GST/HST NETFILE; no pre-clearance or government validation of individual invoices is required.

CRA scrutiny focuses on whether the zero-rating conditions under the Excise Tax Act are substantiated. Penalties include (i) gross negligence penalties equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the amount under section 280, (ii) false statement penalties under section 285.1, (iii) daily compound interest on unremitted GST/HST, (iv) retrospective reassessment for up to 4 years (unlimited for misrepresentation), and (v) personal liability of directors under section 323. Criminal prosecution under the Criminal Code may follow fraudulent export or false zero-rating claims.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada offers full current-account and capital-account convertibility under IMF Article VIII and maintains an open capital account with no exchange controls, quotas, pre-approval permits, or repatriation restrictions on non-resident investments, royalties, dividends, interest, or intercompany settlements. Patent royalty remittances from an Alberta IP HoldCo are subject to Part XIII statutory withholding tax of 25%, reduced to 10% under the Canada-US income tax treaty and most comprehensive double-tax treaties for patent royalties; no provincial-level withholding tax is imposed by Alberta. Remittance documentation must satisfy Canadian transfer pricing rules and income tax reporting, but no central bank licence or FX approval is required for any royalty related cross-border flow.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender obligation, residual maturity timeline, or mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts or cross-border commercial proceeds. Export invoices may be settled in any convertible currency, and proceeds may be retained in foreign currency in Canadian or offshore bank accounts without conversion. Reporting duties are limited to FINTRAC electronic funds transfer declarations for transactions of CAD 10,000 or more and Income Tax Act information returns such as Form T1135 for specified foreign property.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), operating jointly with the Department of Finance Canada, OSFI, and FINTRAC; the Bank of Canada operates the national monetary and FX policy framework and is the settlement authority for the LYNX large-value payment system.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Offshore digital EMIs holding only EU or UK EMI authorisation are not permitted to provide Canadian-domiciled IBANs, local chequing rails, or custodial CAD accounts and cannot act as the primary treasury bank for an Alberta IP HoldCo. Any non-bank provider operating in Canada must register as a money services business with FINTRAC and comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act, which creates medium onboarding complexity and legal risk for high-value intercompany royalties. A foreign EMI is viable only as a low-value disbursement conduit for outbound recurring payments, with feasibility rated Medium and with the prohibition that all inbound royalty collections be routed to a Canadian Schedule I bank account.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined (15% federal Part I + 8% Alberta Provincial Corporate Tax); small-business combined rate 11% (9% federal + 2% Alberta) restricted to CCPCs and unavailable to non-resident-controlled IP HoldCos

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid to non-resident shareholders (s. 212(2) ITA); reduced to 5% under Article X of the Canada–US DTA for a US corporate parent owning at least 10% of the voting stock (5% rate also applies to many EU treaties, e.g., UK, Germany, Netherlands, Luxembourg with shareholding thresholds)

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD — Canada imposes no bright-line statutory threshold for preparing a Local File or Master File; instead, contemporaneous documentation of the transfer price and the selection of the most appropriate method is required for every cross-border non-arm's-length transaction under ITA s. 247(4). The 10% transfer-pricing penalty under s. 247(3) is triggered only where the net adjustment exceeds CAD 50,000 per assessment and the taxpayer cannot demonstrate reasonable efforts; CRA tactical practice intensifies file review and requests for CAD 10,000,000+ royalty flows.

Economic substance scope: Canada applies no formal Economic Substance Regulations in the style of the Caribbean or UAE regimes. Substance is nonetheless mandatory through: (i) the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s. 245 ITA) disallowing tax benefits from transaction avoidance lacking economic substance/business purpose; (ii) treaty anti-abuse jurisprudence and LOB provisions in the Canada–US DTA requiring the IP HoldCo to be genuinely engaged in an active trade or business in Alberta, with employees, office premises, and actual management and control; (iii) CRA's transfer-pricing documentation expectations requiring that the Canadian entity bear real functional, economic, and operational activities and risks corresponding to the royalty income; and (iv) the potential re-characterisation of a hollow holding company as an agent or conduit of the non-resident parent, thereby collapsing treaty access and triggering the 25% Part XIII withholding on any repatriation.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Transfer Pricing Misstatement (s. 247 ITA)

Statutory sanction: 10% penalty on the net TP adjustment exceeding CAD 50,000; tax reassessment for the full quantum of the adjustment; compound interest on the underpayment (prime + 4%); extended normal reassessment period of 6+ years in cases involving misrepresentation attributable to negligence; the penalty cannot be relieved by voluntary disclosure

Director personal liability: Directors of the Canadian licensor or licensee may be personally liable for the unpaid Part I balance attributable to the TP adjustment only where the corporation fails to pay after formal demand (s. 226.1/s. 227.1 ITA); the CRA pursues directors for civil restitution and interest, and criminal negligence that leads to false reporting invites prosecution of controlling officers under s. 239 ITA (fines up to 200% of taxes evaded and imprisonment)
Tax Evasion / False Statements (s. 239 ITA)

Statutory sanction: Fine of 50%–200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to 5 years; parallel criminal prosecution and civil reassessment, plus interest and penalties; the taxpayer bears the burden of proving innocence in criminal matters

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorise or participate in the false return incur personal exposure; the court may order restitution to the Crown and impose a travel ban or seizure of passports pending criminal proceedings; non-resident directors may be extradited to Canada under bilateral extradition treaties
Shell Company Abuse / GAAR and Treaty Shopping (s. 245 ITA)

Statutory sanction: GAAR denial of the tax benefit, full reassessment of the avoided withholding tax at 25% Part XIII, interest, and a 25% penalty of the tax benefit under s. 245(5) unless avoidance was disclosed; in abuse of treaty cases the CRA may also deny the entity's filing compliance once the company is deemed to lack substance; corporate dissolution or strike-off may follow

Director personal liability: Directors may be sued by the CRA as assessable persons, held personally to account for the Part XIII amounts withheld or required to be withheld, and face disqualification from serving as directors; travel bans are available as ancillary relief in proceedings alleging deliberate conduit arrangements, in addition to civil restitution under common law breach-of-fiduciary-duty claims
Failure to Withhold Part XIII on Cross-Border Dividends and Royalties (s. 215 ITA)

Statutory sanction: The Canadian payer is personally liable for the full amount required to be withheld, plus interest and penalties even if the amount was never deducted; CRA routinely issues no-contest assessments; the obligation is not extinguished until it is actually remitted; fines on conviction for deliberate non-remittance can reach 200% of the amount

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable under s. 227.1 ITA for unreported Part XIII withholding, interest, and penalties where the corporation is insolvent; the Director's liability is absolute unless due diligence is proved, and unpaid assessments entitle CRA to freeze director bank accounts, register liens against real property, and seek civil court enforcement and travel restraints
AML / FINTRAC Non-Compliance (PCMLTFA, Special Economic Measures Act)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 1,000,000 per entity violation; criminal conviction on indictment with fines up to CAD 2,000,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years; the CRA also suspends GST/HST and income tax refunds for non-compliant reporting entities and may revoke the business number

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who knowingly fail to implement the AML program are subject to personal AMPs of up to CAD 500,000, criminal fines, imprisonment for up to 5 years, personal asset freezing and travel bans pending investigation; civil liability to counterparties damaged by sanctioned transfers is also actionable
