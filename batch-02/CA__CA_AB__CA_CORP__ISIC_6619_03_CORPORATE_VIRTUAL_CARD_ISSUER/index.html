Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; corporate income tax and GST/HST are administered federally by the CRA through the Business Registration Online portal and CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/services/taxes/business.html). Alberta also imposes no provincial sales tax, so no separate provincial VAT or provincial TIN exists for the CA_CORP entity.

E-government filing portal

(2) Obtain the CRA Business Number (BN) via Business Registration Online or CRA Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number', selecting program account RC for federal and Alberta corporate income tax (T2). Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: A federal or Alberta business corporation is a separate taxpayer under ITA s. 15(1) and is not a pass-through entity. Territorial scope: a corporation incorporated in Canada is resident in Canada and taxed on worldwide income; a non-resident corporation is taxed under Part I only on Canadian-source business and employment income and gains from taxable Canadian property. The CA_CORP entity is subject to federal Part I tax at 15% general rate plus Alberta corporate income tax at 8%, for a combined headline rate of 23%; the small business deduction is unavailable because a foreign-controlled corporate virtual card issuer is not a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC). If the Canadian operations were carried on by a non-resident corporation through an unincorporated branch, Part XIV branch tax at 25% would apply, subject to treaty relief.

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box regime; classification follows corporate law and the Income Tax Act. Available elections include: (i) ETA s. 156 election for closely related corporations to treat taxable supplies as made for nil consideration and to transfer input tax credits; (ii) ITA s. 216 election for a non-resident corporation to be taxed on a net basis on Canadian rental income; (iii) ITA s. 219 branch tax rules for non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada; (iv) hybrid mismatch provisions in ITA ss. 18.4-18.6, 20.01, 94.2 and 95.2 (enacted by Bill C-59, royal assent 20 June 2024, applying to payments made and transactions completed after 30 June 2024), which deny deductions for and impose adjustments on structured and imported hybrid mismatch arrangements; and (v) voluntary registration for GST/HST under ETA s. 240(1). No U.S.-style disregarded-entity election is recognized for Canadian federal tax purposes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters (general small-supplier threshold, ETA s. 148); for a selected listed financial institution (SLFI), the registration threshold is CAD 1,000,000 of annual Canadian revenue (ETA s. 240(1.1)). A corporate virtual card issuer should assess SLFI status under ETA s. 149(1) because most financial services are exempt from GST/HST and exempt supplies do not generate input tax credits unless the SLFI attribution methodology applies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under ETA Schedule VI, exported supplies are zero-rated at 0% GST: (i) goods are zero-rated when physically exported from Canada with export completed as soon as is reasonable in the circumstances, and, where required, evidenced within 60 days; and (ii) services supplied to a non-resident are zero-rated when performed entirely outside Canada, or performed in Canada in respect of exported goods or goods for export (Schedule VI Part V). For financial services, Schedule VI Part IX s. 7 zero-rates a supply of a financial service made to a non-resident person unless the service relates to a credit card or charge card facility issued to a Canadian-resident cardholder, Canadian real property, or insurable property in Canada. A corporate virtual card issuer may zero-rate B2B card-issuing, interchange and processing fees charged to foreign corporate clients only where the card facility is not issued for use by Canadian-resident cardholders; technical legal review is mandatory. Zero-rated supplies preserve the input tax credit mechanism, whereas exempt financial services under Schedule V Part VII do not.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A / CAED or CBSA electronic export report) where physical corporate cards or hardware are shipped from Canada
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill showing departure from Canada and foreign destination
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank wire or foreign-exchange settlement evidence (CRA accepts SWIFT MT103 or FTT/NTR records) proving receipt of foreign-currency consideration from the non-resident client
  • Non-resident status evidence: foreign tax identification number, certificate of residence (NR301/RC91) or jurisdiction-of-incorporation extract, together with the underlying card-issuing contract and transaction-level settlement reports from Visa, Mastercard or other payment rails

No real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control (CTC), digital stamping, or tax-authority API clearance mandate exists in Canada. Canada is progressively adopting PEPPOL e-invoicing for business-to-government transactions (Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Sprint), but CRA does not mandate e-invoice clearance for GST/HST. A GST/HST registrant must issue invoices containing the prescribed information under ETA ss. 169(4), 223 and the Input Tax Credit Information Regulations: supplier name, Business Number (BN) with RT program account, invoice date, description of supply, consideration, GST rate and amount. Electronic invoices (PDF, EDI, XML) are accepted; invoices for exempt financial services must state 'No GST/HST charged'. An SLFI has additional annual information return obligations (Form GST111) and must retain all transaction-level records and rail settlement reports. Records must be kept for at least six years under ETA s. 286.

CRA audit is the principal enforcement mechanism: the normal reassessment period is four years, extended to seven years in cases of misrepresentation, neglect or fraud (ETA s. 298; ITA s. 152(4)). Canada imposes no statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for GST/HST purposes, but CRA field practice treats failure to demonstrate receipt of foreign consideration within 180 days as a red flag that may trigger a zero-rating review. If export evidence is inadequate, the CRA claws back the 5% GST or denies the corresponding input tax credits, assesses interest under ETA s. 280 from the original due date, and may impose the ETA s. 285 gross-negligence penalty equal to the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the tax shortfall. For income tax, ITA s. 163(2) imposes a penalty of 50% of the understated tax for gross negligence. Criminal exposure includes tax evasion under ITA s. 239 and ETA s. 326, with fines of 50% to 200% of tax evaded and imprisonment of up to five years on indictment, as well as Criminal Code fraud liability under s. 380(1)(a). Directors and officers of the CA_CORP entity may also face personal liability under the directors' liability provisions of ITA s. 227.1.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full current-account and capital-account convertibility; Canada accepted IMF Article VIII obligations on 25 March 1952. The Currency Act prohibits unauthorized monetary issuance but imposes no exchange-control licensing, prior approvals or repatriation restrictions. Profit-remittance hurdles are purely tax/administrative: non-resident dividend withholding under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (statutory 25%, treaty-reduced to e.g. 5%/15% under Article X of the Canada-US Convention), Part IV tax on portfolio dividends, T106/NR4 reporting, and ITA s.233.4 foreign-property reporting for assets above CAD 100,000. CAD/USD conversion is executed through the clearing bank at market rates; no central-bank authorization or domiciliary FX allocation is required for card-scheme settlement, hedging or capital inflows.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. The Canadian Foreign Exchange Control Act (1946) lapsed in 1951; there is no mandatory export-proceeds surrender, conversion ratio or statutory repatriation timeline. A corporate virtual-card issuer receiving USD settlement from Visa/Mastercard networks may retain, hold in a USD nostro, convert or offset those proceeds freely. Residual obligations: FINTRAC international EFT reports for each wire at or above CAD 10,000; Statistics Canada trade-in-services reporting for card-processing and issuing fees; CRA Part XIII/T106 exposure on any deemed distributions; and IAS 21 hedge and FX-disclosure requirements.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) — monetary authority, custodian of Canada's official FX reserves and operator of the Lynx high-value settlement system; Department of Finance Canada — policy authority for foreign exchange administration under the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act; FINTRAC — financial-intelligence regulator under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), administering cross-border currency and EFT reporting; OSFI — prudential supervisor of Schedule I clearing banks and sponsor-bank counterparties.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Rating: LOW unless restructured. A non-resident offshore EMI cannot lawfully access Canadian payment clearing or card-network settlement because (i) no EU/UK EMI authorisation is passportable into Canada; (ii) FINTRAC requires registration as a Money Services Business (MSB) or Foreign MSB before providing money-moving services to Canadians (PCMLTFA s.11.1); (iii) the Retail Payment Activities Act (S.C. 2021, c.23) requires Bank of Canada payment-service-provider registration for payment functions, including issuing card-based payment instruments — the regime is being phased administratively, so immediate registration filing is the prudent strategy; (iv) Visa and Mastercard require a Canadian principal-member sponsor bank; and (v) provincial MSB/fintech licensing (e.g., Ontario Money Services Act, Quebec MSB regime) may apply based on client touchpoints. Viable construct: the Canadian-incorporated operating company (CA_CORP) acts as programme manager under a sponsor-bank BIN, holds settlement in a segregated float account at a Schedule I bank, and avoids any deposit-taking activity thereby preserving the Bank Act s.545 exemption.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined federal and Alberta general corporate income tax rate, comprising 15% federal tax and 8% Alberta tax. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally obtain a combined rate of approximately 11% on eligible active business income up to the CAD 500,000 business limit, comprising 9% federal tax and 2% Alberta tax, subject to associated-corporation, income-limit, capital, and sector-specific rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. Common treaty outcomes include 5% for a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting ownership and holding-period conditions and 15% for other qualifying treaty residents; the applicable treaty must be checked separately.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no universal monetary threshold that exempts a taxpayer from maintaining contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation or that automatically triggers a standardized Local File or Master File. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires contemporaneous documentation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. Form T106 generally applies where the total reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000, subject to the statutory calculation rules. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year. Canada does not impose a general OECD-style Master File filing requirement for every taxpayer, but CRA may request robust group and transaction documentation.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a standalone regime formally titled Economic Substance Regulations applicable to all entities. CRA applies the general anti-avoidance rule, sham and beneficial-ownership principles, specific anti-avoidance provisions, transfer-pricing rules, interest-limitation rules, and treaty anti-abuse provisions. A virtual card issuer should maintain Canadian decision-making, risk control, operational personnel, technology and compliance capability, contractual authority, records, and capitalization proportionate to its functions. Conduit arrangements, circular funding, artificial royalty or management charges, and treaty shopping may be challenged even where legal documentation exists.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and false corporate reporting

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, interest, civil penalties, gross-negligence penalties, and criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act. Tax evasion convictions may result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years, subject to the applicable offence.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal prosecution where they personally participated, directed, consented to, or acquiesced in the offence. The corporation remains primarily liable; personal civil liability is not automatic but may arise through statutory director-liability provisions, knowing assistance, fraud, breach of duty, or oppression remedies. Travel restrictions are not automatic and require applicable criminal or judicial processes.
Unregistered money services business and AML violations

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act may reach CAD 100,000 for a minor violation and CAD 1,000,000 for a serious violation, with criminal fines and imprisonment for specified offences. FINTRAC may publish violations and impose registration consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and compliance personnel may be prosecuted for personally directing or participating in offences and may face fines or imprisonment. Corporate veil piercing is not automatic. Individual exposure may also arise for obstruction, false records, tipping-off, or failure to comply with a production or preservation obligation.
Payment-services licensing and safeguarding failure

Statutory sanction: The Bank of Canada may impose administrative monetary penalties, compliance directions, public notices, and registration-related measures under the applicable payment-services framework. Separate provincial, federal banking, consumer-protection, securities, and network-rule consequences may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally exposed for knowing authorization of unlawful operations, misleading regulators, breach of fiduciary duty, or reckless handling of safeguarded funds. Imprisonment is not an ordinary regulatory consequence but may arise from separate fraud, obstruction, or criminal offences.
Unlawful deposit-taking or misrepresentation as a bank

Statutory sanction: The Bank Act and other financial-services legislation may prohibit unauthorized deposit-taking and restricted banking representations. Consequences may include injunctions, prosecution, fines, restitution, contractual termination, and regulatory enforcement.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize or knowingly permit prohibited representations or activities may face prosecution and personal civil claims. Individual imprisonment depends on the specific offence and proof of personal involvement.
Transfer-pricing manipulation and profit diversion

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under section 247, deny or adjust deductions, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the adjusted amount where the statutory conditions are met, apply withholding tax, and charge interest. Treaty relief may be denied under anti-abuse principles.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil claims for approving unlawful or misleading arrangements and may be criminally liable if they knowingly participate in tax evasion or false statements. A mere transfer-pricing adjustment does not itself create automatic personal liability or imprisonment.
Improper dividend, capital reduction, or shareholder extraction

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include deemed dividends, shareholder-loan inclusions, Part XIII assessments, withholding penalties, interest, denial of deductions, director repayment obligations under corporate law, and creditor remedies.

Director personal liability: Directors may be required to repay unlawful distributions and may be liable for unpaid source deductions in specified circumstances under the Income Tax Act. Fraudulent extraction can support oppression, fraudulent-preference, trust, restitution, and criminal claims.
Payroll, source-deduction, and trust-fund failure

Statutory sanction: Assessments, failure-to-remit penalties, interest, director-liability assessments, garnishment, liens, and criminal prosecution may apply. Source deductions and GST/HST collected or withheld are trust-type amounts and receive heightened enforcement priority.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted payroll deductions and other prescribed amounts where statutory due-diligence defenses are unavailable. Criminal prosecution may follow deliberate non-remittance, fraud, or obstruction.
Privacy, card-data, and cybersecurity breach

Statutory sanction: Federal and provincial privacy enforcement, contractual assessments, mandatory breach notifications, civil damages, regulatory orders, card-network fines, remediation costs, and potential statutory penalties may apply. Alberta private-sector privacy law may apply depending on the entity, data, and activity.

Director personal liability: Personal liability is generally not automatic, but may arise from knowing participation, bad faith, negligence, oppression, breach of fiduciary duty, or statutory offences. Directors should maintain documented oversight, incident response, and privacy governance.
Sanctions evasion, terrorist financing, or prohibited counterparties

Statutory sanction: Freezing, seizure, reporting, licence cancellation, administrative penalties, and criminal prosecution may arise under Canadian sanctions and anti-terrorist-financing legislation. Criminal offences can carry substantial fines and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face prosecution where they knowingly participate, direct, facilitate, or recklessly disregard prohibited transactions. Corporate indemnities may not protect against criminal fines or restitution.
Corporate-record and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: Corporate-law offences, fines, compliance orders, registry consequences, account restrictions, AML enforcement, and evidentiary inferences may result. False statements to regulators may create separate criminal exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers responsible for records may face fines and prosecution for knowingly false or incomplete information. Personal travel bans are not an automatic Canadian corporate penalty and require a specific legal or judicial basis.
