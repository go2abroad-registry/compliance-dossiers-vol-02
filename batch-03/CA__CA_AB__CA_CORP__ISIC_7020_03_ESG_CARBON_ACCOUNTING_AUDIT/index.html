Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); official portal: 'My Business Account' and 'Business Registration Online' at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html. Alberta corporate income tax is administered under the federal-provincial Tax Administration Agreement and filed through the federal T2 return, with the Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration as the provincial receiving authority.

E-government filing portal

A Canadian-resident Business Corporation obtains its statutory 9-digit Business Number (BN) automatically through the integrated incorporation process (federal via Corporations Canada/'Business Registration Online' or Alberta via the Alberta Corporate Registry's Corporate Online service). Non-resident and extra-provincial entities must register manually: first complete extra-provincial registration under the Alberta Business Corporations Act, then file Form RC431 (Application to Register a Non-Resident Corporation) or Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) with CRA. The BN is the universal tax identification number for federal and provincial corporate tax; a GST/HST account (BN + 'RT' sub-account) is activated in the same CRA application session, and the BN must be quoted on all invoices, GST/HST returns, T2 corporate income tax filings, and customs declarations.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a Canadian Business Corporation is a separate, non-transparent taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act, taxed on worldwide net income at the federal level plus Alberta corporate income tax through the collection agreement. No flow-through or partnership attribution is available to the corporation, and Canada does not provide a U.S.-style check-the-box pass-through election for Canadian-resident corporations.

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box regime for domestic corporations. The statutory elections relevant to an ESG carbon-advisory corporation include: (i) the GST/HST closely-related corporation election (Excise Tax Act s. 156), which treats qualifying group supplies as taxable at the recipient's rate and can eliminate GST on internal services; (ii) GST/HST group registration (ETA s. 273) consolidating compliance for a corporate group; (iii) hybrid-mismatch arrangement de minimis elections (Income Tax Act s. 18.4(3)); (iv) partnership designation elections (ITA s. 96(3)) for any co-owned joint venture; and (v) non-resident branch-tax elections (ITA s. 219). Each election is made on a prescribed CRA form within the applicable deadline and none alters the entity-level classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in total taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) made during the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters — worldwide supplies for a resident corporation and supplies made in Canada for non-residents. Exceeding the threshold makes GST/HST registration mandatory; voluntary registration is available to recover input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under the Excise Tax Act, Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7(d), a supply of services (including corporate Scope 1-3 carbon footprint auditing, verification, and ESG advisory) is zero-rated at 0% GST when made to a non-resident recipient who is not registered for GST/HST, unless the service relates to real property situated in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada, or is otherwise supplied for consumption in Canada. Intangible personal property such as carbon credits and emissions allowances supplied to a non-resident for offshore consumption may also be zero-rated under the applicable Part V provisions, while an offset-brokerage element that qualifies as an exempt financial service under Schedule VI, Part VII is disregarded for GST. Substantiation requires contemporaneous proof of the recipient's non-resident status, non-registration at the supply date, and offshore consumption of the service.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Border Services Agency B13A / CARM electronic export report) for any tangible deliverables; for pure cross-border services, the governing service contract, statement of work, and engagement letter with the non-resident client constitute the primary export record
  • Bill of Lading, airway waybill, or courier/carrier certificate evidencing physical export from Canada for shipped goods or deliverables; electronic delivery logs and server/IP transmission records for digitally delivered ESG audit reports
  • Inward Remittance Certificate, SWIFT MT103, or bank credit confirmation from a Canadian financial institution evidencing receipt of export consideration from the non-resident

Canada imposes no real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, API-based invoice authorization, or digital stamping mandate. E-invoicing is voluntary, and the federal government is exploring non-mandatory Peppol-based exchange standards. GST/HST compliance is enforced through periodic returns (GST/HST NETFILE or EDI, mandatory for larger registrants generally exceeding CAD 1 million in gross annual revenue) and the ETA s. 286 books-and-records obligation, which requires invoices, receipts, and export substantiation to be retained in Canada or made available electronically on CRA audit.

CRA may reverse zero-rated treatment on audit where the export documentation is incomplete or fails contemporaneous substantiation, reassessing at 5% GST with compound interest and disallowed input tax credits where applicable. A knowingly false or grossly negligent zero-rating claim triggers ETA s. 280 civil penalties of 25% of the evaded tax, and conviction under ETA s. 327 carries a fine of 50%-250% of the evaded tax and/or imprisonment up to two years. Parallel income-tax exposure exists under ITA s. 163(2) (50% gross-negligence penalty) and ITA s. 239 (fine of 50%-200% of the tax evaded and/or imprisonment up to five years). Canada imposes no statutory FX-repatriation deadline; the 180-day figure is a best-practice audit-control benchmark, and unrepatriated export proceeds remain taxable in Canadian Part I income regardless of repatriation date.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible on both current and capital accounts. Canada has no exchange control system, does not restrict remittance of profits, dividends, or capital repatriation, and has accepted Article VIII obligations under the International Monetary Fund Articles of Agreement. Profit remittances from an Alberta business corporation are subject to Canadian non-resident withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (typically 25%, reduced under applicable tax treaties), but no exchange-control authorization is required. Administrative hurdles are limited to bank KYC/CDD, source-of-funds documentation, and tax compliance certificates where applicable.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export proceeds surrender requirements, no mandatory conversion ratios, and no designated timeline within which export receipts must be repatriated. Exporters may hold foreign currency balances in Canadian or offshore accounts, convert such funds at commercial rates, or use them for trade settlement. Canadian reporting obligations apply under the PCMLTFA for cross-border electronic funds transfers exceeding CAD 10,000, and exporters must maintain books and records under the Income Tax Act and Excise Tax Act for tax and customs verification.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), operating under the Bank of Canada Act; foreign exchange and cross-border financial transactions are additionally governed by the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), the Currency Act, and relevant regulations administered by FINTRAC and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Low-Medium viability as a primary treasury rail, but acceptable as a payment-services supplement. Canada does not charter standalone electronic money institutions (EMIs) or digital-only banks as deposit-takers without a banking licence under the Bank Act (Schedule I or Schedule II) or an Alberta credit union charter. Offshore EMIs such as Wise or Revolut cannot offer Canadian IBANs, CAD clearing through Lynx, or CDIC-insured deposit accounts. They may operate as Money Services Businesses registered with FINTRAC and provincial authorities, but they are not a substitute for a regulated Canadian operational account. For this ISIC 7020 carbon advisory entity, an EMI could be used for low-value FX conversions, but all escrow and accounted settlements must sit with a Canadian prudentially regulated bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Capital injections from non-resident shareholders or parent companies should be routed through SWIFT MT103 (with MT202 cover if using an intermediary bank) to the Canadian bank's correspondent account, using the beneficiary's corporate operational account. The remittance must include a clear payment reference such as 'Capital Contribution' or 'Loan Disbursement' and reference the underlying share subscription or loan agreement. Canadian banks will require proof of source of funds, a completed corporate resolution, and tax information under the Canada-U.S. Intergovernmental Agreement or FATCA where applicable. There is no official Canadian 'purpose code' regime; instead, the bank will require a narrative purpose and may file a Large Cash Transaction Report or International Electronic Funds Transfer Report with FINTRAC if the transfer exceeds prescribed thresholds. For inbound capital exceeding the Investment Canada Act financial threshold, an application or notification under the Act may trigger, but this does not alter banking routing.Outbound trade disbursements for ESG carbon advisory and offset brokerage should settle primarily through international wire transfer using SWIFT MT103, or through commercial letters of credit for high-value offset purchases requiring payment against presentation of registry certificates. For CAD domestic settlements, the Lynx system (Canada's high-value payment system) provides final real-time settlement through chartered banks. US-dollar settlements can use correspondent accounts; cross-currency payments require clear SWIFT instructions SEPA or Fedwire. All outbound wires should include mandatory reference to the underlying invoice or offset registry serial number. For commercial LCs, the advising and confirming banks must comply with UCP 600. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File mandatory when aggregate covered cross‑border transactions with non‑resident related parties exceed CAD 5 million in a taxation year; Master File required when such transactions exceed CAD 10 million. A T106 (Information Return) must be filed for any non‑arm's‑length cross‑border transaction where the aggregate value is over CAD 1 million (for loans/advances) or CAD 5 million (for other types of transactions).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a stand-alone Economic Substance Regulations statute like the UAE. Instead, the CRA applies the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under section 245, the 'business purpose' test, and the OECD Multilateral Instrument’s Principal Purpose Test (PPT) to disregard shell arrangements and deny treaty benefits. For an ESG carbon advisory corporation, the CRA will scrutinize the existence of a real economic presence in Canada—such as actual office space, salaried employees, board decisions, and operational risk—before accepting claimed income allocation or treaty relief.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Income Tax Act, s.239)

Statutory sanction: On summary conviction: fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and/or imprisonment for up to 2 years. On indictment: fine up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years. Civilly, a 50% gross negligence penalty under s.163(2) applies, plus compound interest on the entire underestimation.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who directed, authorized, or acquiesced in the act are jointly and severally liable for penalties under s.323, and may face criminal prosecution under s.242, including imprisonment, travel bans, and loss of capacity to act as a director.
Transfer Pricing and Documentation Violation (Income Tax Act, s.247)

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess taxable income to an arm's-length price, and a 10% penalty applies to the net upward adjustment if the taxpayer has not made 'reasonable efforts' to comply. Failure to provide documentation within 90 days of a formal request triggers a penalty of CAD 1,000 per month for each person (up to CAD 5,000).

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participated in the mispricing may be assessed directly for the penalty and interest if they are found to be co-conspirators in a tax evasion scheme; in criminal cases, imprisonment up to 5 years.
Shell Company / Treaty Shopping (GAAR and MLI PPT)

Statutory sanction: The CRA may apply GAAR to re-characterize the arrangement, deny treaty benefits, and impose the 25% statutory withholding tax retroactively. A 50% GAAR penalty under s.245(3.1) may apply if the arrangement is considered 'abusive'. Interest accrues from the original due date.

Director personal liability: Directors who were aware of and endorsed the conduit structure may be jointly liable for the GAAR penalty, and in egregious cases, personal criminal exposure for money laundering or tax evasion.
Anti-Money Laundering (PCMLTFA)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: up to CAD 500,000 for a first violation per assessment, and up to CAD 1,000,000 for subsequent violations. Criminal prosecution under s.90 of the PCMLTFA for deliberate non-compliance can lead to imprisonment for up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly aided or failed to take reasonable steps to report while having knowledge are liable for fines and imprisonment; additionally, the CRA may file a director's liability assessment for any unpaid corporate penalty.
Withholding Tax Remittance Failure (Part XIII and s.227.1)

Statutory sanction: A penalty of 10% of the amount not remitted, plus a 20% penalty for a second or later failure within a single year. The CRA may assess the full amount directly against the corporation, plus daily compounding interest.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally and jointly liable under s.227.1 for the entire unremitted amount, interest, and penalties, regardless of corporate dissolution; they can be pursued civilly, subject to a due-diligence defence, and restrictive travel or asset-freezing orders may be issued.
