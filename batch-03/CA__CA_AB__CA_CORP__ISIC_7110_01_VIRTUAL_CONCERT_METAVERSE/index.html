UBO Ultimate Beneficial Owner Screening Rules Official Registry Only

>=25% ownership / control

Applicable AML statute: Federal AML/CFT requirements principally arise under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, SC 2000, c 17 (PCMLTFA), and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Regulations, SOR/2002-184. FINTRAC beneficial-ownership guidance generally identifies each individual who directly or indirectly owns or controls 25% or more of a corporation, or exercises control in fact. A federal corporation governed by the Canada Business Corporations Act, RSC 1985, c C-44, must maintain a register of individuals with significant control under sections 2.1, 21.1 and related provisions; significant control includes ownership or control of 25% or more of voting rights or shares measured by fair market value, or control in fact. An Alberta corporation is governed principally by the Business Corporations Act, RSA 2000, c B-9, and its regulations. Alberta does not operate a generally public, standalone UBO register equivalent to the federal significant-control regime. Reporting entities must nevertheless obtain and verify beneficial-ownership information under the PCMLTFA when applicable.
Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A corporation must maintain a registered office in Alberta if incorporated under the Alberta Business Corporations Act and must maintain the applicable registered records and service address. A commercial virtual office may be acceptable for registry and service purposes if it is a genuine address capable of receiving legal documents and records, but a mere mail-forwarding address, residential accommodation without consent, or unverifiable address creates compliance risk. For virtual concert hosting, digital ticketing, and tokenized payment activity, a virtual office does not replace operational substance, books and records, tax nexus analysis, employment compliance, privacy controls, consumer disclosures, payment-services compliance, or crypto-asset risk controls. Banks, Stripe, FINTRAC-regulated counterparties, tax authorities, and payment processors may require a physical operating address, management evidence, source-of-funds records, website and contractual evidence, and proof of Canadian business activity.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – federal income tax and GST/HST; Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration (TRA) for provincial corporate income tax, which is administered by the CRA under a tax collection agreement. Primary portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services/business-account.html).

E-government filing portal

A newly incorporated Canadian corporation obtains a 9-digit Business Number (BN) automatically via the provincial/territorial corporate registry, which shares data with the CRA. For foreign entities, registration is required: file Form RC4601 (Request for a Business Number and GST/HST Account) or use the Business Registration Online service. The entity must open a GST/HST program account (RT prefix) for indirect tax and a corporate income tax program account (RC prefix) for federal income tax. Non-resident corporations must provide a registered address in Canada and, where applicable, a certificate of non-resident status. There is no separate Alberta provincial registration for corporation tax; the federal BN suffices for both CRA and Alberta TRA.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — A newly incorporated Canadian corporation obtains a 9-digit Business Number (BN) automatically via the provincial/territorial corporate registry, which shares data with the CRA. For foreign entities, registration is required: file Form RC4601 (Request for a Business Number and GST/HST Account) or use the Business Registration Online service. The entity must open a GST/HST program account (RT prefix) for indirect tax and a corporate income tax program account (RC prefix) for federal income tax. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No general check-the-box election exists under Canadian law. Canadian corporations are inherently subject to entity-level taxation. Certain targeted elections are available: (i) an election to deem associated corporations under subsection 256(2) of the Income Tax Act; (ii) a closely related corporation election under section 156 of the Excise Tax Act to treat GST/HST taxable supplies as non-taxable; (iii) a section 216 election for non-residents filing a Canadian tax return for rental or timber royalty income; and (iv) hybrid mismatch rules under sections 18.2 and 18.21 of the Income Tax Act, which may provide limited elections to align tax treatment with treaty partners. No pass-through or disregarded-entity election is available to a Canadian corporation.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (small supplier threshold; measured as total worldwide taxable supplies in a calendar quarter and over the trailing four consecutive calendar quarters). Non-resident suppliers of digital services to Canadian consumers must register if they exceed this threshold, regardless of place of supply; there is no minimum threshold for services provided to Canadian residents through a permanent establishment or for specific digital intermediary services.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of services (including digital virtual concert performance and ticket tokenization) is zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act when supplied to a non-resident person and the service is not performed in Canada for the benefit of a person in Canada, and the service is not in relation to real property, tangible personal property, or goods situated in Canada at the time the supply is made. For virtual concert metaverse services, the supplier must establish the recipient's non-resident status (by reasonable diligence, such as billing address, IP geolocation, or a declaration) and maintain evidence that the service was received outside Canada. Where the service is supplied to a Canadian resident, even via a metaverse platform, the standard 5% GST applies.

  • Commercial invoice or ticket sale record referencing the service description, purchaser identity, and amount in CAD (or equivalent)
  • Evidence of non-resident customer status (e.g., billing address, IP address, credit card / Stripe transaction metadata, cryptocurrency wallet registry data, or non-resident declaration)
  • Payment settlement records from the payment gateway (Stripe) or cryptocurrency exchange/processor (USDC) demonstrating receipt from outside Canada

No government-side real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping exists. Electronic invoicing is permitted, and GST/HST registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 1,500,000 must file returns electronically using GST/HST NETFILE or the CRA's web services. Invoices must contain prescribed information under section 223 of the Excise Tax Act, including the supplier's BN, GST/HST account number, date, description of supply, and tax amounts. There is no requirement to pre-clear e-invoices with a fiscal authority.

The CRA may reassess a GST/HST registrant to deny zero-rating where supporting documentation is incomplete or inconsistent, resulting in an amount of unpaid GST/HST, interest under section 280 of the Excise Tax Act, and a penalty under section 285 of the Excise Tax Act equal to the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the net tax initially assessed. Where false statements or wilful misrepresentations are involved, the normal four-year reassessment period extends to ten years, and additional penalties of up to 50% of the additional tax may apply. Corporate officers may be jointly and severally liable for gross tax and penalties under sections 323 and 325 of the Excise Tax Act.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is an IMF Article VIII member and maintains full current- and capital-account convertibility. There are no prior-approval requirements, exchange-control licences, or limitations on repatriation of equity, dividends, or loan repayments. Profit remittance from Alberta is unrestricted; however, non-resident dividend distributions are subject to Part XIII withholding tax (statutory 25%, generally reduced to 5%-15% under tax treaties such as the Canada-U.S. Convention), and cross-border payments for services or royalties may trigger Part XIII or Part I withholding. All wire transfers are monitored under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) with mandatory reporting at C$10,000 or above per transaction.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline, mandatory conversion ratio, or central-bank surrender obligation for export receipts; Canadian exporters may hold, convert, or reinvest foreign currency freely at commercial interbank rates. The only administrative requirement is balance-of-payments reporting to Statistics Canada, including the Annual Survey of Foreign Direct Investment where cumulative foreign direct investment reaches C$1,000,000, plus standard FINTRAC record-keeping and source-of-funds documentation.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); no separate foreign-exchange administrative agency exists. The Minister of Finance retains residual authority under the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act, while the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) supervises anti-money-laundering compliance for banking and money-services businesses.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium as a supplementary disbursement corridor; Low as a primary treasury bank. Canada does not license 'electronic money institutions' as a distinct category; cross-border fintechs must register as Money Service Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA (mandatory for any dealer in virtual currency such as USDC) or obtain 'Authorized Foreign Bank' (Schedule III) status under the Bank Act. An MSB registration is operational within 30-45 days but confers no deposit-taking status and requires partner-bank custodial arrangements. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$5,000,000 gross revenue threshold for the taxpayer combined with non-resident non-arm's length transactions exceeding C$1,000,000 for goods or C$500,000 for services, intangibles, and interest, triggers mandatory contemporaneous Local File / Master File preparation under CRA Form RC5105.

Economic substance scope: Alberta and federal Canada do not operate a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime, but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s.245) is applied by CRA to disregard transactions lacking genuine commercial substance. For a virtual concert metaverse business, the platform IP holding entity must maintain actual operational management, employees, and board decisions in Alberta to withstand an economic substance challenge; otherwise, it may be re-characterized as a conduit subject reassessment.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statement in T2 Return

Statutory sanction: Civil penalty under ITA s.163(2) equal to 50% of the tax sought to be evaded, plus gross negligence penalties up to 200% of the tax under criminal proceedings. Criminal penalty on summary conviction: a fine of up to C$200,000 and/or imprisonment up to 2 years; on indictment, a fine of up to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors who know of or instigate false accounting may be jointly assessed to the 50% civil penalty under s.163(2). CRA may invoke mutual legal assistance to seize assets and request director travel bans; criminal directors can be extradited under the Canada-CAN MLAT where treaty applies.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may re-price the cross-border transactions at arm's length (ITA s.247(2)). A 10% transfer pricing penalty is imposed under s.247(3) on the amount of the excess over arm's length, unless the taxpayer can demonstrate that reasonable efforts were made. Unpaid tax attracts daily compound interest.

Director personal liability: Under s.247, directors are not expressly liable solely for TP mispricing, but will be personally liable if they caused the corporation to fail to make reasonable efforts and the result is a 10% penalty; in egregious cases, the penalty can be treated as a fraudulent payment requiring director restitution in an Alberta receivership action.
Failure to Withhold / Remit Part XIII or Branch Tax

Statutory sanction: Under ITA s.227(9), the corporation and the non-resident recipient are jointly and severally liable for the unremitted amount. A penalty equal to 10% of the withheld amount not deducted and remitted (or 20% of the amount deducted but not remitted) is imposed, plus interest. Late remittances accrue a 7% penalty plus daily interest.

Director personal liability: Directors are expressly liable under ITA s.227.1 for a corporation's failure to withhold or remit, notwithstanding the corporate veil. CRA can pursue directors personally without naming the corporation, and director travel bans can be ordered to prevent the disposal of assets pending enforcement.
AML Red Flags / Cryptocurrency Receipt Obscurity

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalty (AMP) of up to C$19 million or two years' jail for Regulated Casino/Service groups, and up to 5 years for an indictable offence; all corporate assets tied to the unreported cryptographic receipt are subject to seizure orders; CRA and FINTRAC enforcement actions can cross-report to the Income Tax Act for willful blindness.

Director personal liability: Directors can be held personally liable under provincial securities and AML legislation for failing to implement a sufficient compliance framework; in serious cases, prosecutors may seek travel bans to preserve the criminal jurisdiction, and a court can order restitution as a condition of bail or probation.
