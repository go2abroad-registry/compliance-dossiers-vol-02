Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — an Alberta-incorporated corporation is a resident of Canada under the Income Tax Act; it is taxable on worldwide income under Part I (federal general corporate rate 15% in 2024) plus Alberta corporate income tax (flat 8% administered by CRA). Profits are taxed at the corporate level; distributions to shareholders are taxed again as dividends, with Part XIII non-resident withholding at 25% under the default Canadian rules, treaty-reduced (e.g., generally 5% for a U.S. corporate shareholder owning at least 10% of voting stock under the Canada–U.S. Tax Convention Article X). The entity is not pass-through eligible; no US-style check-the-box can convert the corporation to a branch for Canadian tax purposes. Territorial/PE re-attribution is relevant only to the non-resident owner's foreign tax credit position, not to the corporation's domestic tax status. No Alberta tax-exempt zone or export-processing-zone incentive applies.

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box regime; classification follows legal incorporation and tax residence. Available statutory elections include: (i) ETA s. 156 election between closely related corporations to value intercompany supplies for GST/HST at cost/nil consideration; (ii) ETA s. 82/229(8) quick-method of accounting for small suppliers (optional); (iii) ETA s. 191 and related elections for non-profit/charitable arms; (iv) ITA s. 85 rollover election (Form T2057) for tax-deferred transfer of property into the corporation; (v) ITA s. 89(2)/84(2) paid-up-capital designations for capital restructurings; (vi) OECD Action 2 hybrid-mismatch treatment: Canada released draft hybrid mismatch rules (November 2022 revision) targeting double deduction and deduction/non-inclusion outcomes — the Canadian corporation's equity financing should be documented to avoid payments from or to hybrid issuers or hybrid instruments; no specific check-the-box election exists to override these.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable worldwide supplies (small-supplier threshold, ETA s. 148(1)); mandatory registration under ETA s. 240(1) when exceeded in a single calendar quarter or in four consecutive calendar quarters. A testing laboratory supplying zero-rated export services should register voluntarily at inception to recover input tax credits on equipment, rent, utilities, calibration services, and professional fees.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating (0% GST) is available under ETA s. 23(1) for a supply of a service made to a non-resident person. The s. 23(2)(b) exception, which would otherwise tax a service performed on tangible personal property situated in Canada, is carved back where the property is brought into Canada solely for the performance of the service (e.g., laboratory testing, drop certification, electromagnetic compatibility assessment) and is removed from Canada within a reasonable time after the service is performed. Therefore, UN38.3 battery drop-testing and CE-RED electromagnetic testing supplied to a non-resident manufacturer on lithium cells/modules temporarily imported for certification qualifies for zero-rating provided: (i) the recipient is a non-resident (not a Canadian branch or division), (ii) the batteries are not sold, transferred, or consumed in Canada, and (iii) export occurs after the testing campaign. The lab also zero-rates services supplied to a non-resident when the service does not relate to real property in Canada and is not performed on an individual present in Canada. The corporation remains entitled to full input tax credits on the 5% GST embedded in its Canadian inputs; excess ITCs are recovered through the GST/HST return net-tax calculation, generating a refund or reducing future remittances. Care: a deliverable sent to a non-resident office of a Canadian-controlled multinational will be challenged; maintain a written service agreement, export schedule, and non-resident identity file.

  • CBSA Canada Export Declaration (B13/CAED) or ACI e-manifest/e-export declaration reference showing the exporter's Business Number, batteries description, quantity, and destination outside Canada (retain the declaration number and date of export).
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical departure of the tested goods from Canada (ocean bill with port of export, or HAWB with airport of departure), matched to the testing job reference.
  • Signed Commercial Invoice / Testing and Certification Service Agreement identifying the non-resident client, the testing service description, the consideration, and the place of performance (Alberta laboratory).
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or SWIFT bank credit advice from the corporation's Canadian bank evidencing remittance from the non-resident client's bank account (including FX conversion reference and invoice number).
  • Customs temporary importation entry (Form B3 / CARM recall) showing the lithium cells entered Canada for testing/certification and were subsequently re-exported, plus customs broker's export certificate where applicable.
  • Certificate of Analysis / UN38.3 Test Report (T1 altitude, T2 thermal, T3 vibration, T4 shock, T5 external short circuit, T6 impact/crush, T7 overcharge, T8 forced discharge) referencing the shipment, which proves that the supply is of testing services rather than goods.

No real-time fiscal clearance, tax-authority pre-approval, or digital stamping mandate exists under Canadian law (unlike LATAM regimes). GST/HST returns are filed electronically via GST/HST NETFILE or CRA API; CBSA customs clearance operates through the CARM system with real-time assessment and API connectivity for import/export declarations. The Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Initiative has adopted the PEPPOL BIS 3.0 standard and is phasing in e-invoicing for purchases by federal departments from fiscal year 2024/2025 onward; private-sector suppliers (including testing laboratories) should be ready to issue PEPPOL-conformant e-invoices to federal clients. CRA retains the right to demand original vendor invoice data; retain PDF/XML, digital certificates, and timestamped records for six years.

CRA's export-service audit will test the non-resident recipient status, the physical location of the batteries at the time of service, the timing and route of the export, and the identity/creditworthiness of the payer. If zero-rating is disallowed, the laboratory is assessed 5% GST on the consideration plus interest under ETA s. 280 (compounded daily from the due date of the return in which the supply was reported), and the ITCs claimed on inputs attributable to that supply are denied. False-statement penalties under ETA s. 280.1 amount to the greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the net tax attributable to the misstatement (50% if made knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence). Where the laboratory or its officers make false statements or omit material facts with intent to evade, CRA may reassess beyond the ordinary four-year limitation period under ETA s. 298(6), and the offence provisions of ETA s. 327 apply: summary conviction fines up to CAD 25,000 and/or six months' imprisonment, or on indictment fines up to CAD 100,000 and/or two years' imprisonment, for each wilful evasion. Directors may become jointly and severally liable for the corporation's unremitted GST under ETA s. 323 subject to the due-diligence defence. The prudent defence is a contemporaneous export-compliance file, centralized bank-remittance mapping, and an annual GST/HST health check.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian Dollar (CAD) is freely convertible on both current and capital accounts. Canada maintains no general exchange controls under the Currency Act, the Bank of Canada Act, or the Financial Administration Act. Current-account payments for services, dividends, royalties, and interest are generally unrestricted. Capital-account inflows (share subscriptions, shareholder loans) and outflows (capital repatriation) are permitted without prior regulatory authorization. The operative hurdles are fiscal and documentary, not currency-convertibility barriers. Profit remittance requires compliance with CRA Part XIII withholding tax (default 25% on dividends, commonly reduced under tax treaties, e.g., to 5% for substantial corporate shareholders under the Canada-U.S. treaty), and outbound wire transfers of CAD 10,000 or more trigger cross-border electronic funds transfer (EFT) reporting obligations for financial institutions under FINTRAC rules. In addition, any transfer of more than CAD 10,000 in physically transported currency or monetary instruments must be declared to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA).

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender or mandatory conversion requirements. There is no requirement to repatriate foreign currency receipts into CAD or to convert invoiced proceeds within a prescribed timeline. Exporters may retain balances in foreign-currency bank accounts, maintain multi-currency treasury positions, and freely use such proceeds for offshore working capital or trade settlements. The applicable compliance duties are informational: accurate B13 export declarations to CBSA and Statistics Canada, FinCEN/FINTRAC wire reporting thresholds, and normal CRA income inclusion and Part XIII withholding rules when distributing earnings to shareholders.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC) as the central monetary authority; prudential banking oversight by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); cross-border payments and anti-money laundering surveillance by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium for payment-rail use; Low to Non-Viable for capital escrow or treasury concentrations. Wise (Wise Payments Canada Inc.) operates in Canada as a registered Money Service Business (MSB) with FINTRAC and holds provincial MSB registration, but it is not a Schedule I bank and does not have Canadian Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC) deposit insurance. It may efficiently execute small and medium cross-border disbursements using local clearing rails (Canada LVTS, US Fedwire/ACH, SEPA, SWIFT) but cannot provide loan facilities, escrow custody, or regulated deposit-taking. Foreign EMIs (e.g., EU/UK e-money institutions) must either register as Canadian MSBs, partner with a licensed Canadian MSB, or use a Canadian Schedule I bank as sponsor. Under provincial money-services statutes and FINTRAC expectations, an offshore EMI without local MSB registration may not open a settled account or accept funds on behalf of the Alberta entity.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal plus 8% Alberta provincial tax. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access an 11% combined small-business rate: 9% federal plus 2% Alberta, subject to the applicable business-limit, associated-corporation, passive-income, and eligibility rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The Canadian domestic withholding tax rate on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is generally 25%. A treaty may reduce the rate, commonly to 5% for a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting ownership and limitation-on-benefits conditions, or 15% for other qualifying portfolio dividends. The Canada-U.S. treaty commonly provides 5% for a qualifying 10% corporate shareholder and 15% otherwise. The payer must withhold, remit, and report using Form NR4.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due within six months after the end of every taxation year. Any corporate tax balance is generally due two months after year-end. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally pay the balance three months after year-end if the statutory conditions are satisfied, including the applicable small-business income and taxable-capital conditions. Installments are due monthly or quarterly under the applicable Income Tax Act rules.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Generally the same six-month post-year-end filing rule applies through the T2 process. Alberta provincial tax installments and balances follow the applicable federal-provincial corporate tax rules.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is optional under Income Tax Act section 247(4). A Canadian taxpayer must maintain contemporaneous documentation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions sufficient to establish the arm's-length price and terms. Form T106 commonly applies when reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1 million in the relevant reporting framework. Country-by-Country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million, or the equivalent statutory threshold. Canada does not impose a universal OECD-style Master File or Local File filing threshold; documentation requirements are transaction-based and audit-driven.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a separate generic Economic Substance Regulation regime equivalent to the regimes of certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is nevertheless relevant under the Income Tax Act, transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership analysis, principal-purpose tests where applicable, and the sham and factual-recharacterization doctrines. A testing laboratory should maintain Canadian operational substance proportionate to its claimed activities, including qualified personnel, laboratory premises, calibrated equipment, customer contracts, risk control, decision-making, accounting records, intellectual-property functions, and evidence that services were actually performed. Contractual labels do not override the actual functions, assets, and risks.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment of tax, interest, gross-negligence penalty generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, denial of deductions and credits, and possible criminal prosecution. Income Tax Act tax evasion convictions may result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and persons exercising control may face criminal prosecution, fines, imprisonment, personal assessment for certain statutory remittances, and civil recovery. Canadian law does not impose automatic personal liability for ordinary corporate income tax solely by holding office, and veil piercing is exceptional; it may arise from fraud, sham conduct, abuse of the corporation, or statutory director-liability provisions.
Failure to Remit Withholding Tax

Statutory sanction: The corporation may be assessed for the unremitted amount, interest, and applicable remittance penalties. Repeated or deliberate non-compliance may trigger prosecution and broader audit exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable under the Income Tax Act for unremitted source deductions, subject to the statutory due-diligence defence and limitation period. Similar statutory exposure may apply to GST/HST amounts under the Excise Tax Act.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust income under section 247, deny or recharacterize deductions, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, and assess interest. Secondary adjustments and treaty competent-authority procedures may become relevant.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable for a transfer-pricing adjustment merely because they approved a transaction. Personal exposure can arise from knowing participation in fraud, false records, obstruction, or statutory remittance failures.
Shell Company, Sham, or Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits, withholding-tax reassessment, transfer-pricing adjustment, GAAR reassessment, interest, penalties, and possible sham or tax-evasion prosecution. The corporation may lose access to small-business deductions and incentive claims.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face civil claims, tax recovery where statutory provisions apply, disqualification or regulatory consequences, criminal fines, and imprisonment if they knowingly participated in fraudulent or evasive conduct. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax sanction but may arise from criminal process, bail conditions, arrest warrants, or immigration consequences.
GST/HST and Payroll Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Assessment of tax, denied input tax credits, interest, late-filing and late-remittance penalties, gross-negligence penalties, and potential prosecution for serious or deliberate conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for unremitted payroll source deductions and GST/HST under statutory director-liability rules, subject to due diligence and limitation rules.
AML, Sanctions, and Suspicious Payment Activity

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance examinations, reporting orders, publication of violations, criminal prosecution for laundering proceeds of crime, and sanctions-related penalties. Proceeds-of-crime offences may carry substantial fines and imprisonment, including imprisonment of up to ten years for certain indictable offences.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal liability for knowing participation, authorization, counselling, or wilful blindness. Assets may be restrained or forfeited. Travel restrictions may arise from criminal proceedings or bail conditions, but are not an automatic consequence of an ordinary FINTRAC administrative penalty.
Laboratory Licensing, Accreditation, and Product-Safety Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Regulatory enforcement, suspension or revocation of accreditation or licenses, contract damages, injunctions, consumer-protection penalties, dangerous-goods enforcement, product-liability claims, and possible criminal charges for fraud or false documents. The precise sanction depends on the represented certification, federal statute, provincial law, accreditation body, and customer jurisdiction.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers may face personal criminal or civil exposure for knowing approval of false reports, negligent professional conduct, fraud, or statutory offences. Corporate limited liability does not protect deliberate personal torts or criminal acts.
Corporate Records and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: Corporate-law penalties, refusal or delay in maintaining corporate status, fines, tax reassessment, audit assumptions adverse to the corporation, and possible offences for knowingly providing false corporate information.

Director personal liability: Directors may face statutory fines and personal prosecution for knowingly false records or failure to maintain required registers. Shareholder claims and derivative actions may also arise where record failures prejudice the corporation.
