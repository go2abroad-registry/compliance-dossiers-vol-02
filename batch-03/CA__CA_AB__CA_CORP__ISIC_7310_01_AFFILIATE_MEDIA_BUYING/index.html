Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) – National portal: CRA My Business Account (MyBA) at canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html

E-government filing portal

Foreign entities not otherwise required to obtain a Canadian TIN must apply for a CRA Business Number (BN/RT) via the CRA Business Registration Online service or by paper form RC1. For GST/HST purposes, the BN is appended with the 'RT' program account. Non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada through a permanent establishment or deriving Canadian-source income must register for income tax under the BN and file T2 returns. The procedure requires proof of incorporation, Canadian address for service (if applicable), and identification of the Canadian agent or permanent establishment contact. Online registration is immediate for most non-resident corporate applicants without a compliance hold; otherwise, mailing to the applicable CRA tax centre with supporting documents is accepted. A separate provincial registration for Alberta Corporate Tax is not generally required when the federal BN is used for Alberta corporate income tax purposes, but a provincial corporate tax account (CRA administered for Alberta) is established automatically via the T2 corporate income tax registration process.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation. A Canadian Business Corporation (federal or Alberta provincial) is a separate legal person and is a resident corporation if incorporated in Canada under the Income Tax Act (ITA). It is subject to Part I tax on worldwide income, unless an exception for non-resident corporations applies. The default classification under the ITA is a taxable corporation, not a pass-through entity, and there is no general check-the-box regime analogous to the US. Alberta does not levy a separate provincial corporate income tax beyond the federal-provincial integrated tax collection: under the Alberta Corporate Tax Act, Alberta uses a single flat rate (currently 8%) applied to taxable income allocated to Alberta, collected by the CRA under the Tax Collection Agreements. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. A Canadian Business Corporation (federal or Alberta provincial) is a separate legal person and is a resident corporation if incorporated in Canada under the Income Tax Act (ITA). It is subject to Part I tax on worldwide income, unless an exception for non-resident corporations applies. The default classification under the ITA is a taxable corporation, not a pass-through entity, and there is no general check-the-box regime analogous to the US. Alberta does not levy a separate provincial corporate income tax beyond the federal-provincial integrated tax collection: under the Alberta Corporate Tax Act, Alberta uses a single flat rate (currently 8%) applied to taxable income allocated to Alberta, collected by the CRA under the Tax Collection Agreements. Territorial scope for a Canadian-incorporated company is full residence-based taxation; Alberta allocation rules apply for corporate income where a permanent establishment exists in Alberta.

Available election forms

No check-the-box classification election is available for federal or Alberta corporate tax purposes. The only entity classification election applicable to corporations is under the 'Canadian-controlled private corporation' (CCPC) status, which arises statutorily if conditions are met (all shares owned by Canadian residents and no public corporation status). There is no hybrid mismatch election for ordinary corporations; however, for cross-border tax planning, an election may be made under subsection 216(1) ITA for a non-resident corporation’s rental income, or under section 216.1 for property gains, and certain treaty-based returns (section 115, 116 and 128.1) require specific filings. In the context of business corporations, an election under section 89 ITA or the capital dividend account may be utilized, but this does not alter entity classification for income tax. For GST/HST purposes, a non-resident corporation may elect to use a branch office registration instead of establishing a Canadian permanent establishment under the Simplified GST/HST Registration regime for non-residents (option to rebate GST/HST on certain inputs), but this does not change the default corporate income tax classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

General GST/HST registration threshold is CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies per calendar quarter (i.e., over 4 consecutive calendar quarters exceeding CAD 30,000). For non-resident persons with no permanent establishment in Canada, the threshold is CAD 30,000 unless the person is a 'registrant' by election or supplies 'advertising services' or 'digital goods/services' to Canadian recipients. Special mandatory registration rules apply to non-resident suppliers of 'digital services' (GST/HST on e-commerce, effective July 1, 2021) and to suppliers operating a digital platform, where the threshold is CAD 30,000 of gross revenue from Canadian-sourced digital supplies. For the affiliate media-buying sector, if the Canadian counterparty is a registered business, the supplier may be zero-rated under the 'non-resident supplier providing services to a Canadian recipient who is a GST/HST registrant' rule (section 179 of ETA), provided the service is exclusively for supply to a registered recipient. Otherwise, the 5% GST must be charged.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

For the affiliate media-buying (ISIC 7310, NAICS 541810) model, zero-rating applies to supplies of services or intangible personal property (including advertising services) exported to non-resident persons if the supply is for use or consumption exclusively outside Canada. Under section 7 of Part V of Schedule VI to the ETA, a supply of a service made by a Canadian entity to a non-resident individual or to a non-resident corporation (that is not a Canadian resident) is zero-rated, provided that the service is not a supply of an exemption listed (e.g., advice, consultation, or some services relating to land in Canada). Advertising services, including digital media buying, affiliate lead generation, and programmatic advertising, are zero-rated under item 7 of Part V if the recipient is a non-resident and the service is not supplied to a Canadian resident or a Canadian branch. Additionally, if the Canadian corporation provides services to a foreign affiliate or non-resident counterpart, the supplier must maintain evidence that the recipient is non-resident and that the service is not 'in connection with real estate situated in Canada' nor 'performed in Canada' under certain deemed supply rules. Where an intermediary Canadian corporation purchases media inventory on behalf of foreign clients, careful documentation is required to demonstrate the supply is made in Canada for zero-rating, as the CRA may treat the service as being a supply to a Canadian-resident advertiser if the Canadian intermediary agrees to indemnify or assume liability. Zero-rating does not require a refund; it allows the supplier to claim input tax credits on inputs without charging output tax. Exporters may claim GST/HST refunds for input tax credits in excess of output tax via the regular ITC refund claim in the GST/HST return or through the Non-Resident Rebate program if applicable. The CRA Form GST189 (General Application for GST/HST Rebate) or standard return must be filed.

Canada does not currently operate a mandatory nationwide electronic invoicing clearance system or real-time fiscal stamping for GST/HST. The CRA accepts electronic invoices and records if they meet the ETA’s prescribed information requirements (set out in the GST/HST Memorandum 8-7 and ETA Regulations). There is no requirement for pre-clearance of invoices by the CRA. However, since 2020, for GST/HST e-commerce rules, platform operators must maintain detailed records of Canadian suppliers’ transactions and may be required to report information to the CRA through the GST/HST E-Commerce Forms (RC5213, RC5214). For large businesses, mandatory electronic filing of GST/HST returns and T2 corporate income tax returns is required if the corporation has gross revenue exceeding CAD 1,000,000 or is a corporate entity required to file electronically. All GST/HST returns generally must be filed and paid electronically using CRA’s GST/HST NETFILE or a registered E-file transmitter; payment may be made by wire transfer or online. The CRA has announced a gradual adoption of Peppol-based e-invoicing standards starting with public sector procurement, but no statutory mandate exists for private B2B transactions in Alberta as of the knowledge cutoff. Digital stamping or real-time clearance is not applicable; instead, the CRA relies on audit-based disclosure of business documents.

The CRA may audit export zero-rating claims for a period of up to four years (and in cases of neglect, misrepresentation, or fraud, an unlimited period). If the CRA determines that the export zero-rating was claimed in error (e.g., the service was actually consumed in Canada, the recipient was a Canadian resident, the documentation is insufficient, or the supply was not zero-rated due to a subsection 2.1 deemed supply), the CRA will retroactively assess the supplier for uncollected GST/HST, interest, and penalties. Penalties under the ETA include: (i) any person who fails to charge GST/HST when required is liable to pay the uncollected tax; (ii) failure to register may result in a penalty of up to CAD 10,000 (non-resident) or a general penalty of CAD 500 plus late-filing penalties; (iii) knowingly making a false or misleading statement in a return (including a zero-rating claim) is subject to a penalty of 50% of the amount of tax sought to be evaded, plus interest under sections 280 and 285 ETA; (iv) late or deficient remittances accrue daily interest at the prescribed rate plus a late-filing penalty of 1% per month up to 12 months on the unpaid tax. In addition, the Income Tax Act may impose penalties for false statements in corporate returns leading to additional assessments and, in egregious cases, criminal liability for tax evasion. For an affiliate media buying entity, clawback risk is heightened if the CRA determines the business is acting as a principal reselling advertising inventory to a Canadian advertiser through a non-resident affiliate, thereby failing the 'direct' zero-rating test; in such cases, the entire historical GST output tax may be assessed plus interest and penalties. The CRA’s GST/HST Audit Manual directs for export substantiation: copies of contracts, invoices, proof of payment, and proof of the non-resident status of the recipient, and a failure to maintain such books is itself an offense with a penalty of CAD 10,000 under subsection 283.1.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully convertible currency for both current and capital account transactions, with the Canadian dollar floating freely. No exchange control permit or central bank authorization is required for current account payments, cross-border dividends, management fees, or capital repatriation. However, payments must be supported by contemporaneous commercial documentation, comply with AML/KYC expectations of the receiving and intermediary banks, and satisfy applicable tax withholdings (e.g., Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on dividends, interest, and certain payments). Profit remittance is unrestricted but bank compliance may request documentary evidence of underlying transaction legitimacy, invoicing, and transfer pricing consistency for intra-group payments.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory surrender requirements, mandatory conversion ratios, or repatriation timelines for export receipts in Canada. Foreign currency may be retained in domestic or offshore accounts, but where export proceeds are received into a Canadian corporate bank account, the corporate must ensure accurate bookkeeping, FX valuation for tax reporting, and FINTRAC large-dollar reporting thresholds (e.g., $10,000 or equivalent in certain transaction contexts). Non-resident-owned corporations and Canadian corporations with foreign accounts must consider T1135 reporting obligations for specified foreign property if applicable.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for currency-related transactional oversight; Canada imposes no central-bank exchange control approval regime.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Moderate-to-High feasibility for licensed and regulated cross-border payment fintechs such as Wise Platform, Airwallex, and Payoneer, provided they hold applicable money service business (MSB) licenses under Canadian provincial regimes or are operating with a registered foreign MSB in Canada. Canadian fintechs may be used as non-bank settlement vehicles, but cannot substitute for a domestic Schedule I bank account where the corporation requires an escrow-capable custody layer, direct access to Canadian clearing infrastructure, or CAD settlement credibility with counterparties. Offshore EMIs (e.g., U.K. EMIs, EU payment institutions) are viable only as supplemental treasury tools for sub-account management and FX conversion; they should not hold operating capital for media buy settlements due to FINTRAC registration concerns, weaker lender-of-last-resort protection, and lack of CDIC deposit insurance. Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant injection of capital into the Alberta corporation should be executed via SWIFT international wire transfers (MT103) or SWIFT GPI instructions from the shareholder's bank to the Canadian operational or capital escrow account. Remittance instructions must include the corporate legal name, account number, reference to shareholder's name, and a purpose description such as 'Contribution to capital under Canada Business Corporations Act' or 'Subordinated shareholder loan for working capital'. Canadian banks do not require standardized purpose codes, but the outward remitting bank will apply an ISO 20022 purpose code (e.g., COMC for corporate contribution, FIN5 for financial assistance, MINE/CNF for cross-border media and advertising services). The receiving bank will ask for the inward remittance advice and may require a sworn capital contribution declaration or legal opinion when the share subscription involves non-resident shareholders. All injections should be reconciled against corporate records, and inbound cross-border amounts above normal thresholds will trigger enhanced AML review; therefore the corporation should pre-advise its relationship manager and provide funding-source documentation before the wire.For media buy settlements and affiliate payouts, the primary clearing rails are: (i) domestic Canadian wire via the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) for CAD-denominated payments; (ii) SWIFT MT103 international wire for cross-border USD, CAD, GBP, and EUR payments to ad networks, publisher partners, and media exchanges; (iii) Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS) via EFT/ACH for low-value recurring domestic payments; and (iv) commercial letters of credit for any supplier requiring payment security, though LCs are less common for performance media since the underlying service is intangible and not a documentary trade. The outbound treasury policy should bilaterally net confirmed media liabilities and maintain segregated disbursement accounts to reduce wire counts. For SWIFT MT103, use standard field 70 (Remittance Information) with invoice or contract reference, and field 50A/K for initiating account. Where possible, use SWIFT GPI to ensure same-day tracking. Offshore EMIs (e.g., U.K. EMIs, EU payment institutions) are viable only as supplemental treasury tools for sub-account management and FX conversion; they should not hold operating capital for media buy settlements due to FINTRAC registration concerns, weaker lender-of-last-resort protection, and lack of CDIC deposit insurance.
