Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) is the national tax authority; federal corporate income tax and Alberta corporate income tax are administered jointly by CRA under the Alberta Tax Collection Agreement. Official portals: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services/business-login.html) and Business Registration Online (BRO). Alberta Treasury Board and Finance remains competent for non-income provincial levies (e.g., fuel tax, tourism levy) but plays no role in corporate income tax or GST administration.

E-government filing portal

1) Incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA) or the Alberta Business Corporations Act (ABCA); if federally incorporated, file extra-provincial registration with the Alberta Corporate Registry. 2) Obtain the statutory tax identifier - the 9-digit Business Number (BN) - through CRA Business Registration Online or Form RC1; the BN is the single TIN for federal corporate tax, Alberta corporate tax, and GST. 3) Activate program accounts under the same BN: RT for GST/HST, RC for corporate income tax, RP for payroll (if Canadian employees/contractors are engaged). 4) No minimum capital deposit is required. 5) Non-resident ownership is permitted without advance tax approval; a Canadian resident officer or Alberta extra-provincial registered office may be required. 6) File the annual T2 corporate income tax return; Alberta corporate income tax is reported on the same T2 through CRA, eliminating separate provincial filing. 7) Retain the certificate of incorporation and CRA confirmation of BN activation as core tax registration evidence.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: The Canadian Business Corporation is a separate taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act (ITA) and has no pass-through eligibility. Territorial scope: Canadian-resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income; Alberta imposes no separate capital tax and offers no tax-exempt zones or special economic zones. Combined general corporate rate: 23% (federal 15% + Alberta 8%). If the entity qualifies as a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC), active business income up to CAD 500,000 is taxed at federal 9% + Alberta 2%, subject to the business limit allocation rules under ITA s. 125.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide an IRS-style check-the-box election. Statutory mechanisms relevant to this entity: (a) ITA s. 125(7) CCPC status election and associated business-limit allocation; (b) ETA s. 191 election to waive zero-rating on export supplies (permits tax to be charged on otherwise zero-rated services); (c) ETA s. 273 joint venture election for co-venturers; (d) ETA ss. 235-238 filing-frequency elections for GST/HST (monthly, quarterly, or annual); (e) ITA ss. 216/217 elections for non-resident withholding (non-core for an operating MCN agency); (f) ITA s. 219 branch-tax election if the corporation were held as a non-resident branch (inapplicable to a Canadian-resident corporation). Hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.4-18.6) apply mandatorily without elective opt-out; the structure must avoid deductible/creditable mismatches on intercompany payments.

Standard VAT / GST rate

0.05%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 - GST/HST registration becomes mandatory once the worldwide taxable supply revenue (excluding sales of capital property) exceeds the small-supplier threshold in a single calendar quarter or across the preceding four consecutive quarters; voluntary registration is available below the threshold. For creator-MCN services, place-of-supply rules under ETA s. 142 determine whether management and representation services are treated as supplied in Canada or Alberta.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Creator-management and MCN agency services supplied by the Alberta corporation to TikTok (a non-resident person) qualify for 0% GST under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 9, provided: (i) the agency service is supplied to a non-resident recipient; (ii) the service is not supplied in connection with real property situated in Canada; (iii) the service is not rendered to an individual present in Canada at the time of performance - agency services to TikTok as a corporate entity satisfy this test; and (iv) contemporaneous evidence of the recipient's non-resident status is retained. Advertising services are similarly zero-rated under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7. The zero-rated exporter must file GST/HST returns to claim full input tax credits (ITCs) on domestic purchases; service-export zero-rating does not rely on a customs export declaration for intangible or service supplies.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A via G7 or CAED) - required only if tangible deliverables accompany the service supply
  • Bill of Lading - or equivalent digital delivery record, server-location log, or written service completion report for pure service exports
  • Inward Remittance Certificate - or Swift MT103 / Wise B2B settlement record evidencing foreign-currency remittance from TikTok
  • Signed MCN Agency Contract and per-creator remittance schedule
  • Non-Resident Status Declaration from the TikTok legal entity

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, API stamping, or digital invoicing mandate exists in Canada or Alberta as of 2025. CRA accepts EDI, PDF, and electronic records; invoices must be retained for six years under ETA s. 286. The Pan-Canadian e-invoicing framework (Peppol/ISO 20022) is voluntary. GST/HST returns are filed electronically via GST/HST NETFILE or CRA My Business Account. Non-resident platform operator rules (ETA s. 211.19) do not alter the standard GST registration obligations of a Canadian-resident supplier.

CRA may reassess zero-rated export claims within the normal 4-year assessment period, extended to 7 years for misrepresentation or negligence and unlimited for fraud. False or unsupported export zero-rating claims attract a penalty of 50% of the tax evaded under ETA s. 281.1, and gross-negligence penalties of 50% of the understated tax under ITA s. 163(2). Criminal evasion prosecutions under ETA ss. 327/328 carry fines up to CAD 25,000 and imprisonment. Clawbacks include disallowance of the zero-rating, reclassification of fees as taxable supplies, compound interest at the prescribed rate, and adjustment of corporate income. Although no federal statute imposes a hard 180-day foreign-currency repatriation deadline on service receivables, CRA tests commercial reasonableness of foreign receivables under ITA s. 17 (deemed interest on overdue amounts owed by non-residents) and s. 247 transfer pricing rules; the 180-day benchmark should therefore be contractually enforced.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible for both current account and capital account transactions. Canada maintains no exchange controls under the Currency Act, and the Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951. There are no licensing, registration, or approval requirements for CAD conversion, no restrictions on profit remittance, dividends, or service-export proceeds, and no capital repatriation license. The only substantive hurdle for non-resident shareholders is tax-side: Part XIII withholding tax applies to dividend remittances at 25% statutory rate, reduced to 10% under the Canada-China Tax Agreement and to 5%/15% under the Canada-US Tax Convention depending on ownership thresholds; CRA Form NR4 reporting applies. Cross-border electronic fund transfers of C$10,000 or more are reportable to FINTRAC. No mandatory conversion ratio or surrender timetable applies; the Alberta MCN entity may hold and settle in USD, HKD, or EUR foreign-currency accounts at Canadian Schedule I banks without statutory conversion obligations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender rule exists in Canada. Export proceeds, including creator-management service fees and MCN agency revenues from TikTok, are subject to zero mandatory conversion and no deadline for repatriation, surrender, or deposit. The Company may maintain USD/HKD settlement balances without converting to CAD. Banks internally may convert incoming foreign-currency wire payments at prevailing interbank rates if credited to a CAD-denominated operating account; this is a commercial practice, not a regulatory mandate. Ongoing procedural obligations are limited to FINTRAC reporting of cross-border EFTs above C$10,000, CRA record-keeping, and, where applicable, Form T1134 (foreign affiliate) and Form T106 (non-arm's length cross-border transactions) filing.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) under the Bank of Canada Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. B-2); federal banking supervision is delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/CFT supervision is administered by FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); retail payment activity supervision is conducted by the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), in force since November 1, 2024.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

High viability, but strictly as a disbursement rail and low-value treasury overlay; not acceptable as the primary capital escrow or statutory deposit. Wise, Payoneer, and Stripe operate in Canada as money services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC, and those offering retail payment services are subject to registration under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) with the Bank of Canada. These entities are not banks, are not CDIC-insured, and their client funds are typically held in segregated trust accounts at a Canadian Schedule I bank. Wise B2B is suitable for TikTok creator payouts in USD/CAD and for supplier remittances, with batch payout API integration. Legal constraints include: (i) EMIs cannot accept deposits as defined under the Bank Act; (ii) no Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation protection; (iii) the entity must verify that the EMI is RPAA-registered; (iv) contractual terms must provide for chain-of-custody of funds and audit rights; and (v) monthly throughput should remain below CAD 100,000 until bank-grade treasury controls are established. All fintech rails must be linked to the primary chartered bank account as downstream clearing nodes, not as treasury concentration points.

FX conversion for outbound USD, HKD, and EUR settlements is executed by the relationship bank's treasury desk at negotiated interbank rates and recorded on a deal ticket. Each disbursement file must contain the executed creator agreement and TikTok payout reconciliation records to support FINTRAC large-value EFT reporting, which aggregates transfers of C$10,000 or more to the same beneficiary within a 24-hour period. Multi-currency notional pooling or zero-balance account sweeps may be implemented with RBC, TD, or CWB to centralize global liquidity.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: High viability, but strictly as a disbursement rail and low-value treasury overlay; not acceptable as the primary capital escrow or statutory deposit. Wise, Payoneer, and Stripe operate in Canada as money services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC, and those offering retail payment services are subject to registration under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) with the Bank of Canada. These entities are not banks, are not CDIC-insured, and their client funds are typically held in segregated trust accounts at a Canadian Schedule I bank. Wise B2B is suitable for TikTok creator payouts in USD/CAD and for supplier remittances, with batch payout API integration. Legal constraints include: (i) EMIs cannot accept deposits as defined under the Bank Act; (ii) no Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation protection; (iii) the entity must verify that the EMI is RPAA-registered; (iv) contractual terms must provide for chain-of-custody of funds and audit rights; and (v) monthly throughput should remain below CAD 100,000 until bank-grade treasury controls are established. All fintech rails must be linked to the primary chartered bank account as downstream clearing nodes, not as treasury concentration points.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined federal and Alberta general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal plus 8% Alberta. Qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations may generally access an 11% combined small-business rate on eligible active business income within the applicable CAD 500,000 business limit, subject to statutory restrictions and grind rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The Canadian domestic Part XIII withholding rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is 25%. An applicable tax treaty commonly reduces the rate to 5% for a qualifying corporate recipient meeting ownership conditions and to 15% in other treaty cases, although the exact rate depends on the recipient's residence, ownership, limitation-on-benefits provisions, and beneficial-ownership status.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no formal statutory Local File or Master File threshold equivalent to jurisdictions that prescribe those documents by transaction value. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires contemporaneous documentation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions sufficient to establish the terms and conditions and the arm's-length basis. A corporation with gross revenue below CAD 10 million generally benefits from the statutory documentation exception for transfer-pricing penalties, subject to the detailed legislation and transaction requirements; this does not eliminate the arm's-length requirement. Form T106 generally applies where the aggregate reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1 million, subject to statutory exclusions and filing rules. Large multinational groups may also have Country-by-Country Reporting obligations when consolidated group revenue reaches the applicable EUR 750 million threshold.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a standalone statutory Economic Substance Regulation regime of the general type found in certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance remains relevant through corporate residence and central-management-and-control principles, treaty beneficial-ownership requirements, permanent-establishment analysis, the general anti-avoidance rule, foreign-affiliate and foreign accrual property income rules, thin-capitalization rules, interest-limitation rules, and transfer-pricing requirements. A Canadian corporation should maintain real decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational capacity, commercial contracts, bank-account control, records, and risk management consistent with its claimed functions and remuneration.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate tax evasion and fraudulent filing

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, conviction for tax evasion may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to two years on summary conviction. Indictable offences may attract a fine generally ranging from 100% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years. Tax, interest, civil penalties, and reassessment remain payable separately.

Director personal liability: The corporation is ordinarily a separate taxpayer, but directors may face criminal prosecution for personal participation, aiding, abetting, counselling, or directing the offence. Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions under section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act and for certain GST/HST amounts under the Excise Tax Act, subject to statutory limitation periods and the due-diligence defence. Civil restitution and asset-recovery orders may apply; travel restrictions are not automatic but may arise from bail, probation, forfeiture, or judicial orders.
Failure to remit payroll, withholding, or GST/HST amounts

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unremitted amount, arrears interest, late-remittance penalties, gross-negligence penalties where applicable, and potential criminal prosecution. CRA collection measures may include liens, garnishment, seizure, and director assessments.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally assessed for certain unremitted source deductions and GST/HST where statutory conditions are met. A due-diligence defence may apply if the director exercised the care, diligence, and skill of a reasonably prudent person. Personal liability does not arise merely from holding office, but resignation does not automatically eliminate liability for amounts arising during the directorship.
Transfer-pricing manipulation and profit shifting

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under section 247, deny or adjust deductions, reallocate income, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the reassessed amount where statutory conditions are met, and charge interest. Secondary adjustments and withholding-tax consequences may arise.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil exposure for knowingly approving unlawful transactions, breach-of-duty claims, shareholder claims, or personal criminal liability where fraud is involved. Transfer-pricing reassessments ordinarily remain corporate liabilities unless a statutory director-liability provision or personal participation in an offence applies.
Treaty abuse, beneficial-ownership failure, or conduit arrangement

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty relief, withholding-tax reassessment to the 25% domestic rate, arrears interest, penalties for failure to withhold or remit, and possible application of the general anti-avoidance rule. The payer may remain liable for tax that should have been withheld.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally exposed where they knowingly participated in false certifications or deliberate withholding failures. Criminal liability requires proof of the applicable offence and culpable conduct; corporate separateness is not a defence to personal fraud or intentional participation.
Shell company, nominee ownership, and beneficial-ownership concealment

Statutory sanction: Corporate-register, tax, AML, and criminal consequences may apply, including administrative penalties, reassessment, forfeiture, proceeds-of-crime orders, and prosecution for fraud, tax evasion, money laundering, or false records. Canadian corporations must maintain accurate individuals-with-significant-control information under applicable federal or provincial corporate law.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly conceal beneficial ownership or facilitate proceeds may face personal criminal prosecution, fines, imprisonment, forfeiture, and civil recovery. A corporate structure does not shield deliberate fraud, asset concealment, or personal receipt of diverted funds.
Anti-money-laundering and proceeds-of-crime violations

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act may impose administrative monetary penalties and criminal sanctions, including substantial fines and imprisonment. Money laundering under the Criminal Code can carry imprisonment of up to 10 years for indictable conduct, together with forfeiture and restraint orders.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and employees may be personally prosecuted if they authorize, direct, facilitate, or knowingly benefit from laundering or reporting failures. Assets may be restrained or forfeited; travel restrictions may be imposed by a court in criminal proceedings but are not automatic.
Books-and-records and information-return failure

Statutory sanction: Failure-to-file and failure-to-provide-information penalties, denial of deductions or credits, extended reassessment periods in cases of misrepresentation or missing records, audit escalation, and potential prosecution for obstruction or false records.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for knowingly authorizing false books or obstructing an audit and may face director-duty claims. Ordinary recordkeeping failures generally create corporate penalties rather than automatic personal liability, but deliberate conduct can pierce practical corporate protection.
Illegal profit distribution and insolvency-related transfers

Statutory sanction: Potential dividend or loan reassessments, shareholder benefit taxation, corporate-law recovery, oppression remedies, creditor claims, preference or transfer-at-undervalue remedies, and tax collection action.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal repayment, contribution, or damages claims under applicable federal or Alberta corporate and insolvency law where they authorized prohibited distributions, acted in bad faith, or breached statutory duties. Imprisonment generally requires separate criminal conduct such as fraud or deliberate tax evasion.
