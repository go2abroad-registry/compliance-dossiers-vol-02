Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) and Revenu Québec for provincial GST/HST administration in Quebec; for Alberta, CRA administers federal GST and Alberta corporate income tax through the My Business Account portal.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must apply for a CRA Business Number (BN) and register for GST/HST using Form RC1 or the Business Registration Online service. Corporate income tax account registration arises automatically on federal incorporation; an extra-provincial corporation must obtain an Alberta corporate registration through the Corporate Registry and then activate its CRA tax accounts. Non-resident entities may also require a Canadian agent for service and must file prescribed forms with CRA.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No check-the-box or full pass-through election exists in Canada for corporations; taxation is automatic at the entity level under the Income Tax Act. Available elections relevant to cross-border structures include: GST/HST small-supplier exemption and voluntary registration; section 216 election for non-resident operators claiming net-basis taxation of rents and timber royalties; and treaty-based election documentation to claim reduced withholding under an applicable income tax treaty. Hybrid mismatch rules apply under the Income Tax Act but do not allow the corporation itself to alter its classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (i.e., taxable revenues exceed CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter or over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; the threshold applies to worldwide taxable supplies for non-resident registrants unless a mandatory registration arises from carrying on business in Canada).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act for supplies of goods exported by the supplier, and for services supplied to a non-resident purchaser where the services are supplied for use wholly outside Canada. The supplier must have WTO evidence of export such as export documentation, shipping records, and payment terms showing the goods left Canada or the services were delivered outside Canada. A GST/HST-registered non-resident can claim input tax credits and net refunds on export operations.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA eManifest or equivalent export reporting documentation)
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill evidencing shipment to the foreign destination
  • Commercial Invoice, sale contract, and related export transaction records
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or equivalent wire/ACH/SWIFT payment evidence establishing consideration received

Canada does not impose mandatory real-time electronic invoice clearance or digital stamping. CRA accepts electronically issued and retained invoices where they satisfy the Excise Tax Act record-keeping and documentary requirements. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically via GST/HST NETFILE, Extranet File Transfer, or approved EDI when annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 1,000,000; records supporting zero-rated exports must be retained for six years.

CRA may reassess zero-rated export claims, disallow input tax credits, and recover GST/HST together with interest. Civil penalties for false or misleading export statements may be levied under subsection 285.1 of the Excise Tax Act at 25% of the claimed amount, and willful evasion carries criminal prosecution exposure under the Excise Tax Act and Income Tax Act. Export substantiation records must be preserved for six years; failure to produce them on assessment triggers retroactive clawback and interest.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a wholly free-floating, fully convertible currency regime; no exchange-control legislation restricts current-account or capital-account flows. Dividend and profit remittances from an Alberta Business Corporation to non-resident shareholders are unrestricted from an FX standpoint but are subject to Income Tax Act (Can.) Part XIII withholding (domestic 25%, generally treaty-reduced to 15% or 5% for US corporate shareholders under the Canada-US Income Tax Convention). Capital repatriation by redemption of share capital is permitted subject only to ABCA solvency tests, without central-bank approval.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable — Canada imposes no surrender, conversion timeline, or repatriation interval for export or service receipts. Foreign-currency proceeds (USD, EUR, or otherwise) derived from programmatic advertising services may be retained freely in any currency; the only statutory reporting is FINTRAC cross-border transaction reporting above CAD 10,000 and standard IFRS disclosure.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) — central monetary and currency authority; prudential banking and foreign-exchange supervision administered by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money-laundering supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal general corporate income tax rate: 15%. Alberta general corporate income tax rate: 8%. Combined general rate: 23%. Federal small-business rate: 9%. Alberta small-business rate: 2%. Combined eligible Canadian-controlled private corporation small-business rate: 11%, generally applicable to qualifying active business income within the applicable small-business limits.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The domestic Canadian Part XIII withholding rate on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is 25%. A treaty commonly reduces the rate to 5% for a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting the ownership and limitation conditions and to 15% in other qualifying cases. Under the Canada-United States treaty, the principal rates are generally 5% for a qualifying direct corporate shareholder and 15% otherwise. Treaty entitlement must be assessed at the payment date.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 and AT1 returns are due six months after the end of each taxation year. Any remaining balance is generally due two months after year-end. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally pay the balance three months after year-end if the statutory conditions are met, including the applicable taxable-income threshold and association rules.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Most annual information returns are due by the last day of February following the calendar year. NR4 slips and returns are generally due by the last day of March following the calendar year. Payroll source deductions are due according to the assigned remitter schedule.

Box-by-Box Return Breakdown (non-resident focus)

BoxField Non-resident guidanceTax-evasion pitfall
T2 Identification Section Legal name, business number, taxation year start and end dates, corporation type, province of permanent establishment, incorporation details, and whether the corporation is resident in Canada. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada must identify its Canadian permanent establishment and file a T2 for the Canadian tax year. A corporation resident in Canada is generally taxable on worldwide income, subject to treaty relief and foreign tax credit rules. Late filing of a T2 generally attracts a penalty of 5% of the unpaid balance plus 1% of the unpaid balance for each complete month late, up to 12 months. Where a demand to file has been issued or a prior late-filing penalty applied, the enhanced penalty can generally be 10% plus 2% per complete month, up to 20 months. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canadian contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is generally required under Income Tax Act section 247(4) for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. A formal documentation exemption generally applies where the corporation and associated corporations have gross revenues of less than CAD 10 million for the year, subject to the statutory rules. The CAD 10 million threshold does not eliminate the arm's-length pricing requirement, T106 reporting, or the need to support material transactions. Canada generally does not impose a universal OECD-style Local File and Master File requirement for every taxpayer. Country-by-Country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year. A qualifying Canadian-resident constituent entity may have notification and filing obligations where the group threshold is met.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone United Arab Emirates-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Economic substance is nevertheless relevant under the Income Tax Act, the transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership analysis, foreign-affiliate rules, and permanent-establishment analysis. A Canadian AdTech corporation should maintain Canadian decision-making, personnel, technology functions, risk control, contractual authority, records, and operational capacity consistent with the profits allocated to Canada. A principal company that contracts with an Alberta corporation while the Alberta entity performs the key platform, sales, data, or risk functions may face a transfer-pricing adjustment, withholding challenge, or permanent-establishment dispute.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act section 239, conviction can result in a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to two years, or both, in addition to reassessment, tax, interest, and civil penalties. Gross-negligence penalties are generally 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal liability where they personally participated, authorized, assented to, or acquiesced in the offence. Corporate status does not protect an individual from prosecution. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax sanction but may arise through criminal process, bail, arrest, or a court order.
Failure to File or Failure to Comply

Statutory sanction: Late-filing penalties, failure-to-file penalties, daily prescribed interest, compliance orders, prosecution, and potential fines apply. Failure to comply with a demand to file or provide information can constitute a separate offence under the Income Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Directors may be prosecuted for directing or consenting to non-compliance. Personal assessment is not automatic for ordinary corporate income tax, but directors may be assessed under specific statutory provisions, including source-deduction and GST/HST director-liability rules.
Unremitted Payroll, GST/HST, or Part XIII Withholding

Statutory sanction: The corporation may be assessed for the unremitted amount, interest, late-remittance penalties, gross-negligence penalties, and prosecution. Part XIII withholding failures can result in liability for the tax that should have been withheld even where the non-resident recipient received the full payment.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally assessed under Income Tax Act section 227.1 for unremitted source deductions and under applicable GST/HST provisions, subject to statutory due-diligence defenses and limitation periods. Personal bankruptcy does not necessarily eliminate these liabilities.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: The CRA may make a primary adjustment under section 247, impose a 10% transfer-pricing penalty on the adjustment where the statutory threshold is met and documentation requirements are not satisfied, assess interest, and deny unsupported deductions. A corresponding adjustment is not automatic.

Director personal liability: Directors generally face corporate assessment rather than automatic personal assessment, but intentional manipulation, false records, or participation in evasion can produce criminal and civil exposure.
Treaty Abuse and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the full 25% Part XIII tax, interest, penalties, and information-return failures. Treaty benefits may be denied under domestic anti-avoidance rules, treaty limitation-on-benefits provisions, or principal-purpose tests.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally exposed where they knowingly approved false declarations or deliberate withholding failures. Civil restitution is generally through tax assessments; imprisonment requires applicable criminal conduct and conviction.
Artificial Permanent-Establishment Avoidance

Statutory sanction: Possible Canadian or foreign permanent-establishment assessments, corporate income tax, withholding tax, GST/HST, interest, penalties, payroll assessments, and treaty disputes. Contract labels do not override the factual permanent-establishment analysis.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for knowing participation in fraudulent records or evasion. Ordinary permanent-establishment errors generally create corporate tax exposure rather than automatic imprisonment or travel bans.
Shell Company, Nominee Ownership, and Beneficial-Ownership Concealment

Statutory sanction: Potential corporate-law penalties, tax reassessment, denial of treaty benefits, anti-avoidance application, money-laundering investigation, and prosecution for false statements or fraud. Canadian corporations must maintain accurate corporate records and comply with applicable beneficial-ownership transparency obligations.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil claims, regulatory orders, fines, disqualification, and criminal liability for fraud or false filings. Travel bans and imprisonment are not automatic and require the applicable enforcement process.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Proceeds-of-Crime Exposure

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act can impose administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, and criminal sanctions. Maximum administrative penalties can reach CAD 1 million for an individual and CAD 4 million for an entity, depending on the violation and statutory classification. Criminal proceeds offences can result in significant fines and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct, authorize, consent to, or participate in laundering or terrorist-financing conduct may face prosecution, asset restraint, forfeiture, fines, and imprisonment. A DSP or SSP is not automatically a FINTRAC reporting entity solely because it processes ordinary advertising payments, but its actual payment, remittance, foreign-exchange, or virtual-currency activities must be reviewed.
Books, Records, and Platform-Data Integrity

Statutory sanction: The CRA may impose penalties, disallow deductions, assess tax using indirect methods, issue requirements to produce information, and prosecute obstruction or destruction of records. Required books and records generally must be retained for the statutory retention period, commonly six years from the end of the relevant year.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face prosecution for obstruction, destruction of records, or false books. Civil liability may include tax restitution, penalties, and damages; imprisonment and travel restrictions require separate criminal or judicial proceedings.
Corporate Distribution and Insolvency Risk

Statutory sanction: Improper distributions may be recoverable under Alberta or federal corporate law, and shareholder loans or benefits may create taxable income. Unpaid trust amounts can trigger statutory assessments and priority claims.

Director personal liability: Directors may face repayment claims, oppression remedies, breach-of-duty claims, and statutory source-deduction or GST/HST assessments. Personal liability depends on the specific statute, conduct, diligence, and limitation period.
Advertising, Privacy, and Electronic-Commerce Compliance

Statutory sanction: Potential enforcement under Canadian federal and provincial privacy, anti-spam, consumer-protection, and competition laws, including administrative monetary penalties, injunctions, damages, compliance orders, and reputational loss. These liabilities can affect tax deductibility, platform contracts, and regulatory risk assessments.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for knowing participation, authorization, or statutory offences where the applicable legislation provides. Tax authorities may challenge payments arising from unlawful or unsupported activities.
