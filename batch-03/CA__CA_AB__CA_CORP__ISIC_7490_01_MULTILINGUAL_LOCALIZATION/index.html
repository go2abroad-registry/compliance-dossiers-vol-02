Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital is prescribed by the Business Corporations Act (Alberta); the Articles of Incorporation may authorize any share capital. The corporation must not commence business or exercise borrowing powers until consideration for shares allotted has been received. Alberta law does not mandate an escrow account; however, subscription proceeds must be deposited into a corporate bank account upon allotment.

Occupational clearance is obtained by compliance with the Alberta Occupational Health and Safety (OHS) Act, Regulation, and Code, including a workplace hazard assessment, first-aid equipment, and an emergency response plan.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

E-government filing portal

For an Alberta Business Corporation (Inc./Corp.): (1) incorporate under the Alberta Business Corporations Act or the federal Canada Business Corporations Act via the Alberta Corporate Registry or Corporations Canada; (2) obtain the 9-digit CRA Business Number (BN) through Business Registration Online (BRO) at canada.ca/business-registration-online, or by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Program Accounts); (3) activate program accounts: 'RC' for Part I corporate income tax, 'RT' for GST/HST, and 'RP' for payroll only if hiring employees in Canada; (4) a non-resident-owned corporation must file Form RC1 together with evidence of foreign beneficial ownership and director details, and thereafter file annual T2 returns within six months of each fiscal year-end under ITA s. 150; (5) where taxable supplies remain below CAD 30,000, the agency may still voluntarily register for GST/HST under ETA s. 240(3) to recover input tax credits. The BN operates as the single statutory tax identity for both federal and Alberta filings.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box regime. A Business Corporation is mandatorily a separate person and taxpayer under ITA s. 248(1) and Part I, subject to worldwide taxation. Relevant statutory elections for the localization agency: (i) voluntary GST/HST small-supplier registration under ETA s. 240(3); (ii) election to treat supplies between closely related corporations as made for no consideration under ETA s. 156; (iii) election of a non-calendar fiscal period under ITA s. 249.1(3); (iv) for non-resident shareholders, an election under ITA s. 216 converts certain Part XIII withholding on investment income to net Part I taxation, but this does not apply to active translation-service revenue. Hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.2, 18.3, 20.2) operate by statutory disallowance and do not offer elective classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 - the 'small supplier' threshold under ETA s. 148(1): registration becomes mandatory when worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter, or in the four preceding consecutive calendar quarters. Translation/dubbing services sold to Canadian businesses exceed the threshold upon CAD 30,000 of taxable revenue; below threshold, voluntary registration remains available to claim input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating of translation, subtitling, media localization, and dubbing services is governed by ETA Schedule VI, Part V, section 7: a supply of a service to a non-resident person is zero-rated unless (a) the service is supplied to an individual present in Canada at the time of performance, or (b) the service is in respect of real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance. Digital deliverables (adapted scripts, subtitle files, audio masters, QC assets) delivered electronically to offshore studios, platforms, or rights holders qualify for 0% GST. The exporter loses zero-rating where the non-resident client is itself a GST registrant carrying on business in Canada and the service relates to that Canadian business, in which case 5% GST applies. ITCs attributable to export supplies remain fully creditable, and refunds are obtained through regular periodic GST/HST returns (or the non-resident rebate process if applicable).

  • Evidence of non-resident status of the recipient (foreign certificate of incorporation, commercial registry extract, or client questionnaire)
  • Signed localization services agreement specifying offshore client, territory of use, and delivery terms
  • Zero-rated invoice bearing the supplier's CRA Business Number and referencing ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7
  • Cross-border payment evidence: SWIFT MT103, Wise B2B, or Payoneer account statement demonstrating foreign-source remittance
  • Digital delivery logs: CMS, FTP, or IP-transfer records proving offshore delivery and absence of performance in Canada
  • Customs export declaration and courier airway bill - required only if physical master media (localized hard drives or broadcast tapes) is exported; not applicable to pure electronic delivery

Canada imposes no real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or CTC (continuous transaction control) mandate. CRA accepts paper or electronic invoices provided each invoice contains the prescribed particulars under s. 3 of the GST/HST Regulations: supplier's Business Number, date of issue, recipient's name, description of service, amount paid or payable, GST rate, and GST amount. Electronic invoices (PDF, EDI, PEPPOL) are valid without government pre-approval or API clearance; the Pan-Canadian e-invoicing standard (PEPPOL) is currently voluntary and used mainly in public procurement. Books and records must be retained in Canada per ETA s. 286, with CRA consent required for offshore record storage.

CRA may reassess and claw back a claimed zero-rating within four years under ETA s. 298(1), and without any time limit where misrepresentation, wilful default, neglect, or fraud is established (ETA s. 298(3)). A failed export claim triggers assessment of 5% GST on the full invoice value plus compound prescribed-rate interest. Civil penalties apply for false statements or gross negligence in GST returns (ETA ss. 280-281), and criminal sanctions for wilful evasion under ETA s. 282 include fines up to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment of up to five years. Where GST findings flow into income, the 50% gross-negligence penalty under ITA s. 163(2) may also be asserted. Retrospective clawbacks cancel input tax credits previously claimed, require repayment of any refund made, and may trigger the CRA unauthorized-collection review under its International and Large Business Audit Directorate.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible as a free-floating currency; Canada meets IMF Article VIII obligations and maintains no restrictions on payments or transfers for current international transactions under the IMF Articles of Agreement. Current-account openness for ISIC 7490 localization services is absolute: invoiced fees from foreign media, translation, and dubbing clients may be received in USD, EUR, CNY, or any freely convertible currency and converted to CAD at the interbank rate without license or quota. Capital-account convertibility is likewise unrestricted for equity subscription, shareholder loans, foreign direct investment, and repatriation of sale/licence proceeds, subject only to (i) corporate solvency and stated-capital requirements under the Business Corporations Act (Alberta), RSA 2000, c B-9, (ii) Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on dividends (25% statutory; reduced to 5% under the Canada-US Treaty for a US corporate beneficial owner of ≥10% voting stock, to 10% under the Canada-China Treaty for a qualifying corporate owner, and 15% for portfolio investors), (iii) transfer-pricing and Form T106/T1134 reporting for non-arm's length connected shareholders, and (iv) FINTRAC reporting of cross-border wire transfers of CAD 10,000 or more. Profit remittance therefore encounters no convertibility hurdle; the only practical hurdles are Canadian withholding on distributions and the requirement that the localization invoices evidence genuine arm's-length service revenue rather than embedded intellectual-property licensing.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada enacts no export-proceeds surrender rule, no statutory repatriation window, and no mandatory conversion ratio for foreign-currency export receipts. A Canadian service exporter — including a professional multilingual translation/localization agency in Alberta — may retain USD, EUR, or other export invoicing proceeds indefinitely in a Canadian foreign-currency bank account or an offshore correspondent account without surrendering currency to the Bank of Canada or any government body. The time line is purely commercial/treasury policy, not statute: best practice recommends (i) monthly conversion using the Bank of Canada noon exchange rate or a bank treasury rate to minimize FX volatility, (ii) a 60-to-90-day receivable settlement window, and (iii) a corporate treasury policy approved by the board. Tax compliance requires the CAD equivalent be recorded in CRA financial statements; the Income Tax Act, s. 261 permits a qualifying Canadian corporation to elect a functional-currency reporting regime to report in its primary foreign currency. FINTRAC-registered financial institutions and money services businesses (including Wise/Payoneer flows) must report all incoming/outgoing international electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more, but this is a reporting duty on the financial intermediary and does not constitute a proceeds-surrender or conversion quota on the exporter.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), Ottawa. Canada operates no specialized exchange-control administration equivalent to SAFE or a foreign-exchange ministry; statutory authority is distributed among the Bank of Canada (currency issuance, monetary policy, lender of last resort), the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI, prudential regulation of Schedule I/Schedule II chartered banks), the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC, AML/CFT supervision under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, SC 2000, c 17), and the Department of Finance Canada (exchange-rate policy). Non-resident investment review sits separately under the Investment Canada Act (RSC 1985, c 28 (1st Supp.)) administered by Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada. No central-bank pre-approval or registration exists for ordinary corporate FX conversion.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility: Medium-High as a supplemental cross-border disbursement and marketplace-receivables rail, but Low as a primary domestic CAD treasury substitute. Wise (Wise Payments Limited, UK FCA-authorised electronic money institution; Wise Payments Canada Ltd., FINTRAC-registered money services business) and Payoneer (Payoneer Canada Inc., FINTRAC-registered MSB) may lawfully collect localization marketplace payouts and disburse micro-invoices to linguists worldwide; neither, however, is a Schedule I Canadian bank under the Bank Act, SC 1991, c 46, neither is a Payments Canada direct clearer, and neither offers CDIC deposit insurance. Legal constraints: (i) both must maintain valid FINTRAC MSB registration and an appointed compliance officer; (ii) funds flow through omnibus settlement accounts held at partner Schedule I banks, so the corporate bank relationship at a chartered bank remains the legal account of record; (iii) the Alberta corporation should document the EMI as an accounts-receivable/disbursement tool, not as the bank of deposit, to avoid tainted-funds and KYC re-characterization by underwriters; and (iv) incoming EMI credits in CAD equivalent to CAD 10,000 or more trigger FINTRAC transaction-reporting obligations at the MSB layer. The metadata target rails (Wise_B2B and Payoneer) are therefore endorsed as secondary rails, contingent on a primary tier-1 Canadian operating account.

Wise (Wise Payments Limited, UK FCA-authorised electronic money institution; Wise Payments Canada Ltd., FINTRAC-registered money services business) and Payoneer (Payoneer Canada Inc., FINTRAC-registered MSB) may lawfully collect localization marketplace payouts and disburse micro-invoices to linguists worldwide; neither, however, is a Schedule I Canadian bank under the Bank Act, SC 1991, c 46, neither is a Payments Canada direct clearer, and neither offers CDIC deposit insurance. Legal constraints: (i) both must maintain valid FINTRAC MSB registration and an appointed compliance officer; (ii) funds flow through omnibus settlement accounts held at partner Schedule I banks, so the corporate bank relationship at a chartered bank remains the legal account of record; (iii) the Alberta corporation should document the EMI as an accounts-receivable/disbursement tool, not as the bank of deposit, to avoid tainted-funds and KYC re-characterization by underwriters; and (iv) incoming EMI credits in CAD equivalent to CAD 10,000 or more trigger FINTRAC transaction-reporting obligations at the MSB layer. The metadata target rails (Wise_B2B and Payoneer) are therefore endorsed as secondary rails, contingent on a primary tier-1 Canadian operating account.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined federal and Alberta general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal plus 8% Alberta. A Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally qualify for an 11% combined rate on eligible active business income within the applicable small-business limits: 9% federal plus 2% Alberta.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian corporation to a non-resident, subject to an applicable tax treaty or other statutory exemption. Common treaty outcomes include 5% for qualifying corporate shareholders meeting ownership thresholds and 15% for other portfolio dividends; the precise rate depends on the recipient's residence, ownership, beneficial ownership, limitation-on-benefits provisions, and treaty article.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due within six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end; a qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally obtain a three-month balance-due period if the statutory conditions are satisfied. Instalments are due monthly or quarterly under the applicable CRA schedule. Alberta generally follows the federal filing and balance-due timing for corporations subject to Alberta tax.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general transaction-value threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is automatically unnecessary. The taxpayer must make reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length prices and maintain contemporaneous documentation under section 247 of the Income Tax Act. A T106 information return is generally triggered when aggregate reportable non-arm's-length transactions and balances with non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000. Canada does not use the OECD Local File and Master File terminology as a universal statutory filing threshold, although OECD-style local and master-file documentation is prudent for material transactions and may be requested by CRA.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate, generally applicable Economic Substance Regulation regime equivalent to certain offshore ESR regimes. Substance is assessed through the arm's-length principle, transfer-pricing rules, beneficial-ownership analysis, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse provisions, principal-purpose tests, specific anti-avoidance rules, and corporate-residence and permanent-establishment principles. The corporation should maintain Canadian decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational control, books and records, banking and contractual evidence, customer and vendor support, and commercially rational pricing. A Canadian corporation with only nominee directors, pass-through invoicing, no operational control, and all functions performed abroad presents significant reassessment and treaty-benefit risk.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, criminal tax evasion may result in fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years. Civil reassessment, interest, gross-negligence penalties, and denial of deductions or credits may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal criminal prosecution where they participated in, authorized, or acquiesced in the conduct. Corporate limited liability does not protect individual perpetrators. Restitution, proceeds-of-crime orders, asset restraint, and travel restrictions may arise in criminal proceedings; imprisonment is possible.
Failure to Remit Payroll or Part XIII Withholding

Statutory sanction: The corporation remains liable for the unremitted amount, applicable penalties, and arrears interest. Director liability can arise for unremitted source deductions under the Income Tax Act, subject to statutory due-diligence defenses.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally for unremitted source deductions and may face prosecution for willful non-compliance. Corporate dissolution or insolvency does not automatically extinguish director liability.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under section 247, adjust income, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where reasonable efforts were not made, deny unsupported deductions, and assess interest. Secondary adjustments and treaty-correlative-relief issues may follow.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable for a corporate transfer-pricing reassessment solely because it is incorrect, but personal exposure may arise for knowing participation in false records, fraud, obstruction, or source-deduction failures.
Shell Company and Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty benefits under treaty limitation-on-benefits provisions, principal-purpose tests, beneficial-ownership principles, or the general anti-avoidance rule; reassess withholding at 25%, impose interest and penalties, and challenge residency or permanent-establishment representations.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur personal exposure for knowingly false residency or treaty representations, fraud, conspiracy, or obstruction. Civil veil piercing is exceptional under Canadian law and is not automatic merely because the corporation lacks substance.
Corporate Registry and Beneficial-Ownership Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Federal or Alberta corporate statutes may impose fines, director and officer penalties, compliance orders, and offences for false or incomplete records. Applicable federal beneficial-ownership reporting requirements and Alberta corporate-record obligations must be assessed based on the incorporating statute.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be prosecuted or fined for authorizing inaccurate records. Personal liability may attach to statutory penalties and related fraud or money-laundering conduct.
AML, Proceeds of Crime, and Sanctions

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include FINTRAC compliance measures where the business is a reporting entity, administrative monetary penalties, criminal money-laundering penalties, forfeiture, and sanctions-related prosecution. The applicable obligations depend on the corporation's activities and whether it falls within a regulated reporting-entity category.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly facilitate laundering or sanctions evasion may face substantial fines, asset forfeiture, and imprisonment. Directors may be personally investigated for authorization, acquiescence, or failure to implement reasonable controls; travel restrictions may be imposed in criminal or forfeiture proceedings.
Unreported Related-Party Information Returns

Statutory sanction: Specific fixed or escalating penalties may apply for each failure and each day of continued non-compliance, in addition to reassessment, interest, and audit-extension consequences. Repeated or deliberate failures may support gross-negligence or criminal allegations.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally exposed where they directed or knowingly permitted false filings, obstruction, or tax evasion. Ordinary corporate filing omissions generally remain corporate liabilities unless statutory director-liability provisions or misconduct apply.
Permanent Establishment and Provincial Allocation Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA and Alberta may reassess taxable income, require late returns, impose interest and penalties, deny unsupported allocations, and assess withholding or payroll obligations. Treaty relief may be denied where permanent-establishment facts are misstated.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability for unremitted source deductions and criminal exposure for deliberate concealment. A genuine allocation error alone ordinarily does not pierce the corporate veil.
Improper Deduction of Foreign or Shareholder Expenses

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny the deduction, assess shareholder benefits or taxable dividends, apply withholding, interest, and gross-negligence penalties, and reassess associated corporations.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed through shareholder-benefit or source-deduction rules where applicable and may face personal exposure for knowingly fabricated support or fraudulent bookkeeping.
