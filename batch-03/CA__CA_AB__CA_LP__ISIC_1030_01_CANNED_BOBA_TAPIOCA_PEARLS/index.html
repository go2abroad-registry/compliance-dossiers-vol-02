Competent corporate registry

Governing statute

Alberta Partnership Act, RSA 2000, c. P-3, Part 3, including provisions governing limited-partnership formation, certificates, amendments, registered particulars, and cancellation. The Alberta Business Corporations Act does not govern the formation of an Alberta limited partnership.

Formation / constitutional filings

Certificate of Limited Partnership filed under Part 3 of the Alberta Partnership Act. The filing must identify the partnership name, registered office, principal place of business, general partner or partners, limited-partner particulars required by statute, contribution terms, and any stated term or other prescribed information.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital applies. A limited partnership must have at least one general partner and one limited partner. Capital contributions and any unpaid commitment should be documented in the limited partnership agreement and contribution schedule. Alberta does not require a statutory escrow account for formation capital; any escrow or restricted account is contractual or lender-driven.

Funding & unlock path: Obtain municipal building and occupancy approval, fire inspection or fire-code clearance, and certificates for fire alarms, suppression systems, emergency exits, and hazardous-material storage where applicable. Register or comply with Alberta Occupational Health and Safety requirements, including hazard assessments, worker training, machine guarding, lockout procedures, chemical safety, ergonomics, incident reporting, and required workplace policies.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — GST/HST and Corporate Business Number registration administered through the CRA's My Business Account portal and GST/HST Program Account Registration.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity must apply to the CRA for a 9-digit Business Number (BN) before opening a GST/HST program account. Non-resident limited partnerships carrying on business in Canada may register through the 'Non-Resident Account Registration' process, either by mail (Form RC1, Request for a Business Number) or by using Represent a Client / My Business Account through a registered Canadian representative. The BN is the master identifier; a separate GST/HST account (RT program) is established for indirect tax remittance. Proof of foreign registration, partnership agreement, and Alberta corporate registry documentation (for extra-provincial registration) are typically required.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible; the Canadian resident limited partnership is fiscally transparent for federal and Alberta income tax purposes, with each partner computing their share of income or loss directly. The partnership itself is not a taxpayer; it is a reporting and GST/HST registrant if carrying on commercial activity. Territorial scope: Canada levies income tax on the aggregate net income allocated to partners based on their residence and taxable presence; Alberta adds a provincial tax on allocated Alberta-source partnership income for resident partners. No tax-exempt zone applies.

Available election forms

The Canada-United States Tax Convention Article IV(7) joint election permits a Canadian limited partnership to be disregarded as an entity for U.S. federal tax purposes while remaining a pass-through under Canadian law. For GST/HST, a partnership can elect to have any member be the 'partner representative' under section 273 of the Excise Tax Act (ETA) for liability and reporting. Hybrid mismatch rules under the Income Tax Act sections 18.4 and 12.7 apply to impose entity-level adjustments where a partnership is treated differently in a foreign jurisdiction, unless a valid section 94.1 or 94.2 election is available for non-resident partner interests.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

A non-resident entity carrying on business in Canada and making taxable supplies (other than zero-rated supplies) in Canada is a 'small supplier' and exempt from mandatory registration until its worldwide gross revenue, excluding zero-rated supplies, exceeds CAD 30,000 in either a single calendar quarter or over the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Once registered, the entity remains subject to GST/HST net tax calculation, input tax credits, and annual filing. This threshold is the same for Alberta (GST at 5%; no provincial component in Alberta).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Canned boba tapioca pearls exported under ISIC 1030 / NAICS 311999 qualify for zero-rated GST/HST treatment under ETA section 16 (export of tangible personal property) provided the supplier has proof that the goods are exported 'as soon after the transfer of possession as the circumstances permit.' The supplier must make the supply for consideration to the non-resident purchaser and must not supply by way of sale to an unregistered consumer in Canada. Substantially all of the consideration must be attributable to the exported goods. For processed food products, whether the goods are 'food for human consumption' can also support zero-rating on domestic sales, but non-food industrial tapioca pearls may not; the export zero-rating path is independent. A supplier may claim an input tax credit (refund of GST paid on inputs) even if the output supply is zero-rated. The manufacturer must hold all supporting documentation at the time of filing the GST/HST return.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form A6 / Canadian Export Reporting System - CERTS transaction number)
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill evidencing shipment from Canada to the foreign destination
  • Commercial invoice identifying the goods, HS code, quantity, and foreign purchaser
  • Bill of Sale / contract evidencing the export sale and terms of delivery (Incoterms)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank Swift MT103 wire confirmation showing the foreign purchaser's payment
  • Cargo Control Document / destination proof (e.g., customs entry from the importing country) when available
  • Packing list and export packaging records demonstrating physical export
  • Foreign Purchaser's tax status documentation (non-resident registration status if any)

Canada does not operate a mandatory real-time fiscal clearance or government-side electronic invoicing approval system. There is no blockchain-style wholesale digital invoicing requirement at the federal level. The CRA accepts electronic invoices (PDF, EDI, APIs) provided they meet the statutory content requirements of section 3 of the GST/HST Regulations (e.g., seller name, BN, date of invoice, GST/HST paid amount, or indication of zero-rating). The CRA has mandated 'Electronic Filing and Remittance Requirement' for service providers with annual GST/HST net tax liability exceeding CAD 1,000,000 to file electronically; digital stamping is not used. Non-resident registrants may use CRA-approved third-party processors via EDI, but no pre-validation or real-time authorization is imposed.

The Canada Revenue Agency can deny zero-rating if documentary proof is lacking, issuing a reassessment to add GST at 5% plus interest. False or misleading statements attract a gross negligence penalty of 50% of the understated net tax (ETA section 285.1), and every person who knowingly makes a false statement on a return is liable to both a minimum penalty of CAD 500 and a maximum of 25% of the tax evaded, plus fines up to 200% of the amount sought to be evaded and imprisonment for intentional evasion under the Excise Tax Act section 327. CRA's 'cross-border and export verification' program conducts risk assessments on exporters claiming household or consumable exceptions; clawbacks can be triggered retroactively for up to four years (or seven years for misrepresentation) plus compound interest at CRA prescribed rates, and ‘tax avoidance transactions’ under section 274 invite a further 10% misrepresentation penalty.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current and capital account. There are no restrictions on maintaining foreign-currency accounts, no central-bank approval for inbound equity or loan proceeds, and no obstacles to converting CAD into USD, EUR, CNH, or other settlement currencies at market rates. Profit repatriation by an Alberta Limited Partnership is unrestricted at the banking level; the sole friction is tax classification. A non-resident partner's distributive share of the partnership's business income is subject to Part I Canadian income tax only where the partner carries on business in Canada through a permanent establishment, and treaty relief may otherwise apply. Distributions of business income to non-resident partners are generally not subject to Part XIII withholding; withholding applies only to payments characterized as interest, rent, royalties, or management fees (default 25%, treaty-reduced where applicable). Loan repayments and trade-settlement funds flow freely without exchange-control clearance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Nil regime; Canada imposes no surrender obligation, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no statutory timeline for export proceeds. The exporter may receive and hold 100% of foreign-currency receipts in domestic or offshore accounts indefinitely, settle exports through any FINTRAC-registered provider, and convert currencies at will. No export-invoice certification must be presented to the central bank; only routine customs (CBSA) and trade-statistics reporting applies.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) serves as the central monetary authority. Canada abolished administered exchange controls in 1951; no governmental foreign exchange administration or licensing body applies exchange-control approvals. OSFI prudentially regulates Canadian Schedule I banks, FINTRAC administers AML/CTF and cross-border reporting, and Payments Canada operates the domestic clearing systems (Lynx and ACSS).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility: Medium. Offshore digital EMI platforms (e.g., Wise Business, Airwallex, or UK/EU EMIs) may legally service an Alberta LP for multi-currency collection and supplier disbursements, but they must be registered as a Money Services Business with FINTRAC and, for retail payment services, meet the registration requirement under the Retail Payment Activities Act (Payments Canada / Bank of Canada oversight). Most offshore EMIs operate by partnering with a Canadian trust company or Schedule I bank; therefore the EMI should be used as an ancillary disbursement rail, never as the sole treasury settlement account. The primary settlement and capital-escrow infrastructure must remain with a Tier-1 Canadian regulated financial institution.

Stage 06

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined federal (15%) and Alberta (8%) general corporate income tax rate when taxable profits are allocated to a corporate partner; the Alberta Limited Partnership itself is fiscally transparent and has no entity-level Alberta corporate income tax.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder. An Alberta LP distribution is not a dividend; distributions of previously taxed partnership income to non-resident partners generally do not attract Part XIII withholding tax.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Within five months after the partnership fiscal period-end; for a calendar-year Alberta LP, the return is due by May 31 of the following calendar year.

Deadline: · Frequency:

A corporation must file its T2 return within six months after its taxation year-end. The balance-due day is generally two months after the taxation year-end, extended to three months for certain Canadian-controlled private corporations claiming the small business deduction.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate fair market value of reportable transactions for filing Form T106 (information return for non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents). CRA may require contemporaneous Local File/Master File-style documentation for any non-arm's-length cross-border transaction above this reporting threshold; no fixed statutory safe-harbour threshold exists below which transfer-pricing documentation is unnecessary.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain offshore-centre Economic Substance Regulations as stand-alone legislation. However, CRA applies substantive anti-avoidance doctrines through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule, treaty beneficial ownership principles, and limitation-on-benefits or principal-purpose tests in applicable DTAs. The Alberta LP must demonstrate genuine operational substance in canned boba and tapioca pearl manufacturing, including physical plant, employees, production decisions, inventory risk, and commercial autonomy.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under section 239 of the Income Tax Act carrying fines from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to two years on summary conviction or up to five years on indictment; civil gross negligence penalty under section 163(2) equal to 50% of the understated tax, plus interest and reassessments.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner who authorize or knowingly acquiesce in false filings may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST, subject to civil restitution orders, director liability penalties, travel bans in serious fraud cases, and imprisonment upon criminal conviction.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement / Inadequate Documentation

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust taxable income under subsection 247(2); a transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment may apply under subsection 247(3), plus interest. Additional penalties apply for failure to file Form T106 or for incomplete documentation.

Director personal liability: Directors who participate in preparing or approving false transfer-pricing records may be liable for gross negligence penalties; where artificial transfer-pricing structures strip assets or income from Canada, courts may lift the corporate veil and impose personal restitution against directing minds.
Artificial Arrangement / Shell Company / GAAR

Statutory sanction: The General Anti-Avoidance Rule may deny the tax benefit and re-characterize the entire arrangement; treaty benefits may be denied under beneficial ownership or principal-purpose provisions; administrative penalties may include the 50% gross negligence penalty, and Alberta registration or licensing authorities may refuse or revoke the LP's commercial licenses.

Director personal liability: Where the limited partnership veil is used as a facade, courts may pierce the veil; directors of the corporate general partner may face personal civil restitution obligations, director liability for distributions made while insolvent, travel restrictions in enforcement proceedings, and potential criminal liability for fraud.
AML / Financial Transparency Violation

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties may reach CAD 500,000 for individuals and CAD 1,000,000 for other persons per violation; criminal liability under the Criminal Code may include imprisonment for up to 10 years for laundering proceeds of crime.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorize or negligently permit AML failures may be personally liable for penalties imposed on the entity; they may face asset freezes, travel bans, and personal criminal liability if they knew or willfully blinded themselves to the unlawful origin of invested funds.
