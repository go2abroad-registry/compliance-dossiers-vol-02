Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — federal registration, GST/HST and income tax administration, via Business Registration Online (BRO); provincial corporate/individual income tax administered by CRA for Alberta under the Tax Collection Agreement; Alberta-specific corporate registry and licence matters administered by Service Alberta and Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration (ATRA).

E-government filing portal

1) Register the limited partnership under the Alberta Partnership Act through the Alberta Corporate Registry to obtain the Corporate Access Number. 2) Apply to CRA via Business Registration Online (BRO) for the Business Number (BN) in the LP legal name, attaching the Alberta registration details and Canadian place of business. 3) Open CRA program accounts: GST/HST (RT0001), Import/Export (RM0001), Payroll (RP0001) where applicable. 4) File T5013 Partnership Information Return annually and allocate income to partners; resident and non-resident partners report their allocable shares directly. 5) Maintain partner identification (name, TIN/BN, residence, ownership percentage) for Alberta securities and CRA beneficial-ownership verification.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — the Alberta limited partnership is fiscally transparent under ITA s. 96: the LP computes income at partnership level but pays no federal/provincial income tax; taxable income, tax credits and losses flow through to partners; for GST/HST the LP is itself a registrable person and is the reporting entity for GST/HST purposes.

Available election forms

No U.S.-style check-the-box election exists; transparency is statutory. Applicable elections include: (i) ITA s. 96 partnership income allocation agreements; (ii) GST/HST s. 156 election to treat supplies between the LP and closely related corporations as nil; (iii) ETA s. 273 elections to capitalize property into or transfer property out of the partnership without deemed supplies; (iv) GST/HST Quick Method and annual-filing elections; (v) late-filing elections to take effect on earlier dates; and (vi) proposed Canadian hybrid mismatch rules implementing OECD BEPS Action 2, effective for taxation years beginning after enactment, limiting deduction/non-inclusion and importation mismatches.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold: mandatory GST/HST registration arises once total worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated export sales) exceed CAD 30,000 in a calendar quarter or in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; voluntary registration is available and should be taken to recover input tax credits on inputs used to produce exported UHT oat milk.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under Part V, Schedule VI to the ETA: goods exported by the supplier (s. 1) and goods delivered to a common carrier for export (s. 3) attract 0% GST/HST; services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated where not excepted. The packaged UHT oat milk is eligible when it is shipped to a foreign customer and documented as exported; domestic sale of retail-packaged oat milk may also qualify as zero-rated basic groceries under Part III, Schedule VI if not within the restaurant/ready-to-consume exclusions. The exporter must act as a GST/HST registrant, show zero-rated supplies on its return, and keep proof-of-export documentation to support input tax credit claims on inputs.

  • CBSA B13A Export Declaration / Canadian Export Declaration transmitted through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing delivery to a destination outside Canada
  • Commercial invoice and packing list describing HS 2202.99 oat milk product and export sale
  • Cargo control and delivery/acknowledgement records from the foreign buyer or carrier
  • Inward Remittance Certificate, bank credit advice, or SWIFT/TT record evidencing foreign-currency receipt

Canada imposes no real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or continuous transaction control mandate. Invoices must contain prescribed GST/HST content (supplier name and BN, date, description, rate and amount or zero-rating statement). Electronic invoicing is permitted; the Pan-Canadian Peppol-based e-invoicing framework operates on a voluntary basis. Records must be retained in Canada or accessible electronically to CRA for audit.

CRA may disallow zero-rating and reassess GST/HST collectible if proof-of-export documents are missing or contradicted, with arrears interest and penalties. Gross-negligence or knowing false statements attract an ETA s. 285 penalty of the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax; late-filing penalties under ETA s. 280 accrue at 5% plus 1% per month up to 25%. Unremitted GST/HST exposes directors/partners to liability under ETA s. 323. False declarations to CBSA attract Customs Act fines/seizure, and deliberate tax evasion is a criminal offence carrying fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment. Normal reassessment periods are generally four years, extended indefinitely for wilful misrepresentation or fraud.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is generally fully convertible for current-account and capital-account transactions. Canada does not impose general foreign-exchange controls, mandatory capital repatriation procedures, or routine central-bank approval for foreign investment, intercompany loans, dividend payments, or conversion of operating receipts. A Canadian limited partnership may generally receive foreign currency, convert funds through its financial institution, and remit distributable cash offshore, subject to partnership agreement restrictions, solvency requirements, contractual covenants, applicable sanctions, tax withholding, transfer-pricing support, and bank anti-money-laundering review. Payments to non-residents may trigger Canadian withholding tax, commonly at 25% before treaty relief, depending on the payment category and recipient jurisdiction. Interest, dividends, royalties, management fees, and related-party payments require separate tax classification and documentation. Non-resident partners may also have Canadian income-tax filing, withholding, and information-reporting obligations where the partnership earns Canadian-source income. Certain foreign acquisitions or sensitive-sector investments may be reviewed under the Investment Canada Act, but such review is not a routine foreign-exchange approval.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no general statutory requirement to surrender export proceeds to a Canadian bank, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no general deadline for converting export receipts into Canadian dollars. Export proceeds may remain in foreign-currency accounts or be converted at the partnership's discretion, subject to commercial banking terms, sanctions screening, tax reporting, customs documentation, and applicable proceeds-of-crime controls. Foreign-currency receivables and settlements should be reconciled to commercial invoices, bills of lading, customs records, contracts, and bank statements. Canadian export controls, sanctions, customs valuation, and sales-tax rules remain applicable independently of the absence of exchange controls.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Foreign exchange transactions are also subject to federal anti-money-laundering and sanctions controls administered principally by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), the Canada Revenue Agency, the Canada Border Services Agency, and Global Affairs Canada, as applicable.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for supplemental payment operations and low to medium as the primary treasury bank. An offshore electronic-money institution or payment institution cannot automatically provide Canadian deposit-taking, payment, trust, or foreign-exchange services without the permissions applicable in its home jurisdiction and, where required, Canadian registration or licensing. Offshore EMI balances may lack Canadian deposit insurance and may be subject to safeguarding, insolvency, settlement, sanctions, and account-closure risks. A Canadian LP should use a FINTRAC-compliant Canadian financial institution or appropriately registered money-services business for material Canadian payment flows, and should verify the provider's authorization, beneficial-owner policy, correspondent-bank chain, Canadian-dollar clearing access, client-money segregation, transaction limits, and tax-reporting capability. Fintech use should generally be limited to controlled collections, vendor payments, card programs, or foreign-currency transfers, with a domestic bank retained as the principal operating and liquidity account.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route capital contributions, partner loans, and third-party financing through the LP's Canadian operating or designated foreign-currency account using a regulated bank and a traceable sender-owned account. Canada does not use a single universal statutory foreign-exchange purpose-code system comparable to certain controlled-currency jurisdictions; nevertheless, the sending and receiving banks may require a transaction purpose, source-of-funds description, beneficial-owner information, and supporting documents. Use precise payment narratives such as 'limited partnership capital contribution,' 'partner loan under agreement dated [date],' or 'equipment financing proceeds,' and do not describe debt as equity or operating revenue as capital. Maintain the partnership agreement, contribution or loan agreement, resolutions, sender-account evidence, ownership chart, invoice or financing support, and tax analysis. Verify beneficiary name, account number, SWIFT/BIC, intermediary-bank details, currency, value date, and sanctions status before release. Reconcile each receipt to the capital ledger and retain records in accordance with Canadian anti-money-laundering and tax-record requirements. Large or unusual transactions may require enhanced due diligence or regulatory reporting by the financial institution.Use domestic electronic funds transfer or wire payment for Canadian suppliers and SWIFT cross-border payments, commonly represented by an MT103 or its ISO 20022 equivalent, for foreign suppliers. Include complete originator, beneficiary, invoice, purchase-order, shipment, and end-use information. For recurring imports of plant equipment, packaging materials, ingredients, and technical services, maintain three-way matching among purchase order, invoice, and receiving or shipping evidence. A commercial documentary letter of credit may be issued or advised through a Canadian bank for higher-risk suppliers or significant equipment purchases; define presentation requirements, Incoterms, insurance, inspection certificates, partial-shipment rules, discrepancy handling, and sanctions clauses. Use CAD or agreed foreign currency, and specify correspondent-bank charges and payment terms. Avoid third-party settlement unless pre-approved and documented. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium for supplemental payment operations and low to medium as the primary treasury bank. An offshore electronic-money institution or payment institution cannot automatically provide Canadian deposit-taking, payment, trust, or foreign-exchange services without the permissions applicable in its home jurisdiction and, where required, Canadian registration or licensing. Offshore EMI balances may lack Canadian deposit insurance and may be subject to safeguarding, insolvency, settlement, sanctions, and account-closure risks. A Canadian LP should use a FINTRAC-compliant Canadian financial institution or appropriately registered money-services business for material Canadian payment flows, and should verify the provider's authorization, beneficial-owner policy, correspondent-bank chain, Canadian-dollar clearing access, client-money segregation, transaction limits, and tax-reporting capability. Fintech use should generally be limited to controlled collections, vendor payments, card programs, or foreign-currency transfers, with a domestic bank retained as the principal operating and liquidity account.

Stage 06

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Entity-level rate not applicable: Alberta Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent. Partner-level burden: non-resident corporate partner with Canadian permanent establishment is subject to 15% federal Part I + 8% Alberta general corporate rate = 23% combined; qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) active business income up to Federal SBD limit: 9% federal + 2% Alberta = 11% combined.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax applies to dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder. A limited partnership itself is not a dividend payer. Cash distributions of Canadian-source business profits to a non-resident limited partner are taxable under Part I as business income (if the partner has a PE). A non-resident corporate partner is also subject to Part XIV branch tax of 25% on after-tax Canadian business earnings remitted outside Canada; a tax treaty, such as Canada-U.S., may reduce or alter this branch tax exposure.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate fair market value of controlled transactions with non-resident non-arm's-length parties triggers the requirement to prepare and retain contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation; CAD 50,000 per controlled transaction triggers Form T106 filing by the reporting Canadian partner or partnership. Large MNE groups meeting consolidated revenue thresholds must also file Country-by-Country Report and CRA Transfer Pricing Information Return.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations, but Canadian tax law applies the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s.245), treaty-shopping jurisprudence, and the OECD permanent-establishment attribution rules. An Alberta LP manufacturing oat milk must possess real physical substance: production plant, UHT machinery, qualified employees, inventory risk, and business management in Alberta. A shell LP with nominal office address, no plant, no employees, or purely ministerial activity will be disregarded for tax purposes; income and expenses will be reallocated to the principal, the limited liability may be pierced, and treaty benefits may be denied.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: Criminal Code s.380(1): imprisonment up to 5 years for tax fraud; Income Tax Act s.239(1): on indictment, imprisonment up to 5 years and fine not exceeding 200% of tax sought to be evaded; on summary conviction, imprisonment up to 2 years and fine up to 100% of tax evaded. Civil gross-negligence penalty under ITA s.163(2): 50% of understated tax; failure-to-report penalty 8% of any unreported tax amount.

Director personal liability: General partners are jointly and severally liable for all partnership debts and tax liabilities; limited partners who take part in management or control lose limited liability. Directors or managing partners may be personally assessed under ITA s.160/227.1 for tax amounts deducted or withheld but not remitted; Criminal Code convictions can trigger travel bans, seizure assets, and imprisonment.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing / Sham Documentation

Statutory sanction: ITA s.247(2) authorizes the CRA to reallocate income, deductions, or allowances between the LP and the non-arm's-length non-resident to reflect arm's-length principles. ITA s.247(3) imposes penalties of CAD 1,000 per month (maximum CAD 25,000) for failure to produce documentation; an additional 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment may apply if the aggregate adjustment exceeds CAD 1,000,000. Interest accrues on the reassessed amount at prescribed CRA rates.

Director personal liability: Directors signing false certificates or colluding in fictitious invoicing face personal civil liability for the reassessed tax plus interest; under ITA s.242, officers/directors who participate in or assent to a false return are indictable and liable to a fine and/or imprisonment. General partners' personal assets are exposed in execution proceedings.
Non-Resident Withholding Non-Compliance (Part XIII / Part XIV)

Statutory sanction: The withholding agent is liable under ITA s.215(1) for the full amount that should have been withheld, plus interest. Failure to remit amounts deducted results in a 10% penalty under ITA s.227.3 with additional daily interest. Gross negligence penalty 50% may apply; criminal prosecution under s.380 is available for deliberate non-remittance.

Director personal liability: Directors and managing partners are jointly and severally liable under ITA s.227.1 for unremitted withholdings and penalties if they knew or should have known of the failure. Directors who authorize payment abroad without reserving funds for Canada Revenue Agency obligations may be personally assessed; CRA can freeze bank accounts and issue garnishment orders; repeat offenders face travel bans and criminal prosecution.
Shell Company / Treaty Abuse / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s.245) denies the tax benefit and recharacterizes the transactions. The CRA may impose a 50% gross-negligence penalty, deny treaty relief under the Beneficial Ownership and Limitation-on-Benefits provisions, and assess back taxes and interest. Interposed conduit entities can be ignored for withholding tax purposes, resulting in full 25% Part XIII tax.

Director personal liability: Partners who structure the conduit arrangement, directors signing false corporate resolutions, and tax advisors who facilitate the arrangement may be liable for gross negligence penalties; courts will pierce the corporate/partnership veil where the entity is a sham. Personal restitution orders, asset freezes, travel bans, and imprisonment under Criminal Code s.380 are possible for fraudulent schemes.
