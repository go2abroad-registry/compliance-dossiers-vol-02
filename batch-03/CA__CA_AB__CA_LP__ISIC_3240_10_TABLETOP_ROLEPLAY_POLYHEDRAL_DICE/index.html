No statutory minimum capital or mandatory escrow account applies to an Alberta limited partnership. Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — official online portal: My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/account-information-payment-information/overview-my-business-account.html)

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned Alberta Limited Partnership (LP) is not itself taxed as a separate income tax person, but it must obtain a Business Number (BN) from the CRA for GST/HST, payroll, and other program accounts where applicable. Procedure: (1) Confirm whether the LP is required to register for GST/HST by applying the small-supplier threshold test (see indirect taxation rules); (2) If registration is required, complete, either online via Business Registration Online (BRO) or by paper form RC1 'Request for a Business Number', and submit to the CRA; (3) Provide a certified copy of the LP’s registration certificate or partnership agreement from Alberta (or the home jurisdiction for extra-provincial registration), plus identification and Canadian/foreign addresses for the general partner(s) and each partner; (4) If the LP has no physical presence in Canada, designate a resident agent or use a non-resident registration pathway; (5) The CRA will issue a 9-digit BN and a GST/HST program account (ending in 'RT') as applicable; (6) Non-resident partners are subject to Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on paid/credited partnership amounts, reported via CRA Form NR4, and should apply for a non-resident withholding tax account; (7) Ensure the LP files any applicable annual information return (e.g., T5013 Partnership Information Return, even where tax-exempt, to report allocation of income to partners).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canadian law does not provide a 'check-the-box' entity classification election; an Alberta LP is statutorily flow-through for income tax. Permitted elections include: (a) a partnership fiscal period election under subsection 249.1(7) of the Income Tax Act (Canada); (b) GST/HST elections under sections 22 and 177 of the Excise Tax Act, such as the 'partner’s share' method for capital property; (c) an election to provide a non-resident partner with a waiver or reduction of Part XIII withholding under the Canada-United States Tax Treaty, where applicable; (d) for hybrid mismatch arrangements, Canada’s income tax rules (e.g., ITA s. 18.2, s. 18.4) impose designated-filing consequences but do not involve voluntary elections. The LP may also elect registration as a small supplier or to register voluntarily for GST/HST under section 240(1) of the ETA.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — a person (including a non-resident partnership carrying on business in Canada) becomes a mandatory GST/HST registrant once its total taxable supplies in Canada exceed CAD 30,000 in a given calendar quarter or over the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters, unless the supplies are zero-rated. Non-resident persons soliciting orders in Canada via a website, catalogue, or other telemarketing and delivering goods by mail or courier are also required to register once the CAD 30,000 threshold is exceeded, and may be permitted or required to use the simplified non-resident GST/HST registration and filing regime.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of goods and services exported from Canada are generally zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act. For goods: the supplier must be registered and the goods must be exported 'directly or indirectly' by the supplier, the recipient, or an agent of either, normally within 60 days after the sale; the supplier must maintain documentary evidence of exportation, such as a fully completed customs export declaration accepted by CBSA, a bill of lading or waybill showing a Canadian point of origin and a foreign destination, and proof that the non-resident customer wired payment in foreign currency. For services (including design/production services incidental to the manufacture of tabletop dice), zero-rating applies when the supply is made to a non-resident person, the supply is not in connection with real estate in Canada, and the recipient is outside Canada at the time of the supply; if the service is rendered to a person in Canada before export, it may be taxable. A GST/HST registered LP can claim input tax credits (ITCs) on inputs used to produce the zero-rated export supplies, and any GST/HST paid on inputs will be available for refund or offset against tax due. Substantiation must be retained in either paper or electronic form and made available to the CRA upon audit.

Canada does not currently mandate real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government pre-approval of invoices. However, GST/HST tax invoices must meet prescribed content requirements under CRA regulations when the consideration is CAD 30 or more, including supplier name/BN, recipient name, date, description, and GST/HST rate and amount. Registered suppliers may issue electronic tax invoices without prior authorization, but must produce readable paper copies upon CRA request. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically (GST/HST NETFILE) in many cases, and the CRA requires XML/API-based integration for bulk filing or third-party software submissions through its gateway; businesses must retain electronic records and will be subject to CRA electronic audit access under the Income Tax Act and Excise Tax Act. No 'national clearance center' exists for export invoices; export documentation is verified during CRA audits and through customs export filings with the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA).

The CRA may conduct targeted audits of zero-rating claims and require reopening of the export transaction records. Where export evidence is insufficient or falsified, penalties include: (a) reassessment of the supply at 5% GST/HST, plus interest from the date the return was due; (b) civil penalties under section 285 of the Excise Tax Act for false statements or omissions, up to 25% of the tax sought to be evaded or, in the case of fraudulent claims, 50% of the erroneously claimed refund/credit, plus a separate penalty of up to CAD 500 per false invoice; (c) discretionary penalties and suspension or revocation of GST/HST registration where the person knowingly misrepresents exports; (d) criminal prosecution under the Excise Tax Act or Criminal Code with fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to five years; (e) for input tax credit clawbacks, the recipient must repay the full amount credited, with interest, and may be barred from future credit claims. Exporters should maintain contemporaneous evidence of legal export (CBSA export declaration, boll of lading, freight forwarding documents, bank remittance) and a written internal export policy to mitigate scrutiny.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility for current and capital account purposes. No restrictions on repatriation of profits or capital contributions. However, Canadian financial institutions must apply the Canadian Dollar Large Transaction Reporting to FINTRAC for all FX conversions equal to or greater than CAD 10,000 within 24 hours, and maintain detailed records for fund transfers. The limited partnership may maintain foreign currency bank accounts in CAD and USD without limitation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No surrender requirement. Export proceeds can be retained in any currency. For trade receipts above CAD 10,000, the bank is required to report the transaction to FINTRAC and may require a completed FINTRAC Large Cash Transaction Report (LCTR) or a thorough record of the trade invoice.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BOC) - primary central bank; FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada) - AML/CFT reporting; Minister of Finance (Canada) - exchange control policy oversight; Alberta Treasury Board and Finance - provincial financial regulation of non-depository entities.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Moderate feasibility for offshore digital Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) such as Wise Business, Payoneer, and Mercury. Canadian regulators do not certify foreign EMIs as banking institutions. Foreign EMIs cannot qualify for Canadian Deposit Insurance (CDIC). For a limited partnership with Alberta nexus, using a foreign EMI for inbound capital injections is legally permissible but may be delayed by compliance reviews and may not provide the required Canadian debits for provincial GST/HST registration. For cross-border trade settlements and treasury clearing, a Canadian domestic bank account remains mandatory to issue Commercial Letters of Credit (LCs). Offshore EMIs are viable only as supplemental receiving rails for PayPal and Stripe payouts, and must be linked to the Canadian corporate bank account for final settlement. The partner must recognize that foreign EMI processing entities may charge additional FX spreads.

(b) The wire transfer instruction MUST include: the partnership's registered business number (BN) or account number, the name of the recipient as 'ABC Limited Partnership - (Alberta)', and the purpose code 'NGIC' (Non-government Investment Capital) or 'CONS' (Consumption) depending on the remitting bank's coding system. For payments from the US, the remitter should use a Federal Reserve Wire (Fedwire) or the Clearing House Interbank Payments System (CHIPS) if the Canadian bank has a USD correspondent. (c) Where the initial capital exceeds CAD 50,000, the bank may require a signed 'Inbound Capital Declaration' form from the LP stating the funds are not proceeds of crime and will be used for legitimate business development. (d) The bank must verify the transfer against the partnership agreement's capital contribution schedule. If the LP receives foreign capital from a partner, the transfer is not subject to any exchange control approval; however, if the capital is provided in-kind (epoxy resin machinery, molds), the LP must apply to the Alberta Corporate Registry to update the Statement of Creditor and Partner Information. (e) For payment routing through the Bank of Canada's Large Value Transfer System (LVTS), the Canadian bank may pre-clear the incoming transfer if it exceeds CAD 10 million; such threshold is not relevant here. (f) The LP should retain the SWIFT confirmation (MT103) and any accompanying payment confirmation from the remitter's bank for 6 years for CRA purposes.Clearing rails for trade settlements: (1) SWIFT MT103 direct wire transfers - suitable for supplier payments for raw materials (epoxy resin, pigments, silicone molds) up to USD 50,000 per transaction. The LP must include the invoice number and the supplier's bank details; the Canadian bank will use the Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication network to send a full-recourse MT103 to the beneficiary's bank. (2) Commercial Letters of Credit (LC) - recommended for large-repeat purchases from a foreign supplier (e.g., China or India) where the LP has no established trade credit. The LP opens an irrevocable sight LC with its Canadian bank, naming the supplier as beneficiary. The LC is advised and possibly confirmed by the supplier's local bank. The LC must cover the goods' value, freight, and insurance. The FP uses Incoterms 2020 (e.g., EXW, FOB, CIF) to allocate costs. The beneficiary's invoice, packing list, and shipping documents must be presented through the bank's documentary collection channel. (3) PayPal Merchant Account with a cross-border withdrawal to the Canadian bank account - a (USD/CAD) settlement via Automated Clearing House (ACH or EFT) or a SWIFT USD wire. For PayPal payouts, the settlement is in a bundled transfer; the LP should not use PayPal for international trade with non-registered suppliers as it may be restricted. (4) Stripe payouts to the bank account (for Kickstarter and direct web sales) - Stripe sends a settlement via local ACH (Canadian Direct Settlement) in CAD if the LP's bank is CDIC-insured. For US dollar sales, Stripe can either convert to CAD at its spread or hold USD in a Stripe Treasury account, which is not recommended under OFSI's consumer-facing rules. (5) For disbursements to a foreign supplier, the LP must ensure the payment instruction includes the supplier's IBAN and BIC/SWIFT code, and the regulatory format should comply with the Payment Services Directive (PSD2) if receiving display for EU suppliers. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Moderate feasibility for offshore digital Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) such as Wise Business, Payoneer, and Mercury. Canadian regulators do not certify foreign EMIs as banking institutions. Foreign EMIs cannot qualify for Canadian Deposit Insurance (CDIC). For a limited partnership with Alberta nexus, using a foreign EMI for inbound capital injections is legally permissible but may be delayed by compliance reviews and may not provide the required Canadian debits for provincial GST/HST registration. For cross-border trade settlements and treasury clearing, a Canadian domestic bank account remains mandatory to issue Commercial Letters of Credit (LCs). Offshore EMIs are viable only as supplemental receiving rails for PayPal and Stripe payouts, and must be linked to the Canadian corporate bank account for final settlement. The partner must recognize that foreign EMI processing entities may charge additional FX spreads.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate annual transaction value with non-arm's length non-residents triggers mandatory contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation and T106 filing. A CAD 50,000,000 global group revenue threshold triggers preparation of a Master File. Even below these thresholds, CRA may demand documentation under s.247(9), and penalty protection requires complete, contemporaneous, and defensible transfer pricing records.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a separate Economic Substance Regulation, but the federal General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245 ITA) and the Limitation on Benefits provisions in modern Canada DTAs will strike down arrangements lacking genuine commercial purpose. An Alberta LP that is only a shell, has no physical resin-moulding or dice-polishing premises, employs no staff, and merely contracts or re-invoices through a non-resident affiliate carries fatal economic substance risk. The LP must demonstrate real business activity, physical presence, operational employees, and substantive board decisions in Alberta.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act (s.238 and s.239), conviction for tax evasion by means of false statements or omissions carries a fine ranging from 50% to 200% of the federal and provincial tax evaded, and imprisonment up to 5 years on indictment. Civil reassessment may add arrears interest, gross negligence penalties of 50% on the understated tax, and a prohibition from claiming credit for provincial taxes.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers of a corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable for unpaid source withholdings, corporate tax remittances, and penalties under s.227.1 of the Income Tax Act. Personal liability includes civil restitution of the evaded tax, travel bans on departing directors, asset tracing through equitable recovery, and imprisonment upon conviction. A limited partner who participates in management or directs the concealment may be stripped of limited liability under Alberta partnership statutes.
Shell Company / Artificial Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may invoke GAAR under s.245 ITA to deny the avoidance transaction and reallocate income to the controlling non-resident or corporate partner. Transfer pricing reassessment under s.247(2) may be imposed at an arm's-length consideration, with a penalty of 10% of the net adjustment where documentary obligations are not met. Treaty relief may be denied under the LOB provision, resulting in a full 25% Part XIII withholding on deemed distributions.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner may be assessed personally for gross negligence penalties and reinstated tax liabilities. Canadian courts will pierce the limited-partnership veil if the limited partner exercises control over the LP's daily commercial activity, creating unlimited personal liability. Directors face civil actions by the CRA for restitution and, where accomplices to the sham arrangement, may be made subject to criminal fines and travel bans pending trial.
AML / Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: Violations of the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act attract administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 1,000,000 for non-registered money service businesses and fines for individual officers of up to CAD 500,000. Intentional money laundering may constitute an indictable offence under the Criminal Code, with imprisonment up to 10 years; prohibited economic sanctions can also expose the LP to consolidated penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and the general partner are personally liable for AML compliance failures under the PCMLTFA. Personal liability may include restitution orders, seizure of personal assets, travel bans to restrict flight, and imprisonment for money laundering or failure to comply with a disclosure order. Compliant entities maintain full KYC file profiles for every non-resident limited partner and ongoing transaction monitoring for all crowdfunding rails.
Transfer Pricing / Withholding Failure

Statutory sanction: Under s.247 ITA, CRA may adjust the taxpayer's revenue and expenses to fair arm's-length values. A penalty equal to 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment is imposed where the documented sale was a gross overstatement or understatement and records were not prepared. Failure to withhold Part XIII tax on royalties or interest results in a payer liability of 100% of the withheld amount, plus interest and penalties. Late or inaccurate Form T106 filings carry separate penalties of CAD 1,000 per month up to CAD 24,000.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII withholding under s.227.1. The CRA may personally collect from the director, place liens on personal homes, and pursue director's civil certificates for the unpaid amount. Transfer pricing fraud with fraudulent materials or steps may be prosecuted criminally, exposing the director to fines, travel restrictions, and imprisonment up to 5 years.
