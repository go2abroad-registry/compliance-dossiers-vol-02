Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); registration and filing portal: CRA 'My Business Account' and GST/HST NETFILE.

E-government filing portal

The foreign investor registers the Alberta limited partnership for a Canadian federal Business Number (BN) with a GST/HST account using CRA Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts). The application may be filed online through the CRA Business Registration Service, with the LP's partnership certificate/registration under Alberta law, the non-resident general partner's authority documents, and a designated Canadian representative where required. CRA then assigns a BN/GST/HST account number to the partnership; a non-resident partnership can also apply by mail or fax after retaining a Canadian agent for service and compliance.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible - Alberta LP is fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax, with income/loss allocated to partners based on the partnership agreement; however, for GST/HST purposes the partnership itself is a separate 'person' and registrant, and partners remain liable in partnership law.

Available election forms

Canada has no federal check-the-box regime. The LP is statutorily flow-through under Income Tax Act section 96 and cannot elect entity-level status. Limited elections available: the partnership method of allocating GST/HST input tax credits may be set by agreement; annual GST/HST fiscal-year elections, quarterly remittance elections, use of selected tax paid basis, and elections addressing hybrid mismatch and cross-border partner allocation rules are available, all subject to CRA interpretation criteria.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of taxable supplies made in Canada over any four consecutive calendar quarters (small supplier threshold); registration is mandatory once the threshold is exceeded, and voluntary GST/HST registration is available to recover input tax credits on export supplies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of raw cane sugar (HS 1701.14 VHP sugar) is zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V of the Excise Tax Act if the tangible personal property is sold to an exporter/overseas buyer and is exported by the supplier, or on behalf of the supplier or purchaser, as soon as reasonably possible, with no use, consumption, or supply occurring in Canada before export. Supporting records must establish the foreign destination and shipment date; the supplier is simultaneously entitled to claim input tax credits or refunds on related costs.

  • CBSA export declaration (Form B13A/B13 or CES/CARM electronic filing) identifying the outward shipment of raw cane sugar
  • Commercial invoice listing Canadian seller, foreign buyer, HS commodity code, tonnes, polarization value, price terms, and export notation
  • Ocean Bill of Lading / Seaway Bill naming the loading port, discharging port, and foreign consignee
  • Terminal and vessel departure records: container/terminal receipts, mate's receipt, or carrier cargo manifest evidencing physical export
  • Payment / inward remittance evidence: SWIFT MT103, letter-of-credit presentation, or bank credit advice confirming proceeds from the foreign purchaser

No real-time fiscal clearance, API tax stamping, or mandatory e-invoicing within Canada. Suppliers must issue GST/HST supporting invoices/records containing prescribed information, retain electronic data for CRA audit, and may voluntarily use EDI/Peppol e-invoicing. Customs export declarations are filed electronically through CBSA's Canadian Export Reporting System / CARM portal.

CRA can reassess an export zero-rating claim where documentation does not prove export; the supply becomes taxable at 5% GST, tax is demanded with net-of-tax adjustments, ITCs/refunds are denied or clawed back, and daily compound interest applies. Given large-commodity audit programs and CBSA export verification, false or gross-negligence representations may trigger penalties under the Excise Tax Act, Customs Act administrative monetary penalties, and criminal prosecution under the Criminal Code or Customs Act for wilful evasion or false declarations.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely convertible and Canada operates a fully liberalised current-account and capital-account regime since the repeal of the Foreign Exchange Control Act (1952). No Canadian statutory filing, approval, or quantitative restriction applies to inbound LP capital contributions, outbound trade payments, profit remittances, or intercompany loans. Foreign-owned Alberta limited partnerships may remit dividends, interest, and return-of-capital without central-bank permission. The only mandatorily reportable thresholds are FINTRAC international electronic fund transfer (ETF) reports for CAD 10,000 or more per transaction and Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) cross-border currency declarations on physical transfers of CAD 10,000 or more. Profit-repatriation friction therefore arises exclusively from bank-level counterparty due diligence and any sanctions screening obligations, not from exchange-control permits.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no surrender obligation, mandatory conversion ratio, or statutory time limit on export proceeds. The CA_LP may retain VHP raw cane sugar proceeds in USD, BRL, COP, or any freely convertible currency in offshore or onshore accounts, and may remit such proceeds to foreign suppliers without converting them to CAD. Notwithstanding the absence of surrender rules, Canadian banks applying OSFI's FINTRAC-related guidelines will expect the LP to document the economic substance of each USD/BRL/COP inflow and outflow, and all international wires exceeding CAD 10,000 must be captured in the bank's FINTRAC ETF reporting within the prescribed post-transaction timeline.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the sole currency-issuing and monetary policy authority; prudential supervision of all Schedule I commercial banks is exercised by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) under the Bank Act (S.C. 1991, c. 46). Foreign-exchange policy is administered jointly by the Department of Finance Canada (policy) and the Bank of Canada (market operations); preventive AML/CFT enforcement is vested in FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). There is no dedicated 'Foreign Exchange Administration' or exchange-control agency in Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium. Canadian law does not permit a non-bank fintech or foreign EMI to hold CA_LP funds as 'deposits' or to issue revolving letters of credit; such functions are reserved to federally regulated Schedule I banks and authorized foreign bank branches under the Bank Act. A foreign e-money institution (EMI) may, however, act as a FINTRAC-registered money services business (MSB) or as a retail payment service provider registered with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), and may provide SWIFT-like payment initiation, USD/CAD conversion, and settlement routing through a Canadian Schedule I bank trust account. The principal legal constraint is that the EMI cannot serve as the primary depository for LP capital, cannot open an escrow or segregated trust account, and cannot issue any form of documentary credit. The CA_LP treasury may optionally use an EMI for small-amount operational disbursements, but the canonical capital-establishment, LC, and trade settlement rails must sit at a Tier 1 Canadian bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

All LP capital contributions must be received via standard SWIFT MT103 ISO 20022 wires, cleared into the CA_LP's CAD/USD operating account through Payments Canada's Large Value Transfer System (LVTS), with the beneficiary identified on tag 59 as the exact CA_LP legal name and Alberta address; the remitter on tag 50K must be the subscribing Limited Partner only, never a third-party aggregator or undisclosed trust, and tag 70/remittance information must quote the Capital Call Notice number and the phrase 'Contribution to LP Capital - Not for Goods or Services'. For USD wires originating in the United States, clearing is routed through the US correspondent/CHIPS network with intraday conversion to CAD under a pre-agreed multi-currency FX line; FINTRAC ETF reportability (CAD 10,000+) is automatically triggered. The bank requires the following verification package prior to first credit: executed LP Agreement, partnership registration certificate from Alberta Corporate Registry, notarized partner register, capital call notice, NAV or tax basis calculation, and source-of-funds letters from each limited partner. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

0% at the Alberta limited partnership level where income is allocated to partners; taxable income is generally taxed in the hands of the partners. The combined federal and Alberta general corporate income tax rate for an Alberta corporate partner is generally 23% before applicable incentives. Individual, non-resident, and other partner rates vary.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax generally applies to taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident. An Alberta limited partnership does not ordinarily pay dividends; partnership distributions generally reduce a partner's tax basis or represent allocated partnership amounts. Characterization must be determined separately for income allocations, interest, royalties, management fees, capital returns, and deemed dividends.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a single OECD-style threshold that automatically determines whether a Local File or Master File must exist. Contemporaneous documentation is required under Income Tax Act section 247(4) for non-arm's-length non-resident transactions. Form T106 reporting generally applies where reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1,000,000 in the relevant reporting framework or otherwise meet the statutory filing conditions. Canada generally requires transaction-level documentation rather than a universally mandatory OECD Master File; large multinational groups may also have country-by-country reporting obligations where the consolidated group revenue threshold is met.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a standalone regime formally titled Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is nevertheless relevant through the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty principal-purpose provisions, beneficial-ownership requirements, transfer-pricing rules, specific anti-avoidance provisions, foreign affiliate and foreign accrual property income rules, interest limitation rules, and common-law sham and agency principles. The LP should maintain commercially credible functions, personnel or contracted capacity, decision-making records, risk control, financing rationale, inventory title evidence, insurance, logistics records, and independent pricing support. A pass-through entity with no meaningful functions, circular funds, artificial related-party margins, or contractual rights inconsistent with actual conduct presents a high reassessment risk.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of tax, interest, gross-negligence penalties, civil penalties, and criminal prosecution. Tax evasion can result in fines generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years, subject to the applicable prosecution route and statutory facts.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, officers, and persons who authorized or participated in the conduct may face criminal prosecution, personal assessments for certain unremitted source deductions and GST/HST, civil restitution, seizure, prohibition orders, and imprisonment. Limited liability does not protect a person from personal fraud, knowing participation, statutory director liability, or criminal conduct.
Failure to Remit Withholding Tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the unremitted amount, applicable penalties, and prescribed interest. CRA may deny treaty reductions, impose non-compliance penalties, audit related payments, and issue director liability assessments.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts where statutory due-diligence defenses are unavailable. Criminal prosecution may apply for wilful non-remittance or falsification.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust the transaction under Income Tax Act section 247, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the adjustment in qualifying circumstances, deny deductions, reassess withholding, and charge interest. Documentation failures materially increase penalty and audit exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal exposure for knowingly participating in fraud, false records, or wilful tax evasion. Ordinary transfer-pricing disagreement generally creates corporate or partnership tax liability rather than automatic personal liability.
Partnership Misclassification or Sham Arrangement

Statutory sanction: CRA may recharacterize the arrangement, deny deductions or treaty benefits, apply the general anti-avoidance rule, reassess partners, impose interest and penalties, and treat the relevant participant as carrying on business or having a permanent establishment in Canada.

Director personal liability: General partners, directors, officers, and controlling persons may face civil restitution, personal tax assessments, injunctions, asset seizure, criminal prosecution, and imprisonment where the arrangement involves deliberate deception. A limited partner remains exposed for its own conduct and any guarantee or statutory liability.
Beneficial Ownership and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Treaty relief may be denied under beneficial-ownership, limitation-on-benefits, principal-purpose, or anti-conduit principles. The payer may owe the 25% statutory withholding, penalties, interest, and reporting corrections.

Director personal liability: Persons knowingly certifying false residency or beneficial-ownership information may face civil penalties, director assessments, criminal prosecution, travel restrictions imposed by a court where legally available, and imprisonment for fraud or tax offences.
AML, Sanctions, and Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: Potential FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, criminal proceeds-of-crime charges, forfeiture, sanctions penalties, customs consequences, banking de-risking, and imprisonment. Sanctions breaches may attract substantial corporate and individual penalties under Canadian sanctions legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and compliance personnel may face personal criminal liability, forfeiture, civil restitution, court-ordered travel restrictions, and imprisonment where they knowingly participate, consent, or wilfully remain blind to unlawful conduct.
Customs, Import Valuation, and Trade Documentation

Statutory sanction: CBSA reassessment of duties and taxes, penalties under customs legislation, seizure, forfeiture, import restrictions, accounting corrections, and possible prosecution for fraud or smuggling.

Director personal liability: Responsible officers and directors may face personal prosecution, fines, forfeiture, civil recovery, and imprisonment for knowing customs fraud or false declarations. Corporate limited liability does not shield direct participants.
Corporate and Partnership Record Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA may impose record-keeping penalties, deny deductions, apply adverse assumptions, extend audit periods, reassess income, and impose penalties for failure to provide information.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may be compelled to produce records and may face personal penalties or prosecution for obstruction, destruction of records, or false statements. Travel restrictions and imprisonment generally arise only through court process connected with enforcement or criminal proceedings.
