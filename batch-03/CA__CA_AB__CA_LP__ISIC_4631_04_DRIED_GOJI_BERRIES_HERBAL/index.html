Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a United States-style check-the-box election for an Alberta limited partnership. A valid Alberta LP is generally treated as a partnership for Canadian income-tax purposes, with income and loss allocated to partners under the partnership agreement and applicable Canadian tax rules; the partnership may nevertheless have information-reporting obligations, including a T5013 partnership information return where prescribed conditions are met. Partnership classification can be affected by the legal form, governing law, facts, and anti-avoidance rules. There is no general election allowing an Alberta LP simply to elect corporate treatment for Canadian tax purposes. Canadian hybrid-mismatch rules may deny deductions, impose inclusion consequences, or restrict relief where foreign classification differences produce a deduction/non-inclusion or duplicate deduction outcome. Non-resident partners may also be subject to Canadian withholding, filing, and section 116 or other source-specific rules depending on the income and assets involved.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, including zero-rated supplies, over the applicable single-calendar-quarter or consecutive-four-calendar-quarter small-supplier measurement period, subject to the detailed Excise Tax Act rules and associated-person aggregation. Registration may be voluntary below the threshold. A non-resident carrying on business in Canada may be required to register even where the operating model involves zero-rated exports.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods supplied in Canada and exported from Canada may generally qualify for 0% GST where the supplier delivers or transfers possession of the goods in Canada and the goods are exported in accordance with the zero-rating rules, including the prescribed export timelines and documentary conditions. Goods exported by or on behalf of the purchaser may qualify where the purchaser is a non-resident and the statutory export conditions are satisfied. The supplier must retain objective evidence that the goods left Canada, such as customs records, carrier documentation, delivery records, purchase orders, commercial invoices, and destination-country import evidence. Offshore services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated only where the specific place-of-supply and non-resident recipient conditions are met; services relating to Canadian real property, tangible personal property situated in Canada, or other excluded categories may remain taxable. Dried goji berries and herbal botanicals must also be classified separately for domestic GST purposes because certain basic groceries can be zero-rated while prepared, medicinal, dietary-supplement, or otherwise excluded products may be taxable. Export zero-rating does not eliminate customs, food-safety, phytosanitary, product-labelling, or destination-country import requirements.

Canada and Alberta do not generally impose a universal real-time GST invoice-clearance, government digital-stamping, or mandatory e-invoicing requirement for ordinary domestic business-to-business transactions. GST registrants must issue compliant invoices or other documentary evidence showing required information, including the supplier identity, GST registration number where applicable, consideration, tax rate, and GST charged. Electronic records and invoices are permitted if they remain reliable, accessible, and auditable. CRA electronic filing and payment requirements apply to specified registrants and filing circumstances. Customs export reporting is separate: where an export declaration is required, the exporter or authorized customs service provider must submit it electronically through CERS, and customs records must reconcile with commercial and transport documentation.

Canada has no general statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds. Export proceeds may ordinarily remain offshore, subject to banking, anti-money-laundering, sanctions, transfer-pricing, foreign-affiliate, partnership, and income-reporting requirements. CRA may reassess GST/HST where export conditions are not proven, goods did not leave Canada as required, the recipient or transaction was mischaracterized, or the supply was improperly zero-rated. The reassessment can recover undercollected GST, deny input tax credits or rebates, and impose arrears interest. Gross-negligence penalties can generally equal 50% of the relevant understated net tax or improperly claimed amount; other administrative penalties may apply for false statements, omissions, late filing, failure to register, failure to maintain records, or failure to provide information. Fraudulent claims may also create criminal exposure under the Excise Tax Act and Criminal Code, including fines and imprisonment. Customs non-compliance may trigger CBSA Administrative Monetary Penalty System sanctions, seizure or forfeiture, intensified examinations, and reassessment of duties and taxes. The partnership should retain export, commercial, customs, and accounting evidence for the applicable CRA and CBSA retention periods and ensure that refund or input-tax-credit claims are directly traceable to completed taxable or zero-rated supplies.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is an Article VIII member of the IMF and maintains full convertibility on both current-account and capital-account transactions; there is no foreign-exchange licensing regime under the Currency Act or the Bank of Canada Act, and the Canadian dollar floats freely. Profit remittance by an Alberta Limited Partnership to non-resident limited partners is legally unrestricted, but the material compliance hurdles are tax-driven: (i) Part XIII withholding tax at the statutory 25% is imposed on Canadian-source partnership distributions to non-resident partners, reducible under applicable income tax treaties (e.g., Canada-US Article V permanent-establishment domestic US partners, or Article XII dividends if the interest is equity-like); (ii) the LP must file annual T5013 partnership information returns allocating income to each partner and must issue NR4/T5 slips; (iii) non-resident partners disposing of an interest in a partnership holding Canadian real property may trigger Section 116 Income Tax Act clearance-certificate obligations; and (iv) FINTRAC cross-border electronic-funds-transfer reports are mandatory for each transfer above CAD 10,000. Practical structuring: maintain a capital escrow account in CAD, convert foreign capital injections at the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate, and document all remittance cables with the treaty-residency certificate of each partner to secure reduced withholding rates.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no holding-period restriction; the Alberta exporter may freely retain proceeds in USD, CNH, EUR, or any other convertible currency in a domestic multi-currency account or in an approved foreign account. For CRA compliance, export revenue must be converted to Canadian dollars for income inclusion using the Bank of Canada daily noon exchange rate prevailing on the date revenue is recognized under the accrual method, unless an alternate consistent rate is elected under section 1.5 of the Income Tax Regulations. Cash export receipts above CAD 10,000 are reportable to FINTRAC as Large Cash Transaction Reports, and cross-border wire receipts above CAD 10,000 are reportable as Electronic Funds Transfer Reports. If the LP holds a 10% or greater ownership interest in a foreign distribution subsidiary or foreign buyer entity, annual Form T1134 (Foreign Affiliate Information Return) must be filed; otherwise no surrender or repatriation timeline applies.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), acting jointly with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for chartered bank supervision, the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for anti-money-laundering and cross-border transaction reporting, the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for export trade documentation, and the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) for tax and information-return oversight. The federal Minister of Finance has residual exchange-control authority, but Canada has not exercised discretionary exchange controls since the 1950s; no prior approval from a foreign-exchange administrator is required for any current-account or capital-account transfer.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM-HIGH for ancillary rails, with strict legal constraints. Canadian-domiciled electronic money institutions and foreign money services businesses (FMSBs) such as Wise Platform, Airwallex, and Payoneer are registered with FINTRAC as MSBs/FMSBs under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and may be used for cross-border receivables concentration, seller/vendor payouts, and multi-currency settlements; however, they are not chartered banks under the Bank Act and cannot lawfully accept Canadian-demand-deposit balances. An Alberta LP may hold operating floats at EMIs provided each account is not presented to customers as a deposit and the aggregate balance stays below the Ontario/Quebec money-movement caps; all cross-border EFTs above CAD 10,000 remain FINTRAC-reportable. Legal risk arises if EMI balances are used to render 'banking-like services' to third parties, which would require a bank license; therefore the EMI layer must be subordinate to a Schedule I bank liquidity anchor and restricted to settlement rather than custody of export proceeds.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound capital injections into the Alberta LP are executed as SWIFT MT103 wire transfers from non-resident investors to the LP's Capital Escrow Account at a Schedule I Canadian bank, using ISO 20022 Purpose Code 'COST' (payment for capital) or 'CNCC' (cash contribution to a limited partnership); the remittance field must state 'Quadro Capital Contribution per LP Agreement — CA_AB'. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: MEDIUM-HIGH for ancillary rails, with strict legal constraints. Canadian-domiciled electronic money institutions and foreign money services businesses (FMSBs) such as Wise Platform, Airwallex, and Payoneer are registered with FINTRAC as MSBs/FMSBs under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and may be used for cross-border receivables concentration, seller/vendor payouts, and multi-currency settlements; however, they are not chartered banks under the Bank Act and cannot lawfully accept Canadian-demand-deposit balances. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% (15% federal + 8% Alberta general corporate rate); individual partners are taxed at progressive personal rates, and Canadian-controlled private corporations may be eligible for a reduced 11% rate on the first C$500,000 of active business income.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII Canadian withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian corporation to non-residents; partnership profit allocations are not dividends and are generally not subject to Part XIII withholding, but rather are taxed under Part I as Canadian-source business income of the non-resident partner.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No statutory de minimis transaction threshold; CRA expects contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation (Local File and Master File) for any cross-border transaction with a non-arm’s length non-resident. Country-by-Country reporting is mandatory for multinational groups with consolidated annual group revenue of €750 million or more (approximately C$1.1 billion).

Economic substance scope: Canada has no formal Economic Substance Regulations (ESR); however, the CRA applies the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and the transfer pricing rules in ITA s.247 to disregard transactions lacking economic substance. Partners must demonstrate genuine commercial activity through physical premises, employees, operational records, and meaningful decision-making to withstand CRA recharacterization.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Willful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Indictable offence under ITA s.239: fine equal to up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years. Civil penalties under s.163(2) apply to gross negligence of 50% of the understated tax, plus interest.

Director personal liability: Directors and partners who actively participate in tax evasion can be jointly and severally liable for the corporate or partnership tax debt through civil restitution. Criminal exposure includes personal fines and imprisonment; travel bans and asset freezes may be imposed pending prosecution.
Failure to File Information Returns (T5013 / T2)

Statutory sanction: T5013 late penalty: C$25 per partner per day, minimum C$100, maximum C$2,500; T2 late penalty: 5% of unpaid tax plus 1% per month up to 12 months, and 10% for repeated failures. Interest at prescribed rates compounds daily.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for the penalty amounts if they authorized, acknowledged, or acquiesced in the failure to file. Under ITA s.227.1, directors can be personally assessed for the corporation’s or partnership’s unreported withholding obligations if they failed to exercise due diligence.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement / Inadequate Documentation

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing penalty under ITA s.247(3): 10% of the net upward transfer pricing adjustment, applied when the adjustment exceeds the lesser of 10% of gross revenue and C$5 million. CRA may also impose a failure-to-document penalty up to C$1,000 per month, and interest on the adjusted tax.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who knowingly sign inadequate documentation or direct artificial pricing structures may be held personally liable for the penalty if the court finds they exercised a controlling influence and failed to correct the misstatement. Criminal gross negligence penalties can extend to personal imprisonment.
Failure to Withhold Part XIII Tax on Non-Resident Payments

Statutory sanction: The payer is liable for the entire unwithheld tax amount, plus a penalty of 10% (or 20% if the payment was already subject to a demand) and daily compound interest under s.227(8). Repeat violations may trigger prosecution under s.239.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted withholding taxes under ITA s.227.1 without proof of fault, subject only to a due-diligence defence. The CRA may pursue personal assets, issue travel bans, and enforce criminal charges for systematic non-compliance.
