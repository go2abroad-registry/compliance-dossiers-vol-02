Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital applies to an Alberta limited partnership; each limited partner must make a genuine capital contribution recorded in the Declaration of Limited Partnership to sustain limited-liability protection. Alberta law does not mandate an escrow account, but the lending bank will require traceable deposit of the subscribed contribution before activating Revolving LC and SWIFT TT facilities.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety certification under the Alberta Fire Code issued pursuant to the Safety Codes Act (RSA 2000, c S-1); structural load inspection certification for the refrigerated warehouse; Alberta Occupational Health and Safety (OHS) registration with the Ministry of Labour and Immigration, including cold-storage hazard controls, worker safety program, emergency response plan, and workplace annual employer reporting.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (Agence du revenu du Canada) via the Business Registration Online portal and GST/HST NETFILE

E-government filing portal

A foreign limited partnership must obtain a Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 with the Canada Revenue Agency, either through the Business Registration Online portal or by mail, providing legal partnership name, registered office in Alberta or Canadian representative, foreign registration certificate, particulars of general partners, and indicating a request for a GST/HST account. CRA issues a 15-character BN/GST/HST registration number, which must be used on invoices and customs documentation.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — A foreign limited partnership must obtain a Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 with the Canada Revenue Agency, either through the Business Registration Online portal or by mail, providing legal partnership name, registered office in Alberta or Canadian representative, foreign registration certificate, particulars of general partners, and indicating a request for a GST/HST account. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not offer a 'check-the-box' entity classification election. The limited partnership is fiscally transparent for income tax; the partnership itself is generally a registrant and collects GST/HST. Non-resident partners may elect under Canadian tax treaties or domestic rules to file returns on a net basis (e.g., section 216 election under the Income Tax Act for certain rental income) or elect to make a partnership GST/HST registration among qualifying partners. Hybrid mismatch rules in section 18.4 of the Income Tax Act apply to disallow excessive deductible expenses.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual taxable supplies (worldwide small supplier threshold) for non-resident businesses carrying on business in Canada; goods imported into Canada are subject to GST at border regardless of the threshold unless a section 168 import election or zero-rated schedule applies

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods (including frozen beef, seafood, and meat products) are zero-rated supplies under Part V of Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act if the supplier is registered for GST/HST and the goods are exported in accordance with the conditions prescribed in sections 20 and 21 of the GST/HST Regulations. Zero-rating may also apply to basic groceries sold domestically for human consumption, but the export route requires proof that property was delivered outside Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Border Services Agency B13 Export Declaration or approved substitute)
  • Commercial invoice and foreign sale contract
  • Bill of Lading or airway bill evidencing export
  • Cargo control documents and customs coded destination (e.g., U.S./foreign port of discharge)
  • Proof of payment matching the exported goods
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank receipts evidencing receipt of foreign exchange proceeds

Canada does not mandate a government-run e-invoicing clearance mechanism; there is no statutory real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping requirement. GST/HST invoices must include prescribed information (e.g., seller name, BN/GST/HST registration number, tax amount), and may be issued electronically provided the CRA is able to access and verify the records. CRA may require documented traceability in audits, but there is no API-based continuous transaction control system in effect.

CRA audits verify export evidence against customs records; failure to substantiate zero-rating results in reassessment at 5% GST/HST, interest, and penalties under sections 285 and 280 of the Excise Tax Act. Willful filing of false export claims may attract penalties equal to 100% of tax avoided or a fine of up to CAD 25,000, plus imprisonment for fraud. Refunds or input tax credits claimed on zero-rated exports are subject to recovery, with a limitation period generally of four years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is an IMF Article VIII member and maintains a market-determined floating exchange rate with full current-account and capital-account convertibility. There are no exchange controls, no FX transaction licensing, no prior-approval requirements for repatriation of profits or partnership capital, and no central-bank allocation of foreign currency. The Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely tradable on the spot and forward markets, and Canadian Schedule I banks may execute any lawful FX transaction without government authorization. The only practical hurdles are fiscal and reporting measures, not convertibility restrictions: (i) an Alberta Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent, and Canada-source income must be reported to each partner via a T5013 statement; (ii) non-resident partners are taxable under Part I of the Income Tax Act on Canadian-source business income and may be subject to Part XIII withholding or treaty-reduced rates on certain distributions, with the partnership responsible for remittances; (iii) the bank must file a FINTRAC Electronic Funds Transfer report for every cross-border transfer of C$10,000 or more; and (iv) source-of-funds documentation is required for any inbound capital or outbound remittance that the bank's compliance desk considers unusual. None of these measures imposes a ceiling, an approval process, or a conversion mandate, and no hurdle prevents profit or capital repatriation once the tax returns and withholding obligations are satisfied.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender regime and no mandatory conversion ratio. The Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52) and Canada's Article VIII obligations under the IMF Articles of Agreement prohibit any requirement to surrender foreign exchange or to convert export receipts into CAD within a prescribed timeline. The statutory remittance timeline is therefore immediate (zero days) and the mandatory conversion ratio is nil (0%). Exporters and importers may retain and deploy foreign-currency proceeds freely in Canadian or offshore accounts, provided the accounts are disclosed in tax filings and the funds are traceable under IFRS. For the import of frozen beef and pelagic fish, the CBSA applies the Bank of Canada daily noon exchange rate to convert foreign-currency invoices into CAD for Customs Act ad valorem duty valuation; this is a valuation rule, not a surrender or conversion obligation.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as the central monetary authority; prudential regulation of banks is vested in the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); payment and settlement infrastructure is administered by Payments Canada under the Canadian Payments Act; Canada maintains no separate central foreign-exchange administration comparable to SAFE, and the Department of Finance Canada retains residual jurisdiction over the Exchange Control Act framework, which was repealed in 1952.
