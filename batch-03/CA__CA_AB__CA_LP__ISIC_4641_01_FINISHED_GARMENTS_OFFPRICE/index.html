Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) is the competent federal tax authority for income tax, GST/HST, withholding tax, and customs-related tax administration; the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) administers customs and export controls. Alberta provincial taxes, including the Alberta corporate income tax, are administered by CRA under federal-provincial tax collection agreements. The primary digital portal is CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html).

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity operating through an Alberta limited partnership must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) for each program account required. The standard procedure is to file Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) together with Form RC459 (Application for a Non-Resident GST/HST Account) when the partnership has no Canadian residence, and to ensure the LP is validly registered extra-provincially under Alberta's Partnership Act if formed outside Alberta. The partnership BN is mandatory for GST/HST registration, income tax account establishment, payroll if applicable, and customs/export account enrolment. Non-resident partners should obtain Canadian individual or non-resident tax identification numbers and disclose their partnership interests to CRA.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible. An Alberta limited partnership is fiscally transparent for Canadian federal and Alberta income tax purposes; it is not taxed as an entity. Each partner is allocated partnership income, gains, losses, and credits pursuant to the limited partnership agreement and Part I of the Income Tax Act (Canada). Canadian-resident partners are taxable on their worldwide partnership income. Non-resident partners are taxable only on Canadian-source income attributable to a Canadian business or permanent establishment, taxable Canadian property, or certain passive Canadian-source amounts, subject to applicable income tax treaty limitations.

Available election forms

There is no Canadian 'check-the-box' election equivalent. The tax classification of an Alberta LP is determined by domestic statute and,jurisprudence, not by unilateral election. However, the partnership may elect a fiscal period under ITA s.249.1, may make capital cost allowance and partnership property elections (e.g., ITA s.97(2) and s.98(3) elections on contributed or distributed property), and may elect to have particular allocations binding for tax. Any U.S. Form 8832 'check-the-box' election made by the underlying partners does not alter the Canadian tax treatment of the partnership, and hybrid mismatch rules may require disclosure or administrative adjustment where there are divergent entity classifications between Canada and a counterparty jurisdiction.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

$30,000 CAD. A non-resident supplier that is not a small supplier must register for GST/HST once its gross worldwide taxable supplies, including taxable supplies in Canada, exceed $30,000 CAD in any single calendar quarter or over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters. Because the subject entity is an Alberta LP in the wholesale apparel liquidation trade, this threshold is measured on gross revenue before deducting purchases or expenses.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of tangible personal property, including finished branded apparel sold on an off-price wholesale liquidation basis, is zero-rated for GST/HST purposes under s.22 of the Excise Tax Act where the supplier either delivers the goods to a carrier for shipment outside Canada or causes the goods to be exported as commercial goods, and the goods are destined for a destination outside Canada. The Alberta LP must also supply in the course of making the sale, retain evidence of export within the CRA-prescribed retention period, and show that title and possession passed to the non-resident buyer outside Canada. Supplies delivered in Canada to a domestic buyer, even if subsequently exported by the buyer, require careful fact analysis and may not qualify for zero-rating if the sale is not a direct export supply.

  • CBSA Export Declaration (Form B13A or CAERS electronic declaration) for controlled, regulated, or goods above the prescribed export-reporting value thresholds
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List describing the finished garments by HS heading/ISIC industry code and identifying the exporter, importer, and ultimate destination outside Canada
  • Bill of Lading, airway bill, or freight forwarder certificate of shipment evidencing actual departure from Canada to the foreign destination
  • Canada Customs Invoice or equivalent export declaration where required by the destination country or requested by the buyer
  • Inward remittance certificate or, equivalently, SWIFT MT103/credit advice evidencing the receipt of export sale proceeds in the partnership's bank account

Canada does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance, continuous controls, or digital stamping regime for GST/HST invoicing. CRA accepts electronic invoices where the supplier and recipient maintain a fully reliable audit trail and the content meets the requirements of the Excise Tax Act. GST/HST returns are required to be filed electronically in most circumstances through CRA My Business Account, GST/HST NETFILE, or EDI. The Alberta LP should implement an ERP-based e-invoicing workflow with permanent invoice attachment of export proof to support zero-rating; there is no statutory requirement for government pre-approval of invoice issuance.

CRA and CBSA audit export-documentation files with a presumption that insufficient contemporaneous evidence will cause the zero-rating to be disallowed. If the zero-rating is denied, the Alberta LP becomes liable for 5% GST on the full export invoice amount plus interest, and may be subject to penalties for false statements or omissions under the Excise Tax Act, including a gross-negligence penalty of 20% of the net tax understatement. Wilful evasion can attract criminal prosecution, fines of up to at least $25,000 CAD, and imprisonment. Customs penalties under the Customs Act may be assessed for incorrect export declarations, and CRA may reassess within the standard limitation period and claw back claimed input tax credits, whether on the export supply itself or on related domestic costs. Documentary reconstruction is not a substitute for contemporaneous export proof; an aggressive audit posture applies to sector code ISIC 4641 because of recurring refund schemes involving high-volume apparel liquidation transactions.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible under the IMF Article VIII obligations. Both capital account (non-resident LP capital contributions, loan injections) and current account (trade payments, operating expenses, management fees) transactions are generally unrestricted. Profit/income distributions from a Canadian limited partnership to non-resident partners are subject to withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (or domestic/treaty reduced rates) only when the LP files as a non-resident-owned partnership investment entity or the payment is income from Canadian sources; capital returns of contributed capital are not taxable remittances. There are no exchange control permits, prior approvals, or profit remittance licenses required for USD/CAD cross-border transfers.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory export proceeds surrender requirements and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts in Canada. Exporters may retain foreign currency in any Canadian or offshore account, stream settlement through a Canadian bank, and convert CAD/USD at commercial rates. The only compliance obligation is the CAD 10,000 cross-border currency and monetary instruments reporting threshold under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) when physically transporting cash or bearer instruments across the border.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for federally regulated deposit-taking institutions; exchange controls are administered by the Canada Border Services Agency and FINTRAC for cross-border currency reporting, not by a dedicated foreign exchange administration.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium with material legal constraints. Offshore digital EMI/neobank platforms (e.g. UK/FCA or EU-licensed EMIs) are not authorized to take deposits or provide Canadian domestic payment accounts under the Bank Act. They may be used only as auxiliary receivable-tooling for USD collections, not as the primary treasury settlement rail. The Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) requires payment service providers operating in Canada to register with the Bank of Canada; offshore EMIs that merely initiate payments into Canadian accounts may be tolerated, but a Canadian CA_LP must maintain a true Canadian-dollar clearing account at a Schedule I bank or credit union to settle supplier and overstock liquidation receipts.

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% (federal 15% + Alberta general corporate rate 8%) for taxable active business income of corporate partners; the Alberta LP itself is fiscally transparent

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory Part XIII withholding tax rate under the Income Tax Act for dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident recipient; for the Alberta LP, profit distributions to non-resident partners are not 'dividends' but allocations of partnership profit. Such allocations may be deemed Canadian-source business income and are taxable under Part I, with Part XIII withholding applying to taxable dividends or certain deemed remittances.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: T106 Form filing is mandatory where the aggregate value of specified non-arm's length transactions with any one non-resident exceeds C$5 million in a fiscal period. Canada does not maintain separate Local File/Master File statutory thresholds; contemporaneous documentation (OECD-compliant) is required for any transaction above C$5 million or where a transfer-pricing adjustment risk exists. Country-by-country reporting applies when the global consolidated group revenue exceeds C$750 million.

Economic substance scope: Alberta is not an Offshore Financial Centre, so an Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) in the Caribbean/OECD sense does not apply. However, Canadian anti-avoidance rules, particularly the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245 ITA), the statutory substantive content doctrine, and judicial concepts of a 'permanent establishment' require the Alberta LP to maintain genuine managerial, operational, and economic presence in Alberta. A 'letterbox' LP with no local bank account, no actual storage, and no real decision-making may be disregarded by CRA and treated as a shell, with the ultimate non-resident owner deemed to have a taxable Canadian permanent establishment.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (underreporting gross receipts or fabricating expenses)

Statutory sanction: Section 239 Income Tax Act: on summary conviction, fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded plus imprisonment of up to 2 years; on indictment, imprisonment up to 5 years. CRA may reassess beyond the normal three-year limitation period and apply a 50% gross negligence penalty on the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner may be held personally liable for unremitted withholding tax, Part XIII taxes, or unremitted GST/HST under section 223/227.1 ITA. Where evasion is proven, a director may be criminally prosecuted, disqualified from serving as a director, and subjected to civil restitution; CRA may also obtain a travel ban under the Federal Administration of Justice law and seize assets in Canada as well as through mutual treaty enforcement.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation and Unreported Cross-Border Transactions

Statutory sanction: Section 247(3) ITA imposes a transfer-pricing penalty equal to 10% of the net upward adjustment to income, in addition to reassessed tax and interest. Administrative monetary penalties may be applied for T106 non-filing; interest runs from the original filing date.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in creating false documentation or who sign returns containing false or misleading information may be personally liable for penalties and restitution. They can be sued by CRA for the full adjustment amount under directors' liability provisions and may face criminal sanctions if their conduct constitutes criminal tax fraud.
Shell Company / Missing Economic Substance (Abusive Tax Planning)

Statutory sanction: CRA may use the GAAR (section 245) to nullify tax benefits, reassess the LP, and attribute the business directly to the non-resident principal. Under Alberta Limited Partnerships Act, a limited partner that takes control of the partnership's business can lose limited liability. AML/ATF penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act can be up to C$1 million for individuals and C$5 million for corporate entities for failing to establish a true commercial transactional identity.

Director personal liability: The director's corporate veil is pierced if the LP is found to be a mere instrumentality; the general partner director becomes jointly and severally liable to creditors for losses, while CRA may assert personal liability for tax amounts where the director actively controls the sham. Court orders may include travel restrictions, asset freezing, and extraterritorial collection in treaty jurisdictions.
Improper Profit Repatriation and Withholding Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Unremitted Part XIII tax becomes immediately due and payable; CRA will assess 10% penalty plus daily interest under the Income Tax Act. Gross negligence penalties up to 50% of the amount not withheld may be imposed, and, if unpaid, CRA may obtain a Director's Liability Judgment.

Director personal liability: Directors are statutorily liable under section 227.1 for all unpaid withholding tax if they fail to cause the partnership to withhold, remit, and report; they may face personal bankruptcy proceedings initiated by the Crown, travel bans, and criminal prosecution if they knowingly concealed distributions. A personal fine of up to C$25,000 and imprisonment for up to two years may result for each count of failure to remit.
