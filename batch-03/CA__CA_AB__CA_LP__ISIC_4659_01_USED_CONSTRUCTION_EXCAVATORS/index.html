Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) - national tax authority administering GST/HST, income tax, and partnership information returns; portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/e-services-businesses/business-account.html) and GST/HST NETFILE; Alberta provincial income tax is collected by CRA under the federal-provincial Tax Collection Agreement, while Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration (ATRA) administers provincial commodity taxes.

E-government filing portal

Step 1 - Determine whether the Alberta Limited Partnership makes taxable supplies in Canada in the course of a commercial activity; mandatory GST/HST registration applies unless the small supplier threshold (CAD 30,000) is satisfied. Step 2 - File Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) online via CRA My Business Account or by paper; non-resident general partners may use CRA's simplified non-resident registration channel and need not have a Canadian place of business. Step 3 - CRA issues a 9-digit Business Number (BN) with account suffixes, including RT for GST/HST and RP for income tax withholding; the BN is the statutory tax identifier in Canada. Step 4 - Register the LP under the Alberta Partnership Act with the Alberta Corporate Registry (Service Alberta) and obtain the provincial registration certificate; this legal registration is a prerequisite for opening Canadian bank accounts and for customs export reporting. Step 5 - File the T5013 Partnership Information Return with CRA within five months of the fiscal year-end and issue T5013 slips to all partners, including non-resident partners, by end of March. Step 6 - Non-resident partners obtain a CRA individual/corporate account (SIN or BN) for filing Canadian tax returns; Canada has no separate TIN beyond the BN/SIN.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible - an Alberta Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax purposes under ITA s.96; the LP itself pays no federal or Alberta income tax and income/losses flow through to partners. For GST/HST purposes the partnership is a separate person and must register in its own name for the zero-rated export of used construction excavators.

Available election forms

Canada has no U.S.-style check-the-box election; classification follows the legal form and CRA guidance (Income Tax Folio S4-F16-C1). Relevant elections: (1) voluntary GST/HST registration by a small supplier under ETA s.240(4); (2) an election under ITA s.216 for non-resident partners owning Canadian rental real property to be taxed on net rental income instead of 25% gross Part XIII withholding; (3) a common fiscal-period election under ITA s.96(3.1) to align the LP's year-end with the year-ends of its partners; (4) hybrid mismatch alignment elections under ITA ss.18.4(4) and 18.5(2) where a partner jurisdiction (e.g., the United States) applies check-the-box entity treatment to the Canadian LP; and (5) an election to treat a non-resident partner's Canadian activities as a permanent establishment under the applicable tax treaty.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 - the small supplier threshold under ETA s.123(1): a person whose aggregate taxable supplies made in Canada (including supplies of associated entities) in the current calendar quarter and the four preceding calendar quarters does not exceed CAD 30,000 is exempt from mandatory GST/HST registration; non-residents not carrying on business in Canada are measured on worldwide supplies under ETA s.123(1.1). Voluntary registration is available from the first dollar of taxable supply activity.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Schedule VI, Part V, s.1 of the Excise Tax Act, a supply of tangible personal property is zero-rated when the goods are exported as part of the supply without further processing in Canada. For used hydraulic excavators and rollers sold by the Alberta LP: (a) if the LP acts as exporter of record, the equipment must be exported as soon as reasonably possible and no later than 60 days after delivery; (b) if the non-resident buyer takes delivery in Canada and is itself a GST/HST registrant that exports the equipment within 180 days, the LP may zero-rate the sale after obtaining the buyer's GST registration number; (c) the LP must retain evidence satisfactory to the Minister, including the B13A export declaration, bill of lading, freight forwarder certificate, and proof of arrival at the foreign destination. A supply of offshore remarketing or brokerage services to a non-resident principal is zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, s.7 when the services are performed wholly outside Canada or the benefit is enjoyed outside Canada. Input tax credits for GST paid on the acquisition, freight, inspection, and repair of the used machinery are claimable on the LP's GST/HST return; the zero-rated export simultaneously creates refundable ITCs.

  • B13A Export Declaration - filed electronically through the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) for shipments valued over CAD 2,000; the LP's BN and 6-digit HS classification (e.g., 8429.52 for hydraulic excavators) must be recorded
  • Commercial invoice including detailed equipment description, serial numbers, HS tariff classification, sale price, incoterms, and buyer/seller BN or registration numbers
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical departure of the machinery from Canada, with vessel name, port of exit, and container/seal identifiers
  • Freight forwarder certificate or carrier certificate of export confirming delivery at the foreign destination, as accepted under CRA GST/HST Memorandum 7-2
  • SWIFT MT103 wire transfer advice or Letter of Credit documentation evidencing receipt of sale proceeds from the non-resident buyer and supporting the consideration stated on the export declaration

Canada does not operate a mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or API-based invoice authorization regime. GST/HST registrants file returns electronically through CRA GST/HST NETFILE and remit via CRA My Payment. Invoices, whether paper or electronic, must contain the prescribed particulars (vendor name and BN, buyer name, description, tax amount) and be retained in readable form for six years under ETA s.286. Electronic invoices are accepted as source documents; the federal government supports voluntary Peppol BIS 3.0 e-invoicing through the openPeppol network for federal suppliers, but no private B2B e-invoicing mandate applies to an Alberta LP exporting heavy machinery.

CRA may reassess GST/HST within four years of the assessment date, and indefinitely in cases of fraud, wilful misrepresentation, or failure to file. If export zero-rating is disallowed, the LP is assessed the uncollected 5% GST on the full selling price plus compounded daily arrears interest, and is exposed to: the failure-to-remit penalty under ETA s.280; the false statement/omission penalty under ETA s.285.1 of up to 50% of the tax sought to be evaded; and criminal prosecution under ETA s.327 for tax evasion, carrying fines up to 200% of the evaded amount and imprisonment up to five years. The clawback applies retrospectively to input tax credits claimed on the machinery, freight, and refurbishment costs and to any partnership allocations; partners, and in particular the general partner, are jointly and severally liable for the LP's GST/HST net tax under ETA s.272.1(2), and non-resident partners remain taxable on their allocated export income subject to treaty relief. If the export price is understated, the transfer-pricing penalty under ITA s.247(3) of 10% of the adjustment (minimum CAD 5,000) applies. Failure to satisfy the 180-day registrant-recipient export window converts the supply to taxable status, triggering full GST reassessment with interest and penalties for the entire consideration.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully floating and freely convertible legal tender regime. The Currency Act, the Bank of Canada Act, and the Financial Administration Act impose no exchange-control licensing, no central-bank conversion approvals, and no restrictions on capital or current account transactions. The Canadian dollar is freely convertible into any foreign currency at market rates. Capital injections by non-resident limited partners, reinvestment of trading profits, principal repayments, and distributions to non-resident partners may be remitted without exchange-control permission. The practical hurdles are limited to: (i) Part XIII non-resident withholding tax under the Income Tax Act on passive payments such as interest, rents, royalties, and management fees; (ii) FINTRAC mandatory reporting of electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more, and of physical cross-border currency movements of CAD 10,000 or more, under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act; (iii) CRA information reporting on the T5013 Partnership Information Return to properly allocate income among Canadian and non-resident partners; and (iv) standard banking due-diligence documentation supporting the commercial purpose of large USD/CAD wires for wholesale machinery trading. Profit remittance by an Alberta limited partnership is otherwise unrestricted.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender obligation, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no repatriation timeline. An Alberta limited partnership engaged in wholesale remarketing of used hydraulic earthmovers and rollers may hold export receivables in USD, EUR, or any other freely convertible currency in a Canadian Schedule I bank account or in foreign correspondent accounts. There is no requirement to convert export receipts to CAD, to deposit them with the Bank of Canada, or to surrender any portion to the central bank. The sole legal duties are: (i) FINTRAC reporting for large-value cross-border transfers at the CAD 10,000 threshold; (ii) Canada Border Services Agency export declarations (B13A and the Canadian Export Reporting System) for used construction equipment under ISIC 4659 / NAICS 423810; (iii) CRA reporting of foreign income and partnership allocations; and (iv) ordinary transactional documentation required by the clearing bank. None of these duties involves foreign-exchange conversion or surrender.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); prudential banking regulation by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing surveillance by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); payments clearing and settlement infrastructure operated by Payments Canada (Lynx for large-value transfers and the Real-Time Rail for retail).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-Low as a supplemental payment rail and Low as a substitute for a CDIC-insured primary banking relationship. Legal constraints: an offshore digital EMI (e.g., Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer) operating in Canada must register with FINTRAC as a money services business and money transmitter, and must maintain an agency or clearing arrangement with a Canadian Schedule I bank to move funds into and out of Canada. Without such registration and clearing partnership, holding an Alberta LP's funds in an offshore EMI creates an AML compliance gap incompatible with a Medium-Risk-Enhanced commercial profile. EMI platforms may be used for collecting small deposits from overseas buyers of used excavators and for settling telematics or freight invoices, provided all balances are swept to the primary Canadian operational account in accordance with the internal treasury policy. The LP's ultimate beneficial owners remain subject to identical CDD/EDD standards at the underlying Canadian clearing bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant capital injection routing requires: (i) each non-resident limited partner or its home bank initiates a SWIFT MT103 (or MT202 for interbank settlement) to the LP's segregated CAD/USD operational account at the chosen Alberta clearing bank, quoting the LP's legal name, account number, and a clear credit reference such as 'LP capital contribution - partner X'; (ii) for any amount at or above CAD 10,000, the sending bank's FINTRAC-compliant EFT report is attached to the credit file; (iii) the receiving bank verifies the remitter against the LP's capital contribution schedule and records the credit to the appropriate partner's capital account in the partnership register; (iv) for contributions originating from jurisdictions with enhanced scrutiny, the LP must supply the underlying purchase or sale documentation to satisfy source-of-funds review; (v) any intercompany loan injection from the general partner or an affiliate must be documented by a promissory note, an interest-bearing term sheet, and a subordination agreement to avoid CRA Part XIII withholding on interest; (vi) capital injections must never be credited to an account held in a third party's name, and each wire must trace to the exact legal entity holding the partnership interest. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium-Low as a supplemental payment rail and Low as a substitute for a CDIC-insured primary banking relationship. Legal constraints: an offshore digital EMI (e.g., Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer) operating in Canada must register with FINTRAC as a money services business and money transmitter, and must maintain an agency or clearing arrangement with a Canadian Schedule I bank to move funds into and out of Canada. Without such registration and clearing partnership, holding an Alberta LP's funds in an offshore EMI creates an AML compliance gap incompatible with a Medium-Risk-Enhanced commercial profile. EMI platforms may be used for collecting small deposits from overseas buyers of used excavators and for settling telematics or freight invoices, provided all balances are swept to the primary Canadian operational account in accordance with the internal treasury policy. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: ITA s.247 contemporaneous documentation required for all non-arm's length cross-border transactions; CRA expects documentation where annual aggregate exceeds C$1,000,000 (goods/services) or C$50,000 (intangibles); no prescribed Local/Master File filing but Canada-located multinationals with consolidated group revenue ≥ C$750,000,000 must file CbCR (Form RC4649 / XML via GMS for foreign parent groups)

Economic substance scope: No standalone Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) in Canada. Substance is tested through: (a) GAAR under ITA s.245 (misuse/abuse and economic substance as central indicator); (b) s.247(2) transfer-pricing recharacterization where arrangements lack commercial rationality; (c) treaty entitlement—Canada-US limitation-on-benefits clause (Art. XXIX-A) and general anti-abuse provisions under Art. 29(8)/(9) of the OECD Model-based treaties; (d) Alberta partnership law requiring genuine business activity—limited partners taking part in control become personally liable (Partnership Act RSA 2000 c P-3 s.19), and corporate general partners must maintain real 'mind and management' in Alberta or risk non-residency and loss of treaty access

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: ITA s.239(1): fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded; summary conviction imprisonment up to 2 years, or indictment imprisonment up to 5 years; gross negligence penalty under s.163(2) equal to 50% of understated tax; CRA may unseal bank accounts and seize assets

Director personal liability: Criminal liability attaches to the person directing the scheme (officers, corporate general partner directors, and actual controlling minds); civil restitution plus travel bans possible under the Canada Revenue Agency enforcement program; conviction carries confiscation order and prohibition from acting as company director
Unremitted Withholding / Part XIII (Shell LP)

Statutory sanction: ITA s.227(9): 10% failure-to-withhold penalty (20% for gross negligence); interest charged daily; CRA can assess the general partner personally for the unremitted amount; refusal to file T5013 penalties up to C$10,000

Director personal liability: Under ITA s.227.1, directors of a corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable for the LP's unremitted withholdings; Alberta Partnership Act s.19 converts a limited partner into a general partner with unlimited liability once the limited partner participates in control; CRA may seek civil judgment and garnish cross-border assets
Transfer-Pricing Non-Compliance and Loss of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: ITA s.247(3) penalty of 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment; adjustments to income plus arrears interest at prescribed rate; CRA may recharacterize the transaction under s.247(2) and treat the trading entity as having no PE in Canada for treaty purposes

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate partner exposed to joint liability for the unpaid penalty where the corporation has been used as a façade for the non-resident principal; Canadian courts will pierce the veil where the LP is a mere agent or sham; treaty relief denied for abusive arrangements, exposing the full 25% withholding on distributions
AML/Financial Transaction Reporting (FINTRAC) Breach

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: administrative monetary penalties up to C$500,000 (first) and C$1,000,000 (subsequent) for failures by non-MSB entities; knowing violation of reporting is punishable on summary conviction with fines up to C$500,000 and/or imprisonment up to 6 months; FINTRAC may freeze/forfeit the assets

Director personal liability: Officers and directors can be held personally liable for knowing participation in AML breaches; CRA and FINTRAC share intelligence, and wilful blindness toward proceeds of crime from cross-border excavator trading subjects general partners to money-laundering charges carrying up to 10 years' imprisonment (Criminal Code s.462.31) plus asset forfeiture
