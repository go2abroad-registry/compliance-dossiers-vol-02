Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Register the Limited Partnership under the Partnership Act (Alberta) at the Alberta Corporate Registry and obtain the LP registration certificate with its provincial registry number. Step 2: Apply to the CRA for a federal Business Number (BN) using Form RC1 or Business Registration Online; the BN is the statutory TIN and is composed of a 9-digit base plus program accounts (RT for GST/HST, RM for import/export, RP for payroll, RC for income tax). Step 3: Open the GST/HST (RT) program account; registration is mandatory once taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or across four consecutive calendar quarters; voluntary registration is advisable to claim input tax credits on aviation inventory and to zero-rate exports. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible - the Alberta Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent for federal and provincial income tax purposes: income, losses, and tax credits flow through to partners, and no entity-level income tax is imposed. Resident partners are taxed on worldwide income; non-resident partners are taxed on their Canadian-source allocable share. The LP is nonetheless a separate deemed person for GST/HST (Excise Tax Act), so it must register, collect, and remit GST on taxable Canadian supplies. No territorial exemption and no designated tax-exempt zone apply.

Available election forms

No federal check-the-box regime exists in Canada. Applicable elections for this LP include: (i) GST/HST group registration election under Excise Tax Act s. 149, permitting a representative member to file and remit for the group; (ii) partnership fiscal-period election under ITA s. 249.1(4), including special rules where any partner is a non-resident; (iii) voluntary GST/HST registration election while below the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold; (iv) for U.S. federal tax purposes, IRS Form 8832 check-the-box election, as a Canadian Limited Partnership is an eligible entity under U.S. Treasury regulations and may elect partnership or association status, subject to recharacterization under the Canada-U.S. Tax Convention and Canada's BEPS Action 2 hybrid and reverse-hybrid mismatch rules.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies made in Canada during a single calendar quarter or four consecutive calendar quarters (the small-supplier threshold); the same threshold applies to non-residents carrying on business in Canada; voluntary registration is available and, once elected, generally binds the registrant for at least one year.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

The registered LP may zero-rate: (i) supplies of surplus civilian aircraft rotables and avionics that are exported from Canada by the supplier, or delivered to a common carrier for export as part of the supply, under Excise Tax Act Schedule VI Part V (export provisions), provided the goods leave Canada within a commercially reasonable period (presumptively 60 days) and title passes to a non-resident recipient; and (ii) qualifying services supplied to non-residents (for example, offshore aviation spares brokering and logistics administration) under Schedule VI Part V s. 7, subject to statutory carve-outs for services performed on goods physically situated in Canada. Zero-rating is substantiation-dependent: failure to retain contemporaneous export documentary evidence reclassifies the supply as taxable at 5% GST, exposing the LP to assessed output tax, interest, and penalties.

  • Customs Export Declaration - CBSA B13A / Canadian Export Reporting System (CES) electronic export record with unique transaction reference
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical export movement of the surplus aircraft parts from Canada to the foreign consignee
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or equivalent bank wire / foreign-exchange credit confirmation evidencing collection of export sale proceeds

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, API pre-clearance, or mandatory third-party invoice validation exists in Canada. Suppliers must issue invoices containing the prescribed information under Excise Tax Act s. 223, including the supplier name, Business Number, supply description, consideration, tax rate and amount, and invoice date. Electronic filing of GST/HST returns is mandatory for registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 1 million; CRA validates data ex post through My Business Account, GST/HST NETFILE, and EDI. Exports are reported through the Canada Border Services Agency's Canadian Export Reporting System (CES) rather than any fiscal stamping regime.

CRA exporter-review programs scrutinize the export documentation bundle, the sales contract, the timing of physical export, and the payment lineage. Missing or inconsistent proof of export triggers reassessment within the standard four-year statutory period (extended to seven years in cases of misrepresentation or negligence), reversal of GST/HST refunds and input tax credits, statutory interest, a gross-negligence penalty of 25% of the net tax amount under Excise Tax Act s. 280 for false statements or omissions, and, for fraudulent conduct, criminal prosecution under Excise Tax Act s. 329 offence provisions carrying fines of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years on indictment.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible. Canada accepts IMF Article VIII obligations; current and capital account transfers, including commercial payments for surplus aircraft parts and non-resident profit remittances, are not subject to exchange permits or surrender requirements. Practical impediments include Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on limited partnership distributions (typically 25% unless reduced under a tax treaty such as the Canada-U.S. treaty), CRS/FATCA reporting obligations, and enhanced financial transaction reporting for wire transfers at or above CAD 10,000.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada does not impose export proceeds surrender rules or mandatory conversion ratios. An Alberta limited partnership engaged in export trade may invoice in USD, hold foreign currency proceeds in Canadian or offshore USD accounts, and choose when to convert to CAD. All foreign-currency earnings remain taxable in Canada regardless of conversion timing; transfer pricing and attribution rules apply to the limited partnership and its investors.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); foreign exchange policy is otherwise administered through the federal Department of Finance Canada, FINTRAC transaction reporting, and Investment Canada Act review. No separate exchange control authority exists at the provincial level in Alberta.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium with legal constraints. Offshore digital EMI platforms are not authorized Canadian banks and cannot accept deposits unless their Canadian partner bank provides custodial settlement. In Canada, payment service providers must register under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) with the Bank of Canada and, if acting as a money services business, with FINTRAC. For aviation spare-parts treasury flows, an offshore EMI is viable only as an ancillary collection/FX processor behind a licensed Canadian chartered bank; the Alberta limited partnership should maintain primary capital and escrow accounts in a tier-1 domestic bank.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not itself subject to federal or Alberta corporate income tax. Canadian-resident corporate partners are generally subject to a combined federal and Alberta general corporate income tax rate of 23% on allocated taxable income, comprising 15% federal tax and 8% Alberta tax. Individual, trust, and non-resident partners are taxed under their applicable regimes.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The general Canadian Part XIII withholding tax rate is 25% on taxable dividends paid or credited to non-residents. An Alberta limited partnership generally does not pay dividends; distributions are normally partnership distributions. Withholding may nevertheless apply to Canadian-source income allocated to or paid for a non-resident partner, depending on the income category and statutory rules.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Generally due within 5 months after the partnership's fiscal period-end. A partnership fiscal period generally cannot exceed 12 months. Partners must observe their own T1, T2, T3, withholding, and information-reporting deadlines.

Deadline: · Frequency:

The deadline depends on the assigned filing frequency and fiscal year. Annual filers generally file within 3 months after fiscal year-end where the filer is a corporation and within 3 months after year-end for other annual reporting cases, subject to statutory exceptions; monthly and quarterly returns are generally due one month after the reporting period.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no separate statutory Local File or Master File threshold in the OECD terminology. Contemporaneous documentation is required for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions under section 247 of the Income Tax Act. Form T106 generally applies where the aggregate value of reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 1,000,000 or other statutory filing conditions apply. Documentation should be prepared by the tax-return filing deadline and must substantiate the arm's-length nature of material transactions.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a standalone United Kingdom-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Canadian tax authorities may challenge arrangements under section 245 General Anti-Avoidance Rule, the specific anti-avoidance rules, transfer-pricing provisions, beneficial-ownership principles, sham doctrine, debt-versus-equity rules, and partnership anti-avoidance provisions. The LP should maintain Canadian commercial rationale, genuine business operations, inventory and title records, personnel or outsourced operational capacity, banking control, decision-making records, appropriate risk allocation, and arm's-length pricing.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of tax, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties, civil penalties, and criminal prosecution. Tax evasion under the Income Tax Act can result on indictment in imprisonment of up to 5 years and a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, in addition to the tax and interest.

Director personal liability: General partners and responsible officers may face criminal prosecution. Directors can face statutory derivative liability for certain unremitted amounts, including source deductions and GST/HST, where the statutory conditions are met. Civil restitution and asset-recovery orders may follow. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty but may arise through bail, criminal process, or enforcement orders.
Failure to File Partnership and Information Returns

Statutory sanction: Daily or monthly late-filing penalties, prescribed maximums, reassessment, loss of deductions or treaty positions, compliance orders, and potential prosecution for deliberate non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Individuals responsible for filing decisions may be assessed penalties where legislation applies and may be prosecuted for knowing or wilful failures. A limited partner is not automatically liable solely by reason of status, while a general partner or managing partner may have direct exposure.
Improper Transfer Pricing and Related-Party Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Primary and secondary transfer-pricing adjustments under section 247, interest, penalties of up to 10% of the applicable adjustment in qualifying cases, denial of deductions, withholding-tax exposure, and possible treaty competent-authority proceedings.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, and officers may face civil exposure where they knowingly participate in unlawful conduct, breach fiduciary duties, or authorize false filings. Personal criminal liability requires proof of the applicable offence and mental element.
Sham Partnership, Beneficial Ownership Abuse, or Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty reductions, withholding at 25%, reassessment under the General Anti-Avoidance Rule, interest, penalties, beneficial-ownership challenges, and potential prosecution for false statements or tax evasion.

Director personal liability: General partners and persons directing the arrangement may face civil claims, restitution, contribution claims, and criminal exposure where fraud or tax evasion is established. Limited liability does not protect a person from personal fraud or their own statutory offences.
AML, Sanctions, and Proceeds-of-Crime Exposure

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, violations may trigger administrative monetary penalties and criminal prosecution, including substantial fines and imprisonment. Sanctions violations under the Special Economic Measures Act or related legislation may result in significant fines and imprisonment, depending on the offence.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, compliance officers, and persons who authorize or facilitate transactions may be personally prosecuted. Assets may be restrained, seized, or forfeited. Travel restrictions may result from criminal charges, bail conditions, or court orders.
Aircraft Parts Export-Control and Certification Violations

Statutory sanction: Potential penalties under the Export and Import Permits Act, Controlled Goods regime, customs legislation, sanctions legislation, and aviation safety laws, including administrative action, licence suspension, seizure, fines, and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Officers, directors, general partners, and compliance personnel who authorize or knowingly permit unlawful exports may face personal criminal or regulatory liability. Corporate separateness does not shield deliberate participation, fraud, or obstruction.
Unremitted Source Deductions, GST/HST, or Part XIII Withholding

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unpaid amount, arrears interest, late-remittance penalties, failure-to-deduct penalties, denial of treaty relief, collection action, and possible prosecution for wilful non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for certain corporate source-deduction and GST/HST liabilities under statutory director-liability provisions if the statutory due-diligence defence is unavailable. General partners may have broader contractual and statutory exposure.
Corporate and Partnership Governance Failure

Statutory sanction: Loss or limitation of limited-liability protection, partnership disputes, civil damages, regulatory enforcement, tax reassessment, dissolution proceedings, and possible personal liability for obligations incurred during unauthorized conduct.

Director personal liability: A general partner is ordinarily exposed to partnership liabilities. A limited partner may lose protection for its own wrongful acts or where applicable law treats its conduct as participation in management. Directors of a corporate general partner may face personal liability for fraud, breach of duty, statutory offences, or knowing authorization of unlawful conduct.
