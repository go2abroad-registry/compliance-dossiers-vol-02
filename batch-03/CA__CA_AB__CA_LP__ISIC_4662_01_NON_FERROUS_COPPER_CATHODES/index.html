Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — GST/HST and Income Tax administration; CRA 'My Business Account' portal at canada.ca; export customs administered by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) via the Canadian Export Reporting System (CAERS).

E-government filing portal

Non-resident foreign entities obtain a federal Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Accounts) with the Canada Revenue Agency, either online through CRA's registration portal or by mail. For a limited partnership carrying on taxable supplies in Canada, the partnership itself registers as a 'person' for GST/HST using the BN and creates a GST/HST account (e.g., BN 123456789 RT0001); the registration application must disclose partner information, deemed residency, and a Canadian business address. Once processed, CRA issues the BN and program-specific account numbers for GST/HST, payroll, income tax, and other required programs.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — Non-resident foreign entities obtain a federal Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Accounts) with the Canada Revenue Agency, either online through CRA's registration portal or by mail. For a limited partnership carrying on taxable supplies in Canada, the partnership itself registers as a 'person' for GST/HST using the BN and creates a GST/HST account (e.g., BN 123456789 RT0001); the registration application must disclose partner information, deemed residency, and a Canadian business address. Once processed, CRA issues the BN and program-specific account numbers for GST/HST, payroll, income tax, and other required programs.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists in Canadian federal tax law; a limited partnership is statutorily classified as pass-through for income tax purposes, with each partner reporting its share of income directly. Partnerships may elect a non-calendar taxation year-end under subsection 249.1(7) of the Income Tax Act. For GST/HST, 'small suppliers' may elect to register voluntarily under section 240 of the Excise Tax Act, and non-resident partnerships may elect to use a simplified non-resident GST/HST registration procedure where applicable. Hybrid mismatch rules apply to cross-border arrangements but do not create entity-classification elections.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (gross worldwide taxable supplies from Canada over four consecutive calendar quarters; exceeds the small supplier threshold triggers mandatory GST/HST registration).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of tangible personal property (refined copper cathodes) are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act when the supplier exports the goods or causes them to be exported as part of the supply, with title passing outside Canada and evidence of physical export. Related services supplied directly to a non-resident (e.g., trading, shipping, brokerage) may also be zero-rated under Part VII of Schedule VI when the services are performed for and benefit a non-resident person. The seller must maintain contemporaneous documentation linking the supply to exportation and the non-resident recipient.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A) submitted via CAERS
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / verified bank confirmation of foreign-currency receipt
  • Commercial Invoice
  • Packing List
  • Customs release documents and proof of exit from Canada (e.g., port manifest, carrier certificate)

Canada does not mandate real-time e-invoicing clearance or fiscal digital stamping. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically through CRA's GST/HST NETFILE service. B2B e-invoicing follows the voluntary Pan-Canadian standard (no government clearance gateway). Export declarations are submitted electronically to CBSA through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CAERS), and commercial invoices/records must be retained for six years; no procedural fiscal invoice stamping is required.

CRA may reassess zero-rating refunds and untaxed export transactions within the normal four-year limitation period, or longer in cases of misrepresentation or fraud. False zero-rating claims attract penalties under section 285 of the Excise Tax Act: a minimum C$100 and 5% of the overstated amount (25% on subsequent or grossly negligent claims), plus interest. Wilful evasion can lead to criminal prosecution under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act, fines of 50% to 200% of tax evaded, and imprisonment. CRA audit programs specifically target base-metal circular trading and round-tripping; exporters must retain substantiating documentation for six years, and clawback may include uncollected GST/HST, penalties, and compound interest.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is a fully convertible, freely floating currency. Canada maintains no exchange-control legislation for capital or current account transactions: the statutory Foreign Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951, and the Currency Act subsequent revisions eliminated all surrender, licensing, and prior-approval mechanisms. Inbound partner capital contributions, repayment of partner loans, foreign trade payments, and the remittance of trading profits to non-resident partners of the Alberta Limited Partnership require no Bank of Canada or government authorization. Practical remittance hurdles are limited to (i) Canada Revenue Agency Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on certain Canadian-source distributions, interest, royalties, or management fees; (ii) FINTRAC reporting of every electronic funds transfer of CAD 10,000 or more within a 24-hour period; and (iii) Bank of Canada balance-of-payments statistical reporting collected by the receiving bank under the International Transactions Reporting System (ITRS). Copper cathode wholesale arbitrage is classified as a trading activity, not a controlled monetary transaction; no license is required for the underlying physical trade.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. There is no statutory timeline for repatriation and no mandatory conversion ratio for export proceeds. The Alberta LP may retain copper cathode export receipts in CAD or any foreign currency indefinitely. For documentary compliance, the LP should retain sales contracts, bills of lading, weight/quality certificates, country-of-origin evidence, and payment instructions for a minimum of five years for CRA and FINTRAC audit readiness; cross-border electronic transfers of CAD 10,000 or more triggered FINTRAC reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) and its associated regulations.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), exercising monetary and financial-system oversight, with operational anti-money-laundering/EFT surveillance conducted by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) and prudential bank regulation by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI). The Bank of Canada does not act as an exchange-control approval authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: MEDIUM as a supplemental payment-rail provider; LOW as the sole or primary banking layer. Offshore digital EMI and banking-as-a-service platforms may be used as foreign-currency concentration agents, trade-payment conduits, or inbound collection accounts, but they do not hold Canadian Schedule I or Schedule II bank status; they cannot maintain insured CAD settlement accounts, cannot clear through Payments Canada LTSS/Large Value Transfer System (LVTS), and cannot issue LME-accepted bank guarantees or standby letters of credit. Legal constraints: (i) the term bank is restrictive under the Bank Act S.C. 1991, c. 46, and any EMI marketing itself as a bank in Canada is exposed to regulatory action; (ii) a foreign EMI remitting funds into Canada must, where applicable, register with FINTRAC as a foreign money services business and report EFTs over CAD 10,000; (iii) the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) regime, administered by the Bank of Canada, now requires all payment service providers to register, maintain settlement funds in statutory trust accounts, and adhere to operational risk and incident reporting rules; and (iv) for the Grade A copper cathode arbitrage model, documentary collections and LME warrant transfers demand a regulated chartered bank as clearing and escrow counterparty.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital contribution from a non-resident partner to the Alberta LP's operational account should be executed via SWIFT FIN MT103, with the beneficiary account titled in the exact LP legal name and the remittance information field (field 70) stating: CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION TO [LP NAME] UNDER LP AGREEMENT DATED [DATE]; where the injection is simultaneously funding a copper cathode purchase, the ISO 20022 purpose code GOOD may be used, while a pure equity injection should use the purpose code EQUI or the bank-specified direct-investment code. The receiving Canadian bank, acting as an ITRS reporting institution, will require the remitter's full legal name, jurisdiction, and account number, the LP's Alberta Corporate Registry registration number, and a board/general-partner resolution authorizing the contribution. Cross-border EFTs of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported to FINTRAC under SOR/2002-184, but no pre-approval, license, or conversion requirement applies. Funds received in USD or EUR may be retained in a foreign-currency sub-account without conversion, and any conversion to CAD is executed at commercial rates through the clearing bank's FX desk.Trade settlements are structured across four primary rails: (i) SWIFT FIN MT103 for priority, low-to-mid value wire payments covering freight, inspection fees, and payment against goods; (ii) Documentary Letters of Credit (MT700, MT707, MT798) for large-value copper cathode imports/exports where the LP's bank issues an irrevocable LC in favor of the seller, with payment against presentation of B/L, warehouse receipt, weight certificate, and certificate of origin; (iii) LME warrant transfers executed through the LME's LMEsmart electronic title registry, supported by a SWIFT MT798/MT400 bank undertaking and a payment-vs-transfer matching instruction at the clearing bank's trade finance desk; and (iv) domestic CAD settlement through Payments Canada's Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) and USD settlement via Fedwire or CHIPS where the counterparty is US-based. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility Rating: MEDIUM as a supplemental payment-rail provider; LOW as the sole or primary banking layer. Offshore digital EMI and banking-as-a-service platforms may be used as foreign-currency concentration agents, trade-payment conduits, or inbound collection accounts, but they do not hold Canadian Schedule I or Schedule II bank status; they cannot maintain insured CAD settlement accounts, cannot clear through Payments Canada LTSS/Large Value Transfer System (LVTS), and cannot issue LME-accepted bank guarantees or standby letters of credit. Legal constraints: (i) the term bank is restrictive under the Bank Act S.C. 1991, c. 46, and any EMI marketing itself as a bank in Canada is exposed to regulatory action; (ii) a foreign EMI remitting funds into Canada must, where applicable, register with FINTRAC as a foreign money services business and report EFTs over CAD 10,000; (iii) the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) regime, administered by the Bank of Canada, now requires all payment service providers to register, maintain settlement funds in statutory trust accounts, and adhere to operational risk and incident reporting rules; and (iv) for the Grade A copper cathode arbitrage model, documentary collections and LME warrant transfers demand a regulated chartered bank as clearing and escrow counterparty.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined federal + Alberta general corporate rate (15% federal plus 8% Alberta); a limited partnership is fiscally transparent, so no entity-level corporate tax applies to the LP itself.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, reduced by applicable tax treaties: generally 5% for corporate shareholders holding at least 10% voting control and 15% for portfolio dividends.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000: mandatory T106 disclosure and contemporaneous Local File documentation for aggregate non-arm's-length cross-border transactions; CAD 1,000,000,000 consolidated group revenue triggers Country-by-Country Report and Master File obligations.

Economic substance scope: Canada applies the General Anti-Avoidance Rule under ITA s. 245, common law sham doctrine, and substance-over-form jurisprudence; a limited partnership in the copper cathode wholesale trade must demonstrate genuine activity, substantive decision-making, real employees or agents, physical trading functions, and commercial purpose, otherwise the CRA may disregard the entity or re-characterize transactions.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: ITA s. 239(1): fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to two years; gross negligence civil penalty under ITA s. 163(2) of 50% of the understatement plus compound daily interest.

Director personal liability: A general partner or director of the corporate general partner may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted withholdings under ITA s. 227.1; criminal prosecution can trigger travel suspensions, asset forfeiture, and director disqualification.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA reassessment under GAAR denying the expected tax benefit, plus penalties up to 50% of the benefit, interest, and recalculated taxable income; sham doctrine may void the partnership arrangement retrospectively.

Director personal liability: Limited partners may lose limited liability protection under Alberta's Partnership Act if they take part in control of the partnership; the general partner remains personally liable for all partnership obligations and may be sued for fraudulent conveyances or preference.
AML / Money Laundering

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act penalties up to CAD 500,000 for corporations and CAD 100,000 for individuals per violation; Criminal Code s. 462.31 money laundering offence carries imprisonment up to 10 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance officers may face criminal liability where they knowingly direct, authorize, or fail to prevent laundering; FINTRAC can issue enforcement orders, freeze assets, and impose travel bans in conjunction with criminal investigations.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: ITA s. 247(3) penalty equal to 10% of the transfer pricing adjustment amount where the adjustment exceeds CAD 250,000 or 10% of gross revenues; aggregate adjustments plus attributable interest; refusal to produce documentation shifts evidentiary burden to the taxpayer.

Director personal liability: Directors of the general partner may be liable for knowing participation in deficient documentation or false certificates; CRA may trace to personal assets in egregious cases and seek restitution for unpaid corporate taxes.
Failure to Withhold Part XIII Tax

Statutory sanction: ITA s. 215(6): the payer is personally liable for the required withholding; penalty under ITA s. 227(8) of 10% of the amount not remitted plus daily arrears interest and potential 20% penalty on repeated failures.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers of the corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable under ITA s. 227.1 for unremitted amounts; CRA can issue director's liability assessments, garnishments, and travel prohibition until the liability is resolved.
