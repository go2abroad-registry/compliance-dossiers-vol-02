Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — federal income tax, GST/HST and excise tax administration; portal: canada.ca/taxes and My Business Account (CRA e-services). Provincial corporate registry: Service Alberta Corporate Registry (corporate-registry.servicealberta.ca). Customs and export controls: Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) via the Canadian International Trade Single Window (SWI) and the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — Under the Income Tax Act (Canada), an Alberta limited partnership is fiscally transparent: the LP does not pay entity-level income tax; income is computed at partnership level and allocated to partners (ITA ss. 96–103). Non-resident partners are taxable under ITA Part I on their share of income from a business carried on in Canada (ITA s. 115), subject to treaty relief (e.g., Canada–US Treaty Art. VII/PE rules). For GST/HST purposes the LP is a separate 'person' under ETA s. 123(1) and must register, charge, collect, and claim ITCs in its own capacity. Alberta does not impose provincial corporate capital tax on partnerships; commercial activity within Alberta is subject to the 5% federal GST with no provincial sales tax.

Available election forms

No US-style 'check-the-box' election exists in Canadian tax law; entity classification follows legal form and CRA administrative policy. Available elections include: (i) fiscal-period election for a partnership under ITA s. 249.1(6)/(7); (ii) the hybrid-mismatch deemed-dividend election under ITA s. 18.2(3), which treats a hybrid payment as a dividend subject to deemed Part XIII withholding so as to preserve deduction where the recipient is subject to sufficient foreign tax; (iii) elections connected to the Excessive Interest Financing Expense Limitation (EIFEL, ITA ss. 18.2(1), 18.4–18.6) for financial institutions and qualified collaborative investment vehicles; and (iv) voluntary GST/HST registration under ETA s. 240(1) once the small-supplier threshold is not exceeded.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — small-supplier threshold (ETA s. 240(1)) measured on annual worldwide taxable supplies of the non-resident partnership made in Canada; registration is mandatory once exceeded and voluntary below the threshold to claim input tax credits on export procurement inputs.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods: zero-rated where the vendor exports tangible personal property from Canada continuously and without let-up (ETA Schedule VI, s. 1), including deliveries made in Canada to a common carrier for export; documentation must evidence physical export. Services: zero-rated where the supply is made to a non-resident person and the service is not primarily for consumption/use in Canada and does not relate to real property in Canada (Schedule VI, s. 7); procurement/brokering commissions on international battery-grade lithium carbonate transactions will qualify where the non-resident principal receives the benefit abroad and retains risk and title. Imported goods into Canada remain subject to 5% GST on importation, recoverable as an ITC by the registered LP; the participating interest in an arbitrage trade does not change the zero-rating status of the underlying exported goods or offshore service.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A filed via CBSA CERS within the Canadian International Trade Single Window; required for regulated goods or shipments above CAD 2,000, and for export-controlled battery materials)
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill (transport evidence demonstrating continuous physical export from Canada)
  • Bank Inward Remittance Confirmation (SWIFT MT103/MT202 or broker statement) — Canada issues no statutory 'Inward Remittance Certificate'; cleared foreign-currency payment advice is the CRA-recognized equivalent.

Canada has no real-time fiscal clearance, API digital stamping, or mandatory e-invoicing clearance regime. CRA accepts electronically issued invoices and electronic books/records (ETA ss. 286, 286.1). GST/HST registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 1 million must file GST/HST returns electronically via GST/HST NETFILE or EDI. Pan-Canadian e-invoicing follows the voluntary Peppol framework (Pan-Canadian B2B E-Invoicing Standard, dual 'PEPPOL BIS' and 'Pan-Canadian' syntax support); Alberta imposes no additional provincial e-invoicing mandate.

CRA assessment window: 4 years from the claim date (ETA s. 298(1)(a)); extended to 7 years for neglect/carelessness misrepresentation (ETA s. 298(1)(c)); unlimited for fraud. An overstated zero-rating claim triggers reassessment of GST at 5% plus prescribed-rate interest and penalties, and pro-rata clawback of related input tax credits where export documents (B13A/CERS declaration, bills of lading, SWIFT remittances) are not produced. Penalty exposure: ETA s. 280 — minimum CAD 250, up to 25% of the net tax sought to be evaded; ETA s. 327 — criminal evasion (fine up to 200% of evaded tax, up to 5 years imprisonment on indictment); ETA s. 286 — CAD 100/day for missing books and records (minimum CAD 250, maximum CAD 2,500). Canada imposes no statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation rule; the 180-day benchmark reflected in this dossier is the agreed treasury/letter-of-credit settlement cycle, and inward remittance records act as evidentiary corroboration rather than a condition of zero-rating.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada offers full current- and capital-account convertibility consistent with IMF Article VIII. No general exchange controls apply to lithium carbonate battery-grade imports, export proceeds, foreign-direct-investment inflows, or profit remittances. Non-resident LP distributions may be freely converted to CAD or foreign currency through Canadian-chartered banks, subject only to Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and standard FINTRAC reporting thresholds. No central-bank approval, conversion restriction, or repatriation permit is required.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export proceeds surrender or conversion ratios. Exporters and trading entities may retain, invoice, and disburse foreign currency proceeds freely outside Canada or in Canadian bank accounts. There is no mandatory repatriation timeline or obligation to convert export receipts into CAD.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) – Canada has no separate foreign exchange control authority; exchange rate and monetary policy are administered by the Bank of Canada under the Bank of Canada Act, and foreign exchange regulatory powers rest with the Department of Finance Canada and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for anti-money-laundering monitoring.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium feasibility with legal constraints. Canada permits regulated fintech payment service providers and electronic money institutions to operate as money services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC rather than as banks. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g. Wise, Airwallex) may provide multi-currency account and cross-border payment rails via partnership with Canadian authorized banks, but such non-bank entities do not hold Canadian bank deposits or CDIC insurance. For a medium-risk lithium-carbonate arbitrage business, an EMI-only solution is legally viable but operationally discouraged due to enhanced due-diligence scrutiny, deposit protection limits, and lack of commercial credit lines. Primary banking relationships with tier-1 Canadian banks are strongly preferred; EMIs may serve as secondary payment corridors for low-value disbursements.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal Part I general corporate rate 15.0% plus Alberta general corporate rate 8.0% = 23.0% combined for corporate partners; non-resident corporate partners are additionally subject to Part XIV branch tax of 25.0% on Alberta-source allocated business income, treaty-reduced to 5.0% under Canada-U.S. DTA Article X(6) where the corporate partner owns at least 10.0% of the underlying entity.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25.0% under Part XIII on dividends paid by a Canadian corporation to non-resident shareholders; for the LP structure, distributions of active business income to non-resident partners are not Part XIII dividends but are taxable under Part I on the allocated share, followed by Part XIV branch tax at 25.0% for non-resident corporate partners, treaty-reduced to 5.0% under Canada-U.S. DTA Article X(6) where the corporate partner owns at least 10.0% of the Canadian operation.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not prescribe a fixed low-value de minimis threshold for Local File or Master File; under s.247(4), the 10.0% penalty applies where the transfer-pricing adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 and 10.0% of gross revenue, unless the taxpayer made reasonable efforts and prepared contemporaneous documentation by the filing due date; mandatory Country-by-Country filing and notification apply to multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least CAD 750,000,000 under s.233.8.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply the Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR); instead, CRA enforces anti-avoidance and anti-conduit doctrines through GAAR (s.245), the beneficial ownership requirement for treaty relief, common law sham doctrine, and the central management and control residence test. An Alberta LP that exists only as a mailbox with all commercial decisions made offshore, and lacking staff, premises, and management functions in Alberta, risks treaty-benefit denial, recharacterization as a conduit, and Part XIII withholding on gross distributions.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Wilful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Under ITA s.239, conviction on indictment attracts a fine of up to 200.0% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to 5 years; summary conviction carries a fine up to CAD 100,000 and imprisonment up to 2 years; CRA may additionally impose a gross negligence penalty of 50.0% of the understated tax under s.163(2) and extend the reassessment window to 6 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers of the general partner who signed false returns are personally liable for the unremitted corporate tax under s.227.1; CRA may issue a Ministerial travel-restriction request where arrears exceed CAD 25,000, freeze bank accounts, and require civil restitution; the Criminal Code further permits proceeds-of-crime restraint orders against personal assets.
Transfer Pricing and Base Erosion

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under s.247(2) to the arm's-length CUP or TNMM margin and apply a 10.0% penalty on the net adjustment exceeding the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 and 10.0% of gross revenue; interest at the prescribed rate accrues from the original filing due date.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve or sign false transfer-pricing documentation face s.163.2 penalties of up to CAD 100,000 for misrepresentation, joint and several liability for the corporate penalty under s.227.1 where the corporation fails to pay, and potential criminal prosecution for tax evasion under s.239(2).
Shell Company and Economic Substance Abuse

Statutory sanction: GAAR under s.245 permits CRA to deny treaty benefits, recharacterize the partnership income as Part XIII dividends or royalties, and assess arrears interest plus a 10.0% penalty on the avoided tax; sham doctrine may void the partnership contract and attribute income directly to the non-resident principal.

Director personal liability: A limited partner who participates in the control or management of the LP loses its limited-liability shield under the Alberta Partnership Act and becomes personally liable for the partnership's Canadian tax obligations; the general partner's directors face civil liability for the full assessed tax, restitution of distributions, and travel restrictions pending resolution.
AML and Beneficial Ownership Violations

Statutory sanction: Violations of the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act attract administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 2,200,000 for entities and CAD 1,100,000 for individuals, plus additional penalties of up to 1.0% of the transaction value; Criminal Code s.462.31 money laundering carries imprisonment up to 10 years.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly structure transactions to avoid FINTRAC thresholds face personal criminal liability, asset restraint orders, travel bans under judicial authorization, disgorgement of profits, and joint and several liability for unpaid corporate fines; CRA also treats the LP as a reportable entity for CRS/FATCA purposes, and the officers of the general partner remain exposed to enforcement.
