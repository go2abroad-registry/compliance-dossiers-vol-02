Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no general United States-style check-the-box election for a Canadian limited partnership. A valid limited partnership is generally taxed as a partnership for Canadian income-tax purposes, with income, losses, and other fiscal amounts determined at the partnership level and allocated to partners under the Income Tax Act and the partnership agreement, subject to statutory allocation rules. Available mechanisms may include a section 96 partnership computation, a section 97(2) rollover for qualifying transfers of property to a Canadian partnership, and partnership agreement elections or designations where expressly authorized by statute. A partnership cannot generally elect to be treated as a Canadian corporation solely by filing a classification form. Foreign classification elections, including a U.S. check-the-box election, do not automatically control Canadian classification and may create cross-border hybrid-entity or hybrid-mismatch issues. Canadian anti-hybrid mismatch rules must be reviewed where foreign deductions, inclusions, or entity classifications produce a deduction/non-inclusion or duplicate-deduction outcome.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, including zero-rated supplies and subject to the statutory small-supplier aggregation rules, measured over a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters. The threshold does not apply in the same manner to every category of supplier, and specific rules apply to non-resident digital-economy suppliers, taxi or ride-sharing operators, charities, public institutions, and persons making only exempt supplies. A supplier may voluntarily register below the threshold if it carries on commercial activities and satisfies the registration requirements.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada are generally zero-rated at 0% GST when they are supplied in Canada and exported in accordance with the Exported Goods provisions of the Excise Tax Act, including delivery or shipment to a destination outside Canada and retention of evidence establishing export. For an Alberta cash-on-delivery e-commerce business, the supplier should retain the customer order, sales invoice, payment or collection record, carrier and fulfillment records, shipping address, tracking data, proof of delivery outside Canada, customs documentation where applicable, and evidence that the goods were not consumed, used, or supplied in Canada before export. Goods removed from Canada by a non-resident purchaser may qualify only where the statutory conditions and documentation requirements are met; merely stating a foreign delivery address is insufficient. Services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated only where a specific Excise Tax Act provision applies. The general exported-services rules are subject to exclusions and conditions, including services relating to real property situated in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada, services performed in respect of persons physically present in Canada, and other expressly excluded services. Export zero-rating does not eliminate customs duties, import GST, foreign import VAT, or destination-country tax obligations.

  • Canada Border Services Agency export declaration through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS), where required by the Customs Act and applicable export-reporting rules; commercial exports to the United States are subject to distinct reporting exceptions and controlled-goods rules.
  • Commercial invoice or equivalent electronic sales documentation identifying the exporter, purchaser or consignee, description, quantity, value, currency, origin, destination, and terms of sale.
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, courier manifest, postal record, or equivalent carrier document.
  • Proof of shipment and delivery outside Canada, including carrier tracking, delivery confirmation, warehouse release records, and fulfillment records.
  • Customer order, payment or cash-on-delivery settlement record, and records linking the order to the exported goods.
  • Import or customs-entry documentation in the destination country where available and relevant to the transaction.
  • Proof of export and GST accounting records sufficient to substantiate the zero-rated treatment during a CRA audit.
  • Inward remittance certificate: not generally a Canadian statutory requirement for GST zero-rating or ordinary export substantiation. Foreign-exchange evidence may nevertheless be retained where required by a financial institution, payment intermediary, financing arrangement, or destination-country law.

Canada has no general federal real-time GST invoice-clearance, mandatory electronic-invoicing, or continuous transaction-control regime for ordinary Alberta businesses. Registered suppliers must issue GST/HST invoices or other prescribed supporting documentation containing sufficient information to substantiate the consideration, GST/HST rate, tax amount, supplier identity, registration number where required, and applicable documentary details. Electronic invoices and electronic business records are generally acceptable if they preserve integrity, accessibility, readability, and auditability. CRA records must generally be retained for six years from the end of the relevant year, subject to longer periods or unresolved-objection and appeal rules. Certain public-sector procurement arrangements may impose separate electronic-invoicing specifications, but they do not constitute a universal tax-clearance mandate. Customs export reporting is separate from GST invoicing and may require electronic filing through the Canada Border Services Agency's Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS).

Canada generally imposes no universal statutory requirement to repatriate export proceeds within 180 days. A GST registrant must maintain reliable books and records and prove that each zero-rated supply satisfies the Excise Tax Act. CRA may reassess the supplier for GST that should have been charged, deny input tax credits or GST/HST rebates, impose arrears interest, and apply penalties for late filing, failure to remit, false statements, gross negligence, or repeated non-compliance. The gross-negligence penalty can generally be 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed amount, subject to the statutory rules. False statements, obstruction, destruction of records, or deliberate tax evasion may result in additional civil penalties or criminal prosecution, including fines and imprisonment. Incorrect customs declarations may trigger Canada Border Services Agency administrative monetary penalties, reassessment of duties and taxes, seizure or forfeiture consequences, and other enforcement measures. A zero-rating error is normally reassessed for the applicable statutory assessment period, subject to the ordinary limitation rules and extended or unlimited periods applicable to misrepresentation, fraud, or failure to file or keep required records.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully liberalized capital and current account regime. The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for residents and non-residents at market rates. No central bank approval is required for profit remittance, partnership distributions, or repatriation of capital, provided the transactions are not connected to sanctioned jurisdictions under the Special Economic Measures Act or the United Nations Act. For a Canadian limited partnership, distributions to non-resident partners are generally subject to Part XIII Canadian withholding tax (normally 25% unless reduced by an income tax treaty), but this is a tax compliance matter rather than a currency control or convertibility hurdle. For proving treaty entitlement, the non-resident partner must provide a valid Canada Revenue Agency form NR301, NR302, or NR303, as applicable. Mail-order retail operations with cash-on-delivery settlement carry no additional currency convertibility restriction.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada does not impose any mandatory surrender, repatriation timeline, or conversion ratio for export proceeds. Foreign currency export receipts may be retained in offshore accounts or sold on the interbank market freely. However, financial institutions must report cross-border electronic funds transfers of C$10,000 or more to FINTRAC within 24 hours, and any transaction physically transporting currency or monetary instruments over C$10,000 must be reported to the CBSA under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Customs export declarations are required for controlled or regulated goods, but no exchange-control surrender requirement exists.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with prudential supervision by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), AML/ATF transaction monitoring and wire-reporting enforcement by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), and residual trade/sanctions enforcement by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) and Global Affairs Canada. Exchange control policy is exercised by the Minister of Finance under the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act; Canada maintains no separate foreign-exchange licensing authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating for offshore digital electronic money institutions (EMIs) is Medium-High, but subject to strict legal constraints. Offshore EMIs (e.g., Airwallex, Windward, Currencycloud) may operate safely as payment initiation or collection service providers only when they are registered as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC and, where applicable, comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) once fully in force. A pure offshore EMI holding client funds in Canada without a Canadian bank partner and without FINTRAC registration is not legal. The trio of Airwallex, local logistics remittance providers, and a Canadian bank is permissible when the EMI merely facilitates collection through the bank's payment gateway and the LP is designated as the merchant of record. The legal contract must ensure that all cash-on-delivery funds are reconciled daily, subject to AML sanctions screening, and remitted to the LP's bank account via the EMI's Canadian bank settlement account. No statutory license is required for the LP itself, because ISIC 4791 mail-order retail is not a regulated financial activity; however any payment intermediary used must be appropriately licensed.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital contributions to the Alberta LP should be routed as a SWIFT MT103 corporate treasury payment. The bank instruction must use the LP's full legal name and Alberta corporate registry number (e.g., 'ABC CA LP / ARN 123456789'); the remitter should use Field 50K with full source account; the beneficiary in Field 59 must be the LP's own operating or capital escrow account. In Field 70 or a designated purpose code, include a clear description: 'LPK-2025-AB-001 capital contribution – ABC CA LP' and a correlation to the capital call notice. For cross-border wires over C$10,000, the sending financial institution must report the transfer to FINTRAC automatically; no separate filing by the LP is required, but the LP should retain the contribution agreement, capital call, and bank confirmation for audit. All inbound injections must be reconciled against the partner's capital ledger and, for non-resident partners, subjected to treaty entitlement review before distribution. For structuring, two-tranche routing is preferred: first a smaller validation transfer (C$5,000 or equivalent) to confirm banking details, then the full contribution via a cleared, pre-verified wire. Avoid any use of crypto or cross-border cash-courier transfers.Trade settlements for inventory, logistics, and COD remittances should be cleared through SWIFT MT103 for immediate cross-border supplier payments, using the purpose code 'Goods Purchase' or 'Commercial Trade Settlement' and referencing the invoice and purchase order number. For high-value or bulk purchases from emerging markets, an irrevocable, at-sight Commercial Letter of Credit (MT700) is permissible and recommended to reduce counterparty risk; the LC must incorporate a clean Incoterms 2020 rule (e.g., FOB/EXW) and require documents to conform exactly to the commercial invoice and CBSA import declaration. Domestic cash-on-delivery logistics remittances may be consolidated through an automated clearing house (ACH) or Interac e-Transfer for small values, but for larger aggregate amounts, use a LVTS (Large Value Transfer System) wire via the Tier-1 bank. For country-specific disallowed payment rails, every outbound transfer must be screened against Global Affairs Canada and OSFI sanctioned jurisdictions before execution. No statutory license is required for the LP itself, because ISIC 4791 mail-order retail is not a regulated financial activity; however any payment intermediary used must be appropriately licensed.
