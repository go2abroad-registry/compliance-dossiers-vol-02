Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; Business Registration Online portal via CRA 'My Business Account' at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/registering-your-business.html

E-government filing portal

Foreign limited partnership must apply to the CRA using Form RC1 Request for a Business Number, indicating the reason for registration (GST/HST account) and providing non-resident entity details. The CRA issues a 9-digit Business Number (BN) plus program account suffixes, e.g., RT0001 for GST/HST. If the LP will carry on business in Alberta, it must also file an extra-provincial limited partnership registration with Service Alberta under Alberta's Partnership Act, obtaining an Alberta registration number. A non-resident registrant should designate a Canadian representative for service of documents and compliance. No in-person filing is required; the process is completed electronically.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — Foreign limited partnership must apply to the CRA using Form RC1 Request for a Business Number, indicating the reason for registration (GST/HST account) and providing non-resident entity details. The CRA issues a 9-digit Business Number (BN) plus program account suffixes, e.g., RT0001 for GST/HST. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not have a check-the-box regime. Under subsection 102(1) of the Income Tax Act, a Canadian partnership whose members are all non-residents may elect for the partnership to be deemed not to be a Canadian partnership, altering Canadian filing obligations. For GST/HST, no entity-classification election is available; a partnership is generally treated as a separate person for GST/HST registration and remittance. Hybrid mismatch provisions in sections 18.2, 18.3 and 20.1 of the Income Tax Act follow legal form and do not provide elective reclassification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000 in worldwide gross revenue from taxable supplies made in Canada in the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters or in the current quarter; non-resident online sellers engaged by a digital platform may be deemed to be registered regardless of this threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A zero-rated supply of used luxury goods is available when tangible personal property is sold to a non-resident recipient and the goods are exported by the supplier, the recipient, or the recipient's agent in the course of shipping the goods to a destination outside Canada. The supplier must retain documentary evidence of export sufficient to satisfy the CRA, including customs, shipping and payment records, and the goods must be exported as soon as is reasonable after delivery.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A / Canadian Export Reporting System) and commercial invoice
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill evidencing physical export from Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank foreign-exchange credit advice demonstrating settlement from abroad

No mandatory real-time e-invoicing, fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or API-based clearance regime exists federally or in Alberta. CRA requires GST/HST returns to be filed electronically for registrants with taxable annual revenues above C$1.5 million, and all registrants are expected to retain detailed electronic records. Since July 1, 2021, digital platform operators are deemed to be the supplier for GST/HST purposes on retail sales made through their platforms, making them responsible for collection and remittance of GST/HST.

CRA may reassess within the normal four-year reassessment period (or later in cases of misrepresentation). If zero-rating is denied, the full 5% GST is clawed back with statutory interest. Civil penalties under the Excise Tax Act for false statements or omissions include the greater of C$100 and 25% of the tax at issue, and may be increased to 50% where the conduct is found to be gross negligence or wilful. Criminal prosecution for tax evasion may result in fines and imprisonment, and fraudulent export claims may also trigger denial of future zero-rating privileges.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has full current-account and capital-account convertibility for the Canadian dollar under the IMF Articles of Obligation. No exchange-control approval is required for inbound equity injections, outbound profit remittances, loan repayments, or trade settlements. Resident and non-resident investors face no statutory conversion limits or surrender requirements. The only binding constraints are regulatory in nature: FINTRAC reporting thresholds, sanctioned-jurisdiction screening, and tax compliance under the Income Tax Act (Canada), including Part XIII withholding on non-resident distributions where applicable.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender requirement applies in Canada. Canadian resident exporters and resellers may retain export proceeds in foreign currency indefinitely in domestic or offshore bank accounts. The Bank of Canada does not mandate conversion ratios, timelines, or repatriation of foreign-currency receivables. For operational clarity, the LP should maintain separate CAD and USD clearing accounts to optimize settlement and hedge timing.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); currency administration and AML/sanctions oversight are supported by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); Canada maintains no separate foreign-exchange control authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low-Medium. Foreign electronic money institutions and non-Canadian fintech payment platforms generally cannot act as primary deposit-taking treasury rails in Canada. They may provide payment facilitation, virtual IBANs, or gateway collection services only if compliant with Canadian anti-money-laundering regulations. A foreign EMI is not a substitute for a regulated Canadian Schedule I bank account when capital escrow, clearing, and LP statutory obligations are required. Payment service providers targeting Canadian merchants must register as MSBs with FINTRAC, maintain a Canadian compliance program, and typically cannot offer unrestricted multi-currency deposit accounts without a Canadian banking partner.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

The Alberta limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and does not itself pay Canadian income tax. Taxable income is allocated to the partners. For an Alberta-resident taxable Canadian corporation receiving allocated income, the general combined federal and Alberta corporate income tax rate is generally 23% (15% federal plus 8% Alberta). An eligible Alberta-controlled private corporation may generally access a combined small-business rate of approximately 11% on qualifying active business income within the applicable business limit, subject to federal and Alberta eligibility rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Canada's general Part XIII withholding tax rate on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is 25%, subject to an applicable income tax treaty. Common treaty outcomes include 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting the ownership threshold and 15% in other qualifying cases, including many portfolio situations. The exact rate depends on the treaty, beneficial ownership, ownership percentage, limitation provisions, and anti-avoidance rules.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T5013 is generally due within five months after the end of the partnership's fiscal period. A partnership fiscal period generally cannot exceed 12 months. T106 is generally due within six months after the end of the reporting taxpayer's taxation year. GST/HST returns are generally due one month after the end of a monthly or quarterly period; annual deadlines depend on the registrant's status and reporting period, commonly three months after year-end for a corporation and four months for other registrants, subject to CRA rules. Each partner's income tax return has a separate deadline based on the partner's legal status.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general automatic local-file threshold stated as a single transaction amount. Under Income Tax Act section 247, contemporaneous documentation supporting arm's-length pricing is required and must be provided to CRA within three months of a written request. T106 reporting generally applies where aggregate non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1,000,000 for the applicable reporting category or otherwise meet the prescribed filing conditions. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational enterprise groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million. A formal OECD-style master file is not universally required for every Canadian taxpayer, but CRA may require extensive group, transaction, and value-chain documentation.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a separate general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the regimes of certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is nevertheless relevant under the transfer-pricing arm's-length principle, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership requirements, corporate residence and central-management analysis, permanent-establishment rules, foreign accrual property income rules, and judicial anti-avoidance doctrines. A resale LP should maintain evidence of Canadian functions, inventory ownership or consignment rights, personnel, decision-making, warehousing, payment controls, customer contracts, valuation processes, and genuine commercial risk. A partner or intermediary lacking commercial substance may be challenged as a conduit or agency arrangement.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act offences can result in fines generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to two years. Criminal Code fraud charges may also apply, with substantially higher penalties, including imprisonment of up to 14 years for fraud exceeding CAD 5,000. Civil reassessment, interest, gross-negligence penalties, and loss of deductions may apply independently.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal criminal exposure for participation, authorization, or wilful blindness. Directors can be jointly and severally liable for unremitted GST/HST and source deductions, subject to statutory due-diligence defences. Ordinary corporate income tax is not automatically a director debt. A limited partner may lose limited-liability protection if it takes part in control or management under applicable Alberta partnership law, while the general partner remains exposed to partnership liabilities.
GST/HST Fraud and Trust-Fund Diversion

Statutory sanction: The Excise Tax Act permits assessments, interest, gross-negligence penalties, administrative monetary penalties, director liability for unremitted amounts, and criminal prosecution. Tax fraud convictions can produce fines and imprisonment, in addition to collection action against the registrant and responsible individuals.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted GST/HST unless they establish the statutory due-diligence defence. Responsible officers may face prosecution where conduct is knowing, reckless, or deliberately deceptive. CRA may garnish accounts, register liens, seize assets, and pursue collection against liable persons.
AML, Proceeds of Crime, and Suspicious Luxury-Goods Transactions

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act can impose FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, and publication of violations. Criminal Code money-laundering offences can result in imprisonment of up to 10 years for indictable offences. Terrorist-financing and sanctions violations may carry severe criminal and administrative consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors, partners, compliance officers, and beneficial owners may face individual criminal exposure where they direct, authorize, facilitate, or knowingly ignore prohibited conduct. Corporate separation does not protect a person from liability for personal participation, conspiracy, counselling, or proceeds-of-crime offences.
Shell Company, Nominee Ownership, and Beneficial-Ownership Concealment

Statutory sanction: Consequences include denial of treaty benefits, transfer-pricing adjustments, GAAR reassessment, beneficial-ownership challenges, penalties for false records, failure-to-file penalties, forfeiture or seizure in proceeds-of-crime cases, and possible Criminal Code fraud or laundering charges.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly provide false beneficial-ownership information or use the entity to evade tax, sanctions, creditors, or AML controls may be personally liable for civil restitution and criminal penalties. Travel restrictions are not automatic but may arise through bail conditions, court orders, outstanding warrants, immigration consequences, or passport measures in legally available cases.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation and Profit Diversion

Statutory sanction: CRA may make a primary transfer-pricing adjustment under section 247, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, deny unsupported expenses, assess withholding tax, and charge interest. Failure to file T106 or provide documentation may result in additional penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers can face personal exposure for knowingly participating in false books, tax evasion, or fraudulent arrangements. Routine transfer-pricing adjustments generally create entity-level tax liability rather than automatic director liability, but source-deduction and GST/HST trust liabilities remain distinct.
Counterfeit, Stolen, or Illicitly Exported Luxury Goods

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include Criminal Code charges, proceeds-of-crime forfeiture, customs seizure, trademark and copyright injunctions, statutory damages, consumer-protection penalties, contractual claims, and business-account termination by payment providers. Penalties depend on the offence, scale, knowledge, and commercial benefit.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly sell, possess, import, or facilitate illicit goods may be prosecuted personally. Directors may be liable for their own acts, authorization, or acquiescence, and the LP or general partner may face civil restitution, asset seizure, and loss of banking or payment access.
Unremitted Payroll Withholdings and Employment Misclassification

Statutory sanction: The Income Tax Act and related legislation permit assessments for source deductions, interest, penalties, payroll remittances, and director liability. Employment Insurance and Canada Pension Plan compliance failures may create additional assessments and penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions, subject to the statutory due-diligence defence. Misclassification can also create employment standards, workers' compensation, payroll-tax, and civil liability.
Prohibited Distribution and Insolvency Misconduct

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include recovery under insolvency and fraudulent-conveyance legislation, breach-of-duty claims, accounting orders, restitution, asset recovery, and criminal charges for fraud where dishonest intent is established.

Director personal liability: General partners and persons who authorize improper distributions may face direct contractual, fiduciary, restitutionary, and insolvency liability. Limited partners may be required to return impermissible distributions or lose protection to the extent provided by applicable Alberta partnership law.
