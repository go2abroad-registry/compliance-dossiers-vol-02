Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital requirement for an Alberta Limited Partnership; no escrow or paid-up capital deposit account rules apply. Limited partners must contribute capital as set out in the partnership agreement, and any withdrawal of capital that renders the partnership insolvent is prohibited under Partnership Act (Alberta), s. 58.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); CRA 'My Business Account' portal and Business Registration Online (BRO) at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html

E-government filing portal

The Alberta Limited Partnership must procure a CRA Business Number (BN) with an active GST/HST Program Indicator (RT) before making taxable supplies in Canada; non-resident partners are not separately required to obtain a Canadian tax ID for indirect tax purposes, but the LP must file Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or RC2 (for non-resident GST/HST registrants) either online via CRA BRO or by mail, and the LP must register under the Excise Tax Act if the total worldwide taxable revenues from supplies made in Canada exceed CAD 30,000 in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters or if the LP voluntarily elects to register. The same BN is used for income tax instalments, payroll, and GST/HST program accounts.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible - An Alberta LP is a flow-through entity for federal and provincial income tax, with each partner taxing their share of the LP's income; for GST/HST, the LP itself is a 'person' under the Excise Tax Act and is the registrant for supplies made through the partnership. Territorial scope: GST/HST applies to supplies made in Canada; Alberta imposes no provincial retail sales tax, so only the 5% federal GST applies in this jurisdiction.

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a check-the-box regime. Available elections include: (i) voluntary GST/HST registration by a small supplier (ETA subsection 240(4)); (ii) Quick Method of accounting for net tax (e.g., for retailers); (iii) partnership fiscal period election under Income Tax Act subsection 249.1(1); (iv) for non-residents with Canadian rental income, a section 216 election under the Income Tax Act, if applicable. Hybrid mismatch rules under the Income Tax Act (ss. 18.4, 20.01) are mandatory anti-avoidance provisions, not elective classification mechanisms.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable revenues from supplies made in Canada over the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; for digital and remote sellers, nexus is determined through the CRA's substantial-presence rules and marketplace attribution mechanisms under the Electronic Commerce (GST/HST) Regulations.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of tangible personal property by an Alberta LP e-retailer is zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V of the Excise Tax Act when the supplier exports the goods as part of the supply by shipping to an address outside Canada, and the supplier maintains documentary proof of actual exportation (e.g., B13A export declaration, customs release, carrier-generated waybill with foreign destination, delivery confirmation). Services supplied to a non-resident are zero-rated if received exclusively by the non-resident and delivered to an address outside Canada. Apparel e-commerce exported to overseas consumers generally qualifies if the goods are shipped by mail or courier and the export documentation is retained.

  • Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) B13A Export Declaration or eManifest export report
  • Commercial invoice indicating the 0% GST/HST rate and export
  • Bill of Lading / Airway Bill / international courier consignment note showing a foreign destination
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or foreign bank advisory/payment proof
  • Sales contract or purchase order with non-resident buyer details
  • Foreign destination address and export tracking history

No mandatory real-time electronic invoicing, fiscal clearance, or digital stamping regime exists in Canada. GST/HST returns are due and remitted electronically through CRA's GST/HST NETFILE (unless the registrant is explicitly exempted). Voluntary e-invoicing is permissible, and invoices must contain the required ETA disclosure elements; for zero-rated exports, the registrant must retain documentary evidence substantiating the export in electronic or paper form.

CRA auditors will verify the complete export trail; absence of any mandatory document can trigger a reassessment at 5% GST plus interest and penalties. Under Excise Tax Act subsection 285(1), the penalty for failing to file or omitting tax is 5% plus 1% per complete month, up to 25%, or up to 20% for gross negligence. For willful fraud, criminal penalties under section 285.1 may apply. Additionally, the Income Tax Act gross-negligence penalty under subsection 163(2) can be imposed on erroneous refund claims, up to 50% of the understated tax. Retrospective clawback periods are generally four years from the filing date (or unlimited in cases of misrepresentation or fraud); for non-resident registrants, CRA may use specialized audit programs and demand security or bond for future compliance.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is generally fully convertible for current-account and capital-account transactions. Canada does not generally restrict foreign ownership, inbound capital contributions, dividend payments, partnership distributions, loan repayments, or the repatriation of investment proceeds. Banks may require transaction-purpose information, beneficial-ownership evidence, source-of-funds evidence, tax residency information, and sanctions screening. Profit or distribution payments by an Alberta limited partnership may still be affected by Canadian tax classification, non-resident withholding obligations, partnership withholding or reporting requirements, transfer-pricing rules, lender covenants, insolvency restrictions, and bank compliance review. Payments involving sanctioned persons, restricted jurisdictions, unusual third-party funding, or opaque ownership may be delayed or rejected.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no general statutory requirement to surrender export proceeds to a Canadian bank, convert export receipts into Canadian dollars, or repatriate export proceeds within a prescribed period. No mandatory conversion ratio applies. Export receipts may generally be retained in foreign-currency accounts or converted at the holder's discretion, subject to contractual obligations, tax reporting, sanctions controls, customs requirements, anti-money-laundering controls, and accurate accounting. Canadian financial institutions may request commercial invoices, shipping evidence, customer information, and the economic purpose of material cross-border receipts.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada; Canada has no separate foreign-exchange administration imposing general exchange controls. Financial institutions and money-services businesses are subject to the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for payment collection and foreign-exchange execution; Low as a complete substitute for a Canadian operating bank account. An offshore electronic-money institution cannot automatically provide Canadian deposit-taking, Canadian-dollar payment services, or access to domestic clearing merely by serving an Alberta limited partnership remotely. The provider must be legally authorized in its home jurisdiction and must lawfully offer services into Canada. Use of payment processors, including Shopify Payments or BNPL providers such as Klarna, remains subject to merchant underwriting, Canadian consumer-protection requirements, card-network rules, settlement-account requirements, sanctions screening, chargeback controls, reserve requirements, and beneficial-owner verification. Customer funds may be safeguarded or held through regulated partners rather than treated as bank deposits of the LP. The LP should maintain a Canadian operating account for processor settlements, payroll, taxes, refunds, supplier payments, and liquidity backup.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Canada: 15% federal general corporate income tax rate. Alberta: 8% provincial general corporate income tax rate. Combined general corporate rate: 23%. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally obtain a combined small-business rate of approximately 11% on eligible active business income within the applicable small-business limit, subject to federal and Alberta rules. A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and does not itself pay corporate income tax; taxable income is allocated to its partners.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The Canadian domestic Part XIII withholding tax rate is generally 25% on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident. Under an applicable income tax treaty, the rate may be reduced, commonly to 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting the ownership threshold, and to 15% for other qualifying portfolio dividends. The Canada-United States treaty commonly provides a 5% rate for qualifying corporate ownership and a 15% rate in other cases, subject to treaty conditions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no universal monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is automatically unnecessary. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length terms and contemporaneous documentation for relevant non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. T106 reporting generally applies to reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions, subject to statutory rules including the CAD 1 million aggregate threshold and specified exclusions. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a multinational enterprise group with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year. Canada does not impose a separate general local-file or master-file threshold in Canadian dollars equivalent to a universal OECD threshold; CRA may request supporting documentation.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone United Arab Emirates-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Substance is assessed through the transfer-pricing arm's-length principle, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty principal-purpose and limitation-on-benefits provisions where applicable, beneficial-ownership analysis, specified anti-avoidance rules, thin-capitalization rules, foreign accrual property income rules, and judicial doctrines concerning sham transactions and abusive avoidance. A limited partnership should maintain genuine commercial functions, decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational capacity, inventory and logistics evidence, banking records, contractual authority, and accounting substance consistent with its stated retail model.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment, interest, a penalty generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit for gross negligence or knowingly false statements, and criminal tax-evasion penalties that may include fines of up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years, subject to the applicable offence and court determination.

Director personal liability: The general partner is ordinarily responsible for partnership obligations and compliance. Directors of a corporate general partner may face criminal liability for personal participation, authorization, acquiescence, or wilful blindness. Imprisonment may apply to convicted individuals. Civil restitution or tax recovery may follow. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax sanction but may arise from criminal process, bail conditions, immigration consequences, or enforcement orders.
Failure to File or Remit GST/HST

Statutory sanction: Assessment of unremitted tax, interest, late-filing penalties, gross-negligence penalties, denial of input tax credits, compliance orders, and potential prosecution for serious intentional conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner may be jointly and severally liable for certain unremitted GST/HST amounts if statutory due-diligence defenses fail. A limited partner is not ordinarily personally liable solely by holding a limited partnership interest, but liability protection may be impaired by management conduct, guarantees, agency, or direct wrongdoing.
Unremitted Payroll and Source-Deduction Amounts

Statutory sanction: Assessment of source deductions, interest, penalties, payroll audits, director-liability assessments, and criminal sanctions for deliberate evasion or obstruction.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner can be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions, subject to the statutory limitation period and due-diligence defense. General partners may bear direct partnership liability. Limited partners generally retain limited liability absent guarantees, participation in management, or independent wrongdoing.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Primary and secondary transfer-pricing adjustments, denial or reduction of deductions, imputed income, withholding-tax exposure, interest, penalties for inadequate documentation, and possible correlative-relief disputes with the foreign jurisdiction.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically personally liable for a transfer-pricing adjustment, but personal liability may arise for knowing participation in false filings, fraud, obstruction, or source-deduction failures. The corporate veil is not routinely pierced merely because a tax reassessment exists.
Shell Company, Sham, or Artificial Allocation

Statutory sanction: Recharacterization, denial of deductions, application of the general anti-avoidance rule, withholding-tax assessments, interest, gross-negligence penalties, and potential criminal investigation where false or deceptive conduct is present.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct or knowingly facilitate the arrangement may face civil recovery, criminal prosecution, fines, and imprisonment. Travel bans are not automatic and require a separate legal basis. General partners remain exposed to partnership liabilities.
Prohibited or Unlawful Distribution

Statutory sanction: Recovery of improper distributions, creditor claims, tax reassessment, denial of deductions, interest, and possible insolvency or fraudulent-preference proceedings.

Director personal liability: General partners may be personally liable under partnership and insolvency law. Directors of a corporate general partner may face statutory director liability or breach-of-duty claims. Limited partners may be required to restore improper distributions in specified circumstances.
AML, Proceeds of Crime, and Sanctions

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC compliance measures, administrative monetary penalties, asset restraint or forfeiture, regulatory reporting, prosecution under Canadian proceeds-of-crime and sanctions legislation, substantial fines, and imprisonment for serious offences.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and responsible managers may face personal criminal liability for authorization, participation, or wilful blindness. Corporate status does not protect a person from liability for their own conduct. Travel restrictions may result from criminal proceedings or court orders but are not automatic.
Customs and Import-Valuation Fraud

Statutory sanction: Customs reassessment, duties and GST, administrative monetary penalties, seizure or forfeiture, importer compliance action, and prosecution for deliberate fraud.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly submit false customs documents may be prosecuted personally. Directors may face liability for their own involvement or statutory obligations, while ordinary shareholder or limited-partner status alone does not create personal liability.
