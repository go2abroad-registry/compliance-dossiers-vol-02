Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) - national tax authority for GST/HST and federal income tax; primary portal is CRA My Business Account at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html. Alberta maintains no provincial sales tax or HST component; the Alberta Corporate Registry handles limited partnership registration under the Alberta Partnership Act, and the Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration (ATRA) administers provincial corporate income tax for corporate partners only.

E-government filing portal

Non-resident limited partnership carrying on business in Canada: (1) apply for a 9-digit CRA Business Number (BN) with a program account suffix using Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1; (2) open a GST/HST account (RT program account) when the CAD 30,000 threshold is exceeded or for voluntary registration; (3) obtain the RM program account for filing Form T5013 partnership information return; (4) register the extra-provincial limited partnership with the Alberta Corporate Registry, including name search and the prescribed Alberta Partnership Act registration; (5) present the BN and CRA registration proof to payment processors (Stripe/PayPal) for tax collection on Canadian sales; no minimum capital requirement applies to the Alberta LP.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — Non-resident limited partnership carrying on business in Canada: (1) apply for a 9-digit CRA Business Number (BN) with a program account suffix using Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1; (2) open a GST/HST account (RT program account) when the CAD 30,000 threshold is exceeded or for voluntary registration; (3) obtain the RM program account for filing Form T5013 partnership information return; (4) register the extra-provincial limited partnership with the Alberta Corporate Registry, including name search and the prescribed Alberta Partnership Act registration; (5) present the BN and CRA registration proof to payment processors (Stripe/PayPal) for tax collection on Canadian sales; no minimum capital requirement applies to the Alberta LP.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a US-style 'Check-the-Box' election; a Canadian limited partnership is mandatorily fiscally transparent for income tax, with partners reporting allocated income and loss, while the partnership itself is treated as a separate person for GST/HST under the Excise Tax Act. Available elections relevant to an e-commerce LP include: (a) ETA section 150 'closely related' election to treat certain supplies between registrants as non-supplies; (b) ETA section 151 group registration; (c) ETA section 156(2) election to align GST/HST fiscal year and use annual filing; (d) deemed supplier treatment for non-resident marketplace operators under ETA section 178.6 where goods are fulfilled through a platform. Canada has not enacted a substantive OECD-style hybrid mismatch election for partnerships; anti-hybrid measures address specific cross-border financial instrument mismatches and do not permit an LP to alter its pass-through default.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 - gross worldwide revenues from taxable supplies made in Canada over any trailing four-consecutive-calendar-quarter period; mandatory GST/HST registration applies once exceeded. Foreign sellers soliciting orders through a Canadian web presence are subject to the place-of-supply rules under ETA section 132 for goods, ETA section 143.1 for permanent establishment, and SOR/2010-151 for services and intangibles. Voluntary registration is permitted below the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is claim-based under ETA sections 23, 39, and Schedule VI Part V; for consumer electronics shipped to foreign customers from Canada, the supply is zero-rated when the goods are actually exported as part of a continuous journey out of Canada, possession transfers to the non-resident buyer for export, and documentary evidence of export is retained. The CRA requires export proof proportionate to risk, including courier tracking with foreign delivery confirmation and destination-country customs entry records; supplies delivered into Canada remain taxable at 5%.

  • Canadian Customs Export Declaration (Form B13 / CBSA CADEX or Canadian International Trade Single Window record)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill showing foreign consignee and ultimate destination
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or equivalent bank/PSP FX record (SWIFT MT103, Stripe/PayPal foreign settlement statement) evidencing non-resident payment
  • Commercial invoice with HS classification (e.g., HS 8517, 8471, 8518 categories), unit pricing, and zero-rated export statement
  • Foreign-country customs entry proof or delivery confirmation (e.g., US CSMS entry record, courier customs manifest)

Canada has no real-time fiscal pre-clearance, e-invoice stamping, or mandatory API clearance regime. Registrants must issue valid GST/HST invoices under ETA section 221 and Input Tax Credit Information Guidelines in SOR/91-45, containing supplier name, Business Number, date, description, and tax amount; e-invoicing is permitted in electronic form. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically through CRA GST/HST NETFILE (Form GST34) for most registrants. The Pan-Canadian B2B e-invoicing framework (future BIS standard) is voluntary in Alberta. Digital stamping and pre-clearance API requirements are not applicable.

CRA may reassess an improper zero-rating claim within the normal four-year reassessment window, which is unlimited where misrepresentation, fraud, or neglect is found; reversed supplies are restored to taxable status with 5% GST and arrears interest. A person who knowingly or under circumstances of gross negligence makes a false or misleading statement on a return is liable under ETA section 285(1) to a penalty equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the federal tax sought to be evaded, plus mandatory interest. Wilful evasion under ETA section 280 and Income Tax Act section 239 carries fines of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to 5 years for an indictable offence. High-volume e-commerce exporters of low-value electronics face heightened CRA scrutiny of export evidence; failure to repatriate export proceeds within the 180-day benchmark is an audit red flag and may trigger denial of zero-rating treatment and application of the General Anti-Avoidance Rule under ETA section 274 if artificial offshore interposition is created solely to obtain an export refund.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility on both current- and capital-account transactions. Canada is an IMF Article VIII member and imposes no general licensing, surrender, or repatriation requirements for trade receipts, dividends, capital injections, or profit remittances. Profit distributions from an Alberta limited partnership are unrestricted but trigger withholding obligations under Income Tax Act subsection 212(2) for non-resident partners at 25% of the income allocation, subject to treaty reduction. Qualifying exports of consumer electronics are zero-rated for GST/HST purposes under section 9 of the Excise Tax Act.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender rule, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no repatriation deadline exist in Canada. Export proceeds denominated in USD, CNY, or other foreign currencies may be retained offshore or converted freely. The LP must, however, maintain comprehensive CAD books and records for Alberta corporate and GST/HST filings, and foreign-currency gains or losses are recognized under subsection 39(1.1) of the Income Tax Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC); prudential supervision of deposit-taking institutions by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/ATF supervision by FINTRAC; payments clearing and settlement operation by Payments Canada (Lynx RTGS and ACSS). Canada does not maintain a dedicated foreign-exchange control statute; the Foreign Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Medium-High feasibility. Licensed Money Services Businesses (MSBs) such as Wise Canada Inc., Payoneer Inc., and Nuvei provide multi-currency wallets, low-cost FX conversion, and cross-border disbursements. They are suitable as supplementary payout rails for small USD/CNH flows, but not as the sole treasury or capital-escrow banking counterparty for the LP.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

A Canadian limited partnership is generally not subject to income tax at the entity level. Its income and losses flow through to its partners. For taxable Canadian corporations carrying on business in Alberta, the general combined federal and Alberta corporate income tax rate is 23%: 15% federal and 8% Alberta. The combined small-business rate is generally 11%: 9% federal and 2% Alberta, subject to the Canadian-controlled private corporation and business-limit rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The Canadian domestic Part XIII withholding tax rate is generally 25% on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident. Under the Canada-China income tax treaty, the rate is generally reduced to 10% where the beneficial owner is a company that directly or indirectly controls at least 10% of the voting power of the payer, and 15% in other treaty-eligible cases. Treaty relief requires beneficial ownership, residence, limitation-on-benefits or anti-abuse compliance where applicable, and procedural support.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T5013 partnership information return and related partnership slips are generally due within five months after the partnership fiscal period-end. A partnership with a December 31 year-end generally files by May 31 of the following year. A partnership with a non-calendar fiscal period generally files five months after its fiscal period-end. Partner income tax returns and tax payments follow the separate deadlines applicable to each partner.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general revenue threshold that automatically triggers a statutory Local File or Master File for every taxpayer. Contemporaneous documentation is required under section 247 of the Income Tax Act for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions unless the statutory small-business exception applies. A generally relevant exception is where the aggregate value of the taxpayer's non-arm's-length non-resident transactions for the year is below CAD 1,000,000, subject to the detailed statutory conditions. Form T106 is generally required for reportable transactions with non-arm's-length non-residents and has separate filing requirements. Country-by-Country reporting generally applies to an international group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year, subject to Canadian reporting rules. Canada does not generally impose an automatic OECD-style Master File filing requirement solely because a group exceeds a local revenue threshold.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a separate general Economic Substance Regulation regime equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Canadian tax analysis nevertheless requires commercial purpose, factual substance, beneficial ownership, arm's-length pricing, and application of the general anti-avoidance rule in section 245 of the Income Tax Act. The sham doctrine, specific anti-avoidance provisions, transfer-pricing rules, thin-capitalization rules, interest-limitation rules, foreign affiliate rules, and principal-purpose or treaty anti-abuse standards may deny benefits where arrangements lack commercial substance or are designed primarily to obtain an abusive tax advantage. A consumer-electronics e-commerce LP should maintain evidence of personnel, inventory functions, logistics, platform activity, pricing decisions, payment processing, customer support, and risk control.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil penalties may include a penalty of 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit for false statements or omissions and a gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the relevant tax amount. Criminal tax evasion may result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to five years, or both, together with repayment of tax, interest, and civil penalties.

Director personal liability: A limited partner is generally not personally liable solely because of partnership tax debt if the partner does not participate in management beyond the statutory limited-partner boundary. A general partner, and potentially the corporate general partner's directors or officers for their own misconduct, may face personal civil exposure. Directors may also face derivative or statutory liability for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST. Imprisonment is personal and can arise from criminal conduct; travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty but may arise from criminal proceedings, bail conditions, or immigration consequences.
Failure to File Partnership and Information Returns

Statutory sanction: Daily late-filing penalties, fixed information-return penalties, electronic-filing penalties, demand-to-file consequences, reassessments based on available information, and interest may apply. Repeated or deliberate failure can support prosecution and extended audit periods.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers can be personally exposed where they authorized, acquiesced in, or participated in non-compliance, particularly for source deductions and GST/HST. Limited partners generally retain limited liability unless they act as general partners, give guarantees, commit wrongdoing, or lose statutory protection through improper management participation.
GST/HST and E-Commerce Tax Misclassification

Statutory sanction: Assessment of uncollected GST/HST, interest, gross-negligence or other penalties, denial of unsupported input tax credits, and potential criminal prosecution for fraudulent conduct. Import GST, customs duties, and provincial consumer-protection obligations may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally for certain unremitted GST/HST amounts where the statutory director-liability conditions are met. A limited partner is not automatically personally liable, but personal guarantees, fraud, or direct participation can create separate exposure.
Transfer Pricing and Related-Party E-Commerce Arrangements

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust income under section 247, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the relevant adjustment where statutory conditions are met, deny deductions, assess withholding tax, and apply interest. Repeated non-compliance may increase audit scope and reassessment risk.

Director personal liability: Tax adjustments are generally imposed on the taxpayer or partnership and allocated to partners. Directors, officers, general partners, and advisers may face personal liability for fraud, knowing participation, statutory remittance defaults, or professional misconduct, but not merely because a transfer-pricing adjustment was made.
Shell Company, Treaty Abuse, and Beneficial Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: The CRA may deny treaty benefits, apply the full 25% Part XIII withholding rate, assess arrears, interest, and penalties, and invoke the general anti-avoidance rule or treaty anti-abuse provisions. Withholding agents may be liable for tax that should have been withheld.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors and officers may face personal liability for unremitted source deductions where statutory requirements are met. Fraudulent treaty claims may lead to criminal prosecution, fines, and imprisonment. A travel ban is not an automatic civil consequence but may follow criminal proceedings or court orders.
AML, Proceeds of Crime, and Payment-Processor Misuse

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include account suspension, seizure or forfeiture, administrative monetary penalties, prosecution under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and Criminal Code, substantial fines, and imprisonment. FINTRAC obligations may apply depending on the entity's regulated activities.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and controlling persons may face criminal liability for knowing participation, counselling, directing, or benefiting from the conduct. Corporate or limited-liability status does not protect a person from liability for personal criminal acts.
Payroll, Worker-Classification, and Source-Deduction Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: The CRA may reassess payroll deductions, CPP, EI, penalties, interest, and taxable benefits. Failure to deduct or remit can result in criminal prosecution in serious cases and denial of related deductions.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions if statutory conditions are satisfied. General partners and persons exercising operational control may also face personal exposure for deliberate non-compliance.
Limited Partnership Governance and Improper Partner Conduct

Statutory sanction: Possible loss of limited-liability protection, contractual claims, partner oppression or accounting proceedings, tax reassessment, creditor recovery, and insolvency consequences. The applicable Alberta partnership legislation and partnership agreement govern the specific consequences.

Director personal liability: A limited partner may become exposed to partnership liabilities to the extent it is treated as participating in control or otherwise loses statutory protection. The corporate veil is not pierced automatically, but courts may impose personal liability for fraud, sham arrangements, oppressive conduct, or deliberate misuse of the entity.
