Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No minimum paid-up capital is prescribed for an Alberta limited partnership. A limited partner's liability is capped at the amount of the capital contribution recorded in the declaration; contributions may be cash or property at agreed value. No escrow account or capital deposit is required by the Registrar.

E-government filing portal

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — Canadian income tax treats an Alberta Limited Partnership as fiscally transparent: income and losses are computed at partnership level and allocated to partners under ITA s.96, with an information return (T5013) filed instead of a corporate return. Exception: a publicly traded 'specified investment flow-through' (SIFT) partnership is deemed to be a corporation under ITA s.197. For GST/HST purposes the LP is a distinct 'person' and registers in its own name under Part IX of the Excise Tax Act. Alberta has no provincial sales tax, no provincial capital tax, and no separate provincial sales-tax registration; only the federal GST applies.

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box election for Alberta LPs. Relevant statutory elections include: (i) ITA s.97(2) election to fix the agreed cost amount of property contributed to the partnership; (ii) ITA s.96(3) election for exclusion of certain foreign-source income of a Canadian partnership; (iii) ETA s.273 election to treat supplies between closely related registrants as made for nil consideration; (iv) ETA s.156 election for deemed self-assessment of GST/HST on specified taxable supplies; (v) ETA s.211/212 elections applicable to financial institutions, relevant only if the LP holds material investment accounts; and (vi) waiver or narrowing of reassessment limitation periods under ITA s.152(4) and ETA s.298 in the context of export-claim audits.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable revenues (small-supplier threshold, ETA s.148(1)); a non-resident e-commerce supplier of digital products or remote services supplying Canada must register once Canadian taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000. Once registered, 5% GST applies to Canadian point-of-sale sales and qualifying exports are zero-rated.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act, supplies of tangible personal property (musical instruments and studio audio gear) are zero-rated when (i) the goods are supplied to a non-resident and are exported from Canada as part of the sale, or (ii) the goods are delivered or made available outside Canada by the supplier or recipient in the manner contemplated by the Act. The supplier must cause the goods to leave Canada within a reasonable time and must obtain and retain documentary proof of export within two years (CRA GST/HST Memorandum 5-4-1). Online orders fulfilled from Canadian inventory to foreign destinations qualify for 0% GST where title transfers outside Canada and the shipping documentation identifies a foreign consignee and destination. Incidental services supplied in connection with the exported goods are also zero-rated when supplied to a non-resident.

  • CBSA B13A Export Declaration (required where export value exceeds CAD 2,000, filed via CBSA Single Window or a licensed customs broker)
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill evidencing foreign destination, consignee, and export routing
  • Commercial invoice annotated with '0% GST export' and including HS/NAICS 459140 classification and full goods description
  • Export packing list and cargo control manifest
  • Destination-country customs entry or import clearance evidence
  • Proof of payment from Stripe or PayPal settlement statements, or bank inward remittance certificate
  • Freight forwarding instructions and insurance certificates evidencing international delivery
  • CRA proof-of-export template or internal export-substantiation declaration per Memorandum 5-4-1

Canada does not impose real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or a mandatory centralized e-invoicing clearance framework. Suppliers must issue GST/HST invoices containing prescribed particulars under ETA s.221 and the relevant Regulations, retain electronic records in machine-readable format (CRA Information Circular IC-79-10R7), and file returns via GST/HST NETFILE or the GST/HST Internet Filing system with CRA. Customs export declarations are filed electronically through the CBSA Integrated Import Declaration system or by using Form B13A through a customs broker; no API-based government invoice stamping is required for B2B e-commerce sales.

CRA audited export-claim reviews cross-match zero-rated GST/HST claims with shipping logs, warehouse outbound records, foreign customs entries, and Stripe/PayPal settlement data. Where proof of export is not obtained within the two-year statutory window, the supply is recharacterized as taxable at 5% GST, and CRA may reassess the LP within four years (extended to ten years for misrepresentation, and unlimited in the case of fraud), with compound daily interest under ETA s.280.1. Civil penalties include a 25% gross-negligence penalty under ETA s.285 and ITA s.163.2; knowing tax evasion triggers a 50% penalty under ETA s.327(1), plus criminal liability under Criminal Code s.380 and ITA s.239 for the partnership, general partners, and senior management. Refunds may be denied, offset, or clawed back under ETA s.296(2), and general partners are jointly and severally liable for the LP's GST/HST debts and penalties. Canada imposes no statutory 180-day invoice-repatriation deadline; the 180-day figure is used here as the internal treasury compliance target aligning with GST/HST return cycles and settlement calendars of the target payment rails.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The CAD floats independently with full current-account and capital-account convertibility for corporate treasury purposes; no exchange-rate approval, quota, or surrender certificate is required for inbound capital subscriptions, trade collections, or outbound partner distributions. For the CA_AB Limited Partnership, the sole conversion friction is fiscal rather than monetary: a non-resident Limited Partner is subject to Part XIII withholding at 25% on taxable Canadian-source distributions (treaty-reduced to 15% under the Canada–U.S. Tax Convention for US partners, or to 0% on properly documented business-profit components attributable to an exempt PE), and the partnership is obligated to file Form T5013 with the Canada Revenue Agency. As the entity structure carries no minimum capital requirement and no statutory licensing prerequisite, daily clearing and conversion of operational cash are unrestricted.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada does not impose export-proceeds surrender rules. There is no mandatory conversion ratio and no statutory repatriation timeline: the LP may lawfully retain Stripe/PayPal/direct-wire export receipts in USD, EUR, or any freely convertible currency in a foreign-currency account at a Canadian Schedule I bank. The only binding statutory requirements are: (i) the bank files a FINTRAC EFT Wire Report when any cross-border electronic funds transfer exceeds CAD 10,000 within a rolling 24-hour window; (ii) Canada Border Services Agency declaration applies only if physical currency, cheques, or bearer instruments of at least CAD 10,000 are carried across the border; and (iii) exports of musical instruments and audio gear must be zero-rated under Excise Tax Act s. 24 and documented through GST/HST computable invoices, which preserves the partnership's full right to the foreign-currency inflow without any central-bank conversion requirement.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) — the sole central-bank and monetary authority for the Canadian dollar. It operates the Lynx high-value payment/RTGS system, manages domestic FX intervention policy via the Exchange Fund Account, and is the lender of last resort. Prudential supervision of Schedule I/II banks rests with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money-laundering and cross-border wire-transaction reporting are enforced by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). No foreign-exchange approval authority exists because Canada maintains a liberalised, freely convertible currency regime.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium-High for collection and payout mechanics (Stripe/PayPal/DSW), but Low for legal equivalence as a primary treasury rail. Under s. 392(1) of the Bank Act, only a Schedule I/II bank or a registered credit union may accept CAD deposits on a banker-customer basis; offshore EMIs (Wise Business, Payoneer, Airwallex, Stripe Treasury) must hold client balances through a licensed Canadian clearing partner and are not covered by Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation. These platforms may serve as a 'collection wallet' for the LP's marketplace receivables, but inbound capital contributions and outbound partner distributions must route through the chartered-bank master account. Weekly repatriation (balances above CAD 10,000) is recommended to neutralise counterparty risk and FX mark-up drag.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Canada federal general corporate rate: 15%; Alberta general corporate rate: 8%; combined general corporate rate: 23%. These corporate rates generally do not apply directly to an Alberta limited partnership because the partnership is ordinarily fiscally transparent. Income is allocated to partners and taxed at partner level. The federal small-business rate is 9% and the Alberta small-business rate is 2% for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations, producing a combined rate of 11%; an LP is not itself a CCPC.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The standard Canadian Part XIII withholding rate on dividends paid or credited to non-residents is 25%. A treaty may reduce it, commonly to 5% for qualifying corporate ownership and 15% for other treaty-eligible dividends; the applicable rate depends on the treaty, ownership percentage, beneficial ownership, limitation-on-benefits provisions, and anti-avoidance rules. An LP distribution is not automatically a dividend; its characterization depends on the underlying income and recipient status.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a universal transaction-value threshold before contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is required. Canadian taxpayers must make reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length pricing and maintain contemporaneous documentation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. Form T106 reporting generally becomes relevant where aggregate reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1,000,000, subject to the detailed rules. A Canadian Local File and Master File framework applies principally to large multinational enterprise groups subject to Country-by-Country reporting, generally at the EUR 750 million consolidated-group-revenue threshold; no general Canadian Master File obligation applies to every LP.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone regime named Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is enforced through transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, sham and beneficial-ownership principles, treaty anti-abuse provisions, specified anti-avoidance rules, thin-capitalization rules, interest-limitation rules, and ordinary business-purpose analysis. The LP should maintain real decision-making, commercial contracts, personnel or properly controlled service providers, inventory and fulfillment evidence, banking records, accounting books, and risk-control documentation consistent with the stated business model.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Deliberate Understatement

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of tax, interest, gross-negligence penalties, civil penalties, and criminal prosecution. Tax-evasion convictions can result in fines ranging from a percentage of tax sought to be evaded to multiples of that amount and imprisonment of up to five years under the Income Tax Act.

Director personal liability: A general partner, signing officer, or person who authorized or participated in the conduct may face prosecution, fines, imprisonment, director-liability assessments where statutory conditions are met, civil restitution, asset seizure, and restrictions on serving as a director. Travel restrictions may arise from court orders or immigration consequences but are not an automatic tax penalty.
False Partnership Allocations and Sham Arrangements

Statutory sanction: Reallocation of income, denial of losses or deductions, GAAR application, interest, gross-negligence penalties, and possible criminal prosecution for knowingly false returns or documents.

Director personal liability: The general partner and responsible individuals may incur personal tax assessments, penalties, civil claims, and criminal exposure. Limited liability does not protect a person from personal wrongdoing, fraud, statutory director liability, or breaches of fiduciary duty.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Transfer-pricing reassessment, a potential 10% transfer-pricing penalty on the relevant adjustment where statutory conditions are met, interest, denial of deductions, Part XIII withholding assessments, and documentation penalties.

Director personal liability: Responsible officers may face civil penalties or prosecution for knowing false statements. Ordinary pricing errors generally do not automatically create personal liability absent statutory involvement, gross negligence, fraud, or other misconduct.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Part XIII Tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed the unremitted withholding, interest, failure-to-deduct or failure-to-remit penalties, and related information-return penalties. Treaty reductions cannot be applied without reasonable supporting evidence.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible officers may be personally assessed under director-liability rules after statutory collection conditions are satisfied, particularly where the failure was authorized, tolerated, or continued despite known obligations.
GST/HST Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Assessment of tax, interest, penalties for late filing or late remittance, denial of input tax credits, gross-negligence penalties, and prosecution for tax fraud.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate partner may face statutory director liability for unremitted GST/HST. A general partner may face direct exposure for partnership obligations and misconduct; limited partners remain protected only within the limits of applicable partnership law and their conduct.
AML, Proceeds of Crime, and Payment-Platform Abuse

Statutory sanction: Account freezing, transaction blocking, reporting to FINTRAC or law enforcement, administrative monetary penalties, forfeiture, criminal prosecution, and imprisonment under applicable proceeds-of-crime, terrorist-financing, sanctions, or Criminal Code provisions.

Director personal liability: Individuals may face personal criminal liability, forfeiture, fines, imprisonment, prohibition orders, and court-imposed travel restrictions. Corporate or partnership separateness does not shield knowing participants or beneficial owners.
Beneficial Ownership and Corporate Transparency Failure

Statutory sanction: Registrar penalties, tax information penalties, FINTRAC consequences where applicable, denial of services, reassessment, and criminal penalties for false or misleading statements.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly provide false information or obstruct regulators may be prosecuted and may face fines, imprisonment, civil claims, and disqualification or prohibition orders.
Customs, Import, and Inventory Misdeclaration

Statutory sanction: Customs duties, GST on imports, interest, administrative monetary penalties, seizure, reassessment, importer suspension, and prosecution under customs legislation.

Director personal liability: Responsible individuals may face personal fines, prosecution, forfeiture, and civil recovery where they directed or knowingly participated in the misdeclaration.
Corporate and Partnership Law Breach

Statutory sanction: Loss or limitation of limited-liability protection, partner claims, accounting orders, damages, injunctions, dissolution, and regulatory penalties.

Director personal liability: A general partner is ordinarily exposed to partnership liabilities. A limited partner may lose protection for taking unauthorized management actions or participating in fraud. Directors of a corporate partner may face personal liability for statutory breaches or wrongful conduct.
Records, Books, and Audit Obstruction

Statutory sanction: Requirement to produce records, reassessment based on available evidence, penalties, adverse inferences, court enforcement, and criminal prosecution for obstruction or destruction of records.

Director personal liability: Responsible persons may be fined or prosecuted for obstruction, destruction, or knowing failure to maintain records. CRA may pursue statutory director liability where applicable.
