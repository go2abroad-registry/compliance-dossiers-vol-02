Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) via the My Business Account portal and Business Registration Online (BRO). Customs/export declarations are administered by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) under the Single Window Initiative; Alberta corporate registry filings (Certificate of Limited Partnership) are administered by Service Alberta under the Partnership Act (Alberta).

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Register the Alberta Limited Partnership (LP) under the Partnership Act (Alberta) by filing the Certificate of Limited Partnership with the Alberta Corporate Registry (Service Alberta); a foreign LP must obtain an extra-provincial registration before carrying on business in Alberta. Step 2: Obtain the federal 9-digit Business Number (BN) online via Business Registration Online (BRO) or by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) with CRA, identifying the LP's legal name, Alberta registration number, Canadian business address, and agent for service. Step 3: Open the required CRA program accounts in the same application: GST/HST (program code RT), Payroll (RP) if employees are engaged, and Import/Export (RM) with the CBSA. Step 4: For a non-resident-owned LP, designate a Canadian-resident agent for service and provide the non-resident partners' identities; CRA will issue confirmation of registration with a BN. Step 5: Retain registration correspondence (electronic confirmation or RC1/RC2 letters) for customs, banking, and counterparty due diligence.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — An Alberta LP is fiscally transparent for income tax: the LP itself pays no federal income tax (Alberta levies no provincial income tax on partnerships), files only an information return (Form T5013), and allocates income/loss to partners under s. 96 of the Income Tax Act. For GST/HST, the partnership is a separate legal 'person' under the Excise Tax Act and is the registered entity that collects and remits GST/HST (5% GST in Alberta). Territorial scope: Canadian-resident partners are taxed on worldwide income; non-resident partners are taxed only on Canadian-source income allocations. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — An Alberta LP is fiscally transparent for income tax: the LP itself pays no federal income tax (Alberta levies no provincial income tax on partnerships), files only an information return (Form T5013), and allocates income/loss to partners under s. 96 of the Income Tax Act. For GST/HST, the partnership is a separate legal 'person' under the Excise Tax Act and is the registered entity that collects and remits GST/HST (5% GST in Alberta). Territorial scope: Canadian-resident partners are taxed on worldwide income; non-resident partners are taxed only on Canadian-source income allocations. The LP is not a 'tax-exempt zone' entity.

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box election; classification is statutorily determined. Relevant elections: (1) Quick Method for GST/HST (Form GST74) where annual taxable supplies (including associated persons) do not exceed CAD 400,000; (2) s. 156 ETA election between the LP and a closely related corporate general partner to treat supplies as made for nil consideration; (3) s. 96(3) Income Tax Act election, if all partners are resident in Canada, to compute partnership income on a modified separate-person basis; (4) determination of 'Canadian partnership' status under s. 102 Income Tax Act (all partners resident) to access domestic rollover rules and avoid certain foreign-property reporting; (5) no hybrid mismatch election exists for this entity, but partnership allocations must be consistent with partnership agreements to avoid recharacterization under s. 103.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the 'small supplier' threshold under s. 148 ETA, determined on worldwide taxable supplies of the LP plus associated persons. A non-resident supplier carrying on a commercial activity in Canada, including through a Canadian fulfillment warehouse or as a deemed supplier of goods under the 2021 digital-economy rules (ss. 143.01 et seq. ETA), must register for GST/HST once taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000. Since the LP's kitting/fulfillment operation is a Canadian commercial activity with invoiced services, registration is mandatory once the threshold is met.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating at 0% GST/HST applies when: (1) Goods — the LP supplies tangible personal property that it ships or delivers to a destination outside Canada, with export in accordance with the Exported Goods Regulations and CRA GST/HST Memorandum 17-4; (2) Services — kitting, assembly, packaging, warehousing, and fulfillment services supplied to a non-resident customer in respect of goods located in Canada qualify for zero-rating under the export provisions of Schedule VI, Part V, ETA (including CRA GST/HST Memorandum 17-5 and administrative policy for 'services in respect of goods for export'), provided the goods are exported by or on behalf of the LP as soon as is reasonably practicable after the service is performed and the recipient is a non-resident not registered for GST/HST. Zero-rating is refused where goods are sold to Canadian end-consumers through domestic fulfillment, in which case GST/HST (5% in Alberta) applies to the local supply and the 2021 fulfilment-entity deeming rules may impose registration obligations on the foreign seller.

  • Customs Export Declaration — Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) / CBSA Form B13A data transmission, including the LP's Business Number, product harmonized codes, quantities, and declared destination.
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill — carrier-issued transport document together with warehouse pick/pack records, kitting assembly specifications, work orders, and inventory movement logs evidencing the goods' identity, quantity, and final foreign delivery.
  • Payment Evidence — SWIFT/wire transfer confirmation, foreign exchange record, or bank statement evidencing receipt of consideration from the non-resident customer; this is the functional equivalent of an Inward Remittance Certificate, which Canadian law does not formally issue.

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, government-approved invoicing software, or digital stamping. Electronic invoicing is permitted and the CRA supports the Peppol PINT standard on a voluntary basis. Registrants must issue sales invoices containing prescribed GST/HST information (s. 221 ETA), retain all books and records for six years, and file GST/HST returns electronically via GST/HST NETFILE or compatible third-party software. All export shipments require electronic transmission of the customs export declaration to CBSA through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) or an approved service provider; no other government API clearance applies to warehouse/kitting output.

The CRA may reassess within the normal four-year limitation period under s. 298 ETA, and without limitation for fraud or misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default. If zero-rating is denied, the CRA will (a) assess unreported GST/HST at 5% on the full supply value plus arrears interest; (b) impose a false-statement penalty of the greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the net tax understated (s. 280(1) ETA), or an evasion penalty of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded (s. 285 ETA) upon conviction; (c) claw back input tax credits and refunds already paid; and (d) apply the Income Tax Act gross-negligence penalty of 50% of any understated income (s. 163(2) ITA), with criminal fines of 100% to 200% of the tax and imprisonment under ss. 238/239 ITA. The general partner, and limited partners to the extent of undistributed assets, are jointly and severally liable for the LP's GST/HST obligations (s. 272.1 ETA; Partnership Act, Alberta). CRA/CBSA conduct joint 'follow-the-goods' audits of fulfillment warehouses; all export substantiation records must be retained for six years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for ordinary current-account and capital-account transactions; Canada has no general requirement for prior approval to receive or remit investment capital, trade proceeds, or partnership distributions. Banks and regulated payment providers may request source-of-funds, beneficial-ownership, transaction-purpose, sanctions, and tax documentation. Alberta limited-partnership status does not itself restrict non-resident ownership or cross-border transfers. Tax treatment is separate from convertibility: a non-resident partner may be taxable in Canada on its share of Canadian-source partnership income, and applicable withholding or reporting obligations must be assessed by income type, partner status, and treaty eligibility. Maintain partnership allocation, tax, and remittance records; do not characterize a distribution as tax-free solely because it is a capital or profit remittance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no general statutory export-proceeds repatriation deadline or mandatory foreign-currency conversion ratio. Export proceeds may be retained in foreign currency or converted through an authorized financial institution or payment provider, subject to ordinary AML, sanctions, tax, contractual, and bank documentation requirements.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no general foreign-exchange administration imposing routine exchange controls. Federal financial-crime, sanctions, banking, and payment-system requirements are administered by relevant authorities, including the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and the Department of Finance Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium to High for routine CAD/USD collections, foreign-exchange conversion, and international payments, subject to provider eligibility, account limits, supported currencies, transaction corridors, and underwriting. Airwallex may be considered if its Canadian service is available for the entity and use case. A fintech or electronic-money provider is not necessarily a bank, and balances may not be eligible for Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation protection. Verify the contracting legal entity, Canadian regulatory status and FINTRAC registration where applicable, safeguarding terms, settlement arrangements, and withdrawal rights. Retain a Canadian bank account as the primary operating and contingency account.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Canada federal general corporate income tax rate: 15%. Alberta general corporate income tax rate: 8%. Combined general corporate rate: 23%. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may access a combined small-business rate of approximately 11% on eligible active business income, subject to the federal CAD 500,000 business limit and Alberta eligibility rules. An Alberta limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and does not itself pay corporate income tax; taxable income is allocated to its partners.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Canada's general Part XIII withholding tax rate on dividends paid or credited to non-residents is 25%. A treaty may reduce the rate, commonly to 5% for a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting ownership conditions and 15% for other qualifying portfolio dividends. An Alberta limited partnership ordinarily distributes partnership profits rather than dividends; the tax treatment depends on the recipient's legal status, the character of the payment, and whether the payment is a partnership allocation, distribution of capital, interest, royalty, or other amount.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T5013 is generally due within 5 months after the end of the partnership's fiscal period. A partnership fiscal period generally cannot exceed 12 months. Each partner reports its allocated partnership income in the partner's applicable Canadian tax return. A Canadian corporate partner generally files its T2 within 6 months after its taxation year-end, although any balance owing is generally payable within 2 months, or 3 months for certain Canadian-controlled private corporations satisfying statutory conditions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For Canadian contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation, the principal threshold is generally CAD 1,000,000 of non-arm's-length cross-border transactions with a non-resident, subject to the statutory exceptions and transaction-specific rules. The documentation must be prepared by the taxpayer's filing due date and provided to the CRA within 3 months of a written request. Canada does not impose a universal OECD-style Master File requirement on every taxpayer. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or designated surrogate entity of a multinational enterprise group with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone regime labelled Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is nevertheless relevant under the Income Tax Act's general anti-avoidance rule, transfer-pricing provisions, treaty anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership analysis, foreign-affiliate rules, thin-capitalization rules, principal-purpose tests, and judicial doctrines concerning sham transactions and misuse or abuse. A fulfillment and kitting LP should maintain Canadian operational substance proportionate to its claimed activities, including personnel or contracted operational capacity, warehouse and equipment records, customer and supplier contracts, risk controls, inventory records, commercial decision-making evidence, and bank and accounting records.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, tax evasion may result in prosecution, fines generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded on summary conviction, or higher statutory consequences on indictment, together with imprisonment of up to 5 years for indictable offences. Reassessment, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties, and collection action may apply.

Director personal liability: Individuals who authorize, participate in, or counsel evasion may be prosecuted personally. Directors and officers may face criminal fines and imprisonment. Corporate status does not protect a person from liability for personal misconduct. A director may also be assessed personally for unremitted source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts under statutory director-liability provisions.
Failure to Withhold or Remit Non-Resident Tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the unwithheld amount, arrears interest, penalties, and applicable information-return penalties. Treaty relief may be denied where beneficial ownership, residence, limitation-on-benefits, or anti-avoidance requirements are not satisfied.

Director personal liability: Directors may become personally liable for source deductions and other prescribed remittances where the corporation or entity failed to deduct or remit and the statutory director-liability conditions are met. Wilful participation may create separate criminal exposure.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: The CRA may make a transfer-pricing adjustment, deny deductions, impute income, impose arrears interest, and apply a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met and the adjustment exceeds the applicable materiality exception. Treaty competent-authority relief may be sought where available.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily liable solely because a transfer-pricing adjustment is made. Personal exposure arises from knowing participation in false records, fraud, evasion, obstruction, or failure to remit amounts covered by statutory director-liability provisions.
Partnership Allocation Abuse and Limited-Partner Loss Misuse

Statutory sanction: The CRA may reallocate partnership income, deny losses or deductions, apply the general anti-avoidance rule, assess penalties and interest, and impose tax at the partner level. A partnership information-return penalty may apply for inaccurate or late reporting.

Director personal liability: General partners may bear contractual and statutory partnership liability depending on the partnership agreement and Alberta law. Limited partners may lose limited-liability protection for conduct inconsistent with applicable partnership law or may incur personal tax liability for their allocated income.
GST/HST Fraud and Improper Input Tax Credits

Statutory sanction: The CRA may deny credits, assess tax, interest, gross-negligence or fraud penalties, freeze refunds, and prosecute. Fraudulent GST/HST conduct may result in substantial fines and imprisonment under federal tax legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for unremitted GST/HST where statutory conditions are satisfied. Individuals who knowingly create or use false invoices may face personal criminal liability.
Payroll and Employment Tax Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: The employer may be assessed for source deductions, employer contributions, interest, penalties, and information-return failures. Deliberate evasion may be prosecuted.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted employee source deductions under the Income Tax Act and may face criminal consequences for deliberate falsification or obstruction.
Shell Entity, Sham Transactions, and Beneficial-Ownership Concealment

Statutory sanction: A shell entity is not unlawful merely because it has limited operations. Liability arises from the underlying conduct, which may trigger reassessment under anti-avoidance and sham doctrines, denial of treaty benefits, penalties, forfeiture, and criminal prosecution for fraud, tax evasion, or money laundering.

Director personal liability: Individuals may face civil restitution, asset tracing, personal tax assessments, fines, imprisonment, and director or officer liability where they directed or assisted the misconduct. Travel restrictions are not automatic but may arise from bail conditions, court orders, immigration consequences, or enforcement proceedings.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act may impose administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, reporting obligations, and criminal prosecution. Money laundering offences under the Criminal Code can result in significant fines, forfeiture, and imprisonment, including imprisonment of up to 10 years for indictable offences depending on the charge.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, partners, and employees may be prosecuted personally where they participate in or facilitate the conduct. Courts may order forfeiture and restitution. Bail, sentencing, or immigration measures may restrict travel, but no automatic travel ban applies solely because an entity receives an AML classification.
Books, Records, and CRA Obstruction

Statutory sanction: The CRA may impose record-keeping and failure-to-provide penalties, draw adverse inferences, reassess on available information, suspend refunds, and pursue prosecution for obstruction or tax offences.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, partners, and advisers may face personal prosecution for obstruction or destruction of records. Civil protection is not available for deliberate concealment or misleading conduct.
Unlawful Corporate or Partnership Dissolution and Asset Stripping

Statutory sanction: The CRA and other creditors may pursue collections, reassess transferees, challenge transfers, impose interest and penalties, and seek civil remedies under insolvency and fraudulent-conveyance law.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, and persons receiving diverted assets may face personal restitution, director-liability assessments, clawback proceedings, injunctions, and in serious cases criminal fraud charges. Travel bans are not an automatic tax consequence but may arise through criminal or insolvency court orders.
