Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital. Limited partners' capital contributions are fixed contractually in the Limited Partnership Agreement; no escrow account, deposit, or paid-up capital filing is mandated by Alberta law for limited partnerships.

Stage 03

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; official portals are Canada.ca Business Registration Online and My Business Account (MyBA). The Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) administers export declarations. Alberta has no provincial sales tax and no separate LP income tax at entity level; Alberta corporate income tax is administered in coordination with the CRA.

E-government filing portal

A foreign or domestic Alberta Limited Partnership obtains its federal Business Number (BN) by filing CRA Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number). The filing must include certified formation documents, the Alberta LP registration extract, the partnership agreement, and a completed CRA authorized-representative form. If the LP will be required to collect GST/HST, it must add an RT program account; this is mandatory where worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or where the LP voluntarily elects registration to recover input tax credits. Non-resident LPs without a Canadian-resident partner or director should use CRA International and Non-Resident Services, may be required to appoint a Canadian representative for service of documents, and should submit paper Form RC1 where online registration is unavailable.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — A foreign or domestic Alberta Limited Partnership obtains its federal Business Number (BN) by filing CRA Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number). The filing must include certified formation documents, the Alberta LP registration extract, the partnership agreement, and a completed CRA authorized-representative form. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a check-the-box or Form 8832 entity-classification election. An Alberta LP is a flow-through vehicle for federal and provincial income tax: income is computed at the partnership level and allocated to partners, and only the partners are taxed. For GST/HST purposes, the LP is itself a person and may elect to register voluntarily even below the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold. Non-resident partners are taxable only on Canadian-source income allocated from the LP, subject to treaty relief. Canada has statutory hybrid mismatch rules for certain cross-border arrangements, but no elective hybrid-mismatch classification mechanism is available to an LP.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable GST/HST supplies in a single calendar quarter, tested on the same basis over the preceding four calendar quarters. Once the threshold is exceeded, the LP ceases to be a small supplier and must register for GST/HST; voluntary registration is available below the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada qualify for the 0% GST/HST rate where ownership is transferred to a non-resident and the goods are exported or delivered to a carrier for export within the statutory export window under the Excise Tax Act. International air cargo, air charter, and related freight brokerage services may be zero-rated where they constitute international transportation services or supplies directly connected to exported goods and are supported by contemporaneous export documentation. Zero-rating is not automatic merely because the customer is non-resident; the supply must satisfy the applicable place-of-supply and export documentation rules. A broker must document the departure and destination airports, the foreign principal relationship, and the cargo export classification to sustain the 0% rate and any related input tax credit refund claim.

  • Customs export declaration or CBSA eManifest export reporting data evidencing the Canadian export
  • Bill of lading or original air waybill showing international routing and cargo description
  • Commercial invoice and freight brokerage invoice for the specific export movement
  • Cargo manifest and aircraft charter agreement evidencing the air cargo charter
  • Inward remittance certificate or SWIFT/Fedwire receipt evidencing FX repatriation matching the export invoice
  • Signed foreign principal agreement confirming the non-resident customer and destination

There is no CRA real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or mandatory e-invoicing regime currently in force. GST/HST invoices may be issued in paper or electronic form if they contain the prescribed information under the Excise Tax Act. CRA accepts authenticated electronic records for audit; Pan-Canadian B2B e-invoicing interoperability is being developed on a voluntary basis and is not a condition of export zero-rating at this stage.

CRA may reassess a zero-rated export within the normal four-year limitation period, and without limitation where there is misrepresentation, fraud, or willful default. If export documentation is insufficient, CRA will recharacterize the supply as taxable at 5%, assess interest, deny or claw back any related input tax credits and refunds, and may impose a gross-negligence penalty equal to 25% of the net tax understated under the Excise Tax Act. Knowingly false zero-rating claims, fabricated export documents, or fraudulent refund applications may result in additional civil penalties, criminal prosecution, fines, and CBSA administrative enforcement measures. The 180-day FX repatriation deadline is a compliance covenant of the underlying task model; failure to repatriate does not itself invalidate the zero-rating assessment but materially weakens CRA documentary proof of a genuine export transaction.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility under IMF Article VIII status and the OECD Code of Liberalisation of Capital Movements. Both current-account and capital-account transactions, including non-resident limited-partner capital contributions, dividend and profit remittances, and repatriation of liquidation proceeds, are free from exchange-control approval or compulsory conversion. The only frictions are tax-driven: payments of income to non-resident partners are subject to Part XIII withholding tax (generally 15% to 25% under applicable tax treaties) and must be reported on NR4 slips; banks independently apply AML, sanctions and beneficial-ownership screening. There are no central-bank restrictions on holding foreign currency, opening foreign-currency accounts, or settling cross-border trade invoices in any G10 or freely convertible currency.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None applicable. Canadian law imposes no statutory timeline for repatriation of export proceeds, no mandatory surrender ratio, and no obligation to convert air-cargo charter brokerage receipts into Canadian dollars. Export proceeds may be retained in any freely convertible currency in a domestic or offshore account, commingled with working capital, and converted at market-determined rates. Banks must report cross-border wire transfers of CAD 10,000 or more to FINTRAC, but this is a reporting obligation on the financial institution, not a surrender or conversion requirement on the exporter.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary authority and issuer of the Canadian dollar; the Minister of Finance acting through the Exchange Fund Account for official foreign-exchange reserves; the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential supervision of foreign-exchange positions of authorized deposit-taking institutions; FINTRAC for cross-border wire-transfer reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Canada maintains no dedicated foreign-exchange-control authority because the currency is freely floated and all current-account and capital-account transactions are fully liberalized.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Limited Auxiliary Viability - Non-Core. Foreign digital EMIs (including Wise, Airwallex, and similar payment-fintech firms) can support low-value auxiliary disbursements in the CAD 10,000 to 50,000 band, but are not acceptable as the primary escrow, operating, or liquidity account for an Alberta Limited Partnership engaged in air-cargo charter brokering. Legal constraints: any EMI holding or moving Canadian-dollar settlement funds must be registered as a Money Services Business with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA and licensed as a Payment Service Provider under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), supervised by the Bank of Canada. Offshore EMIs that lack a Canadian-registered entity, a domestic settlement account at a Schedule I bank, and discretionary access to Lynx cannot lawfully provide CAD-clearing services. The Medium_Risk_Enhanced AML classification of air-cargo brokerage favors direct, regulated relationships with Schedule I banks; fintech rails are acceptable only for sub-threshold testing, small-amount refunds, or contingency disbursements.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Opaque beneficial-ownership chain caused by multiple non-resident limited partners, nominee LP interests, or a foreign corporate General Partner.

Root cause: Opaque beneficial-ownership chain caused by multiple non-resident limited partners, nominee LP interests, or a foreign corporate General Partner.

Defense evidence: Certified copy of the Alberta LP registration certificate; complete Limited Partnership Agreement with the capital contribution schedule; register of limited partners and any nominee holdings; General Partner certificate of incumbency; current Alberta Corporate Registry search; FINTRAC-compliant beneficial-ownership chart identifying all individuals with significant control (25% or more) supported by passport or national-ID copies; written Canadian tax-counsel opinion confirming the pass-through classification of the LP and the non-resident partners' tax treaty eligibility.
Sanctions and export-control exposure arising from cargo destinations, foreign flag-state aircraft, or charter counterparties linked to restricted jurisdictions (SEMA, United Nations Act, and OFAC for USD-cleared flows).

Root cause: Sanctions and export-control exposure arising from cargo destinations, foreign flag-state aircraft, or charter counterparties linked to restricted jurisdictions (SEMA, United Nations Act, and OFAC for USD-cleared flows).

Defense evidence: Copy of the entity's sanctions-compliance manual aligned with the Special Economic Measures Act, the United Nations Act, and OFAC regulations; negative-hit screening report for all charterers, sub-charterers, aircraft registrations, and beneficial owners; aircraft registry extract and CTA operating licence of the underlying air carrier; cargo-screening log showing the broker does not arrange movement of goods controlled under the Export and Import Permits Act (EIPA); signed attestation of no business with airlines on Consolidated Canadian Autonomous Sanctions List, OFAC SDN list, or any UN sanctions list.
High-value MT103 outbound wires to offshore intermediaries, aircraft lessors, or FBO service providers with inconsistent invoice patterns.

Root cause: High-value MT103 outbound wires to offshore intermediaries, aircraft lessors, or FBO service providers with inconsistent invoice patterns.

Defense evidence: Signed Charter Agreement, Capacity Purchase Agreement, or Aircraft Sublease Agreement under which the brokerage commission is earned; broker invoice and carrier invoice reconciliation pack covering the preceding and projected 90-day settlement cycle; schedule of intermediary payments with the commercial justification for each tier; Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) of every counterparty; three months of bank statements evidencing the cash-flow cycle and commission margin.
Politically Exposed Persons (PEP) or state-owned enterprises involved in charter financing, aircraft ownership, or government cargo tenders.

Root cause: Politically Exposed Persons (PEP) or state-owned enterprises involved in charter financing, aircraft ownership, or government cargo tenders.

Defense evidence: World-Check, Dow Jones, or Refinitiv PEP screening results for all partners, directors, and beneficial owners; source-of-wealth and source-of-funds declaration for any PEP-linked capital contribution; enhanced-monitoring plan approved by the bank's Financial Crime Unit covering PEP transactions; copy of the government procurement contract or state-owned airline charter agreement with redaction of commercially sensitive clauses.
FATCA and CRS classification ambiguity where the LP could be treated as a passive NFFE, exposing the bank to US withholding and information-reporting risk.

Root cause: FATCA and CRS classification ambiguity where the LP could be treated as a passive NFFE, exposing the bank to US withholding and information-reporting risk.

Defense evidence: IRS Form W-8BEN-E (or W-9 for US partners) correctly classifying the LP as an active non-financial foreign entity (NFFE) based on the freight-arrangement brokerage activity; CRS self-certification forms for the LP and all non-resident limited partners; the LP's principal-purpose test analysis confirming active air-cargo charter brokerage income rather than passive investment; Canada Revenue Agency T5013 partnership return excerpt evidencing ordinary commercial business activity; confirmation letter that no US limited partner holds an indirect interest absent proper FATCA documentation.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Non-resident limited partners inject capital via SWIFT MT103 to the designated Capital Escrow Account at a Schedule I bank. Field 70 (Remittance Information) must state LP capital contribution under the Alberta Limited Partnership Agreement dated [X]; field 50K must show the remitter's full legal name; and the bank account must already have completed FATCA and CRS self-certifications. Funds may be received in the original foreign currency and either retained in a foreign-currency sub-account or converted to CAD at the bank's spot rate without central-bank approval. For any inbound wire of CAD 10,000 or more, the receiving bank files a FINTRAC cross-border wire report; no purpose-code authorization, foreign-investment screening, or surrender obligation attaches to the contribution.Settlement of cargo charter invoices, aircraft lease payments, fuel purchases, landing fees, and brokerage payouts uses SWIFT MT103 for CAD and cross-currency wires, MT202 for interbank cover between correspondent institutions, and Fedwire or CHIPS for USD-denominated disbursements through the bank's US clearing arm. CAD-denominated high-value disbursements settle through Lynx, Payments Canada's real-time gross settlement system, providing finality on the same business day. Commercial letters of credit (SWIFT MT700) and documentary collections (SWIFT MT4xx) are available for cargo purchases, aircraft sub-leases, or cartage settlements where a documentary condition must be satisfied. All outbound wires of CAD 10,000 or more are reported to FINTRAC, and the bank will apply the broker's counterparty due-diligence protocol to every payment instruction. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal 15% + Alberta 8% = 23% general corporate; 9% federal + 2% Alberta = 11% for Canadian-controlled private corporation on qualifying small business income. The Alberta Limited Partnership itself is fiscally transparent and has no statutory corporate rate; partners include the LP income at their resident rates.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (ITA) for dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident. An Alberta LP is fiscally transparent and does not pay dividends; actual distributions of accumulated capital or profit to a non-resident partner are generally not subject to Part XIII dividend withholding. The non-resident partner's allocated share of Canadian-source business income is taxed under Part I on a net basis, subject to treaty protection if no Canadian permanent establishment exists.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: T106 information return reporting is mandatory for an Alberta LP with reportable non-arm's-length cross-border transactions where the aggregate fair market value exceeds CAD 1,000,000; country-by-country report is required for an MNE group with consolidated revenue of at least CAD 750 million. Contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation and a penalty-protection file should be prepared whenever any cross-border transaction with related parties exceeds this threshold, even if the LP is not the ultimate parent.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have formal Economic Substance Regulations as applied in Gulf Cooperation Council states. However, CRA may apply the general anti-avoidance rule (ITA s.245), section 247 transfer pricing rules, and common-law sham doctrine to disregard an Alberta LP if it lacks real activities, independent contractual capacity, staff, premises, and business purpose. The LP must maintain board minutes, a substantive partnership agreement, actual cash flows, and documented commercial operations; otherwise CRA can reattribute income to a Canadian permanent establishment and impose gross-negligence penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Gross negligence penalty equal to 50% of the tax understated (ITA s.163(2)); criminal fine of 50%-200% of the tax evaded and/or up to 5 years imprisonment (ITA s.239); CRA may issue a direction under section 240(3) ordering travel restrictions or seizure of passport on criminal charges.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners who directed, acquiesced in, or signed false returns are jointly liable for the corporation/LP's tax debts where withholdings were not remitted (ITA s.227.1); court-ordered restitution, departure bans, and imprisonment can be imposed on individuals.
Shell Company or Sham Partnership

Statutory sanction: CRA denies the tax benefit under GAAR (ITA s.245), reassesses partners on allocated income plus interest; sham transactions are void at common law; penalties up to 50% of the misrepresented amount under s.163(2); Alberta registrar may strike the LP and impose reinstatement fees.

Director personal liability: General partners are personally liable at common law for debts and taxes incurred in the name of a sham LP; directors who signed declarations of compliance may be liable for false representations; in serious fraud, Criminal Code prosecutions, asset freezes, and imprisonment may follow.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment using the arm's-length price; penalty equal to 10% of the net upward transfer pricing adjustment (ITA s.247(3)); interest on overdue taxes; T106 late filing penalty of CAD 100 per day up to CAD 2,500.

Director personal liability: CRA may assess directors under ITA s.227.1 for unremitted withholding at source; for transfer pricing penalties the adjustment is normally levied on the entity, but directors who cause false records may face criminal liability under s.239.
AML/ATF and FINTRAC Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties under PCMLTFA up to CAD 1 million per violation for individuals and up to CAD 2 million for entities; knowing false reports can lead to imprisonment for money laundering under Criminal Code s.462.31; FINTRAC can publish the name and impose suspension.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who directed, authorized, assented to, or acquiesced in an offence are parties to the offence and liable on summary conviction to fines or imprisonment; senior officers may be banned from financial activities.
