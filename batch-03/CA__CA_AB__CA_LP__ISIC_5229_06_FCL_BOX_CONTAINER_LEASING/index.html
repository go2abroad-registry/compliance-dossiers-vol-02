Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada — national authority for GST/HST and federal income tax; Alberta provincial corporate income tax is administered by Alberta Treasury Board and Finance, Tax and Revenue Administration (ATRA). Online portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services.html) and ATRA Corporate Access.

E-government filing portal

A non-resident limited partnership must obtain a federal Business Number (BN15) by registering with the CRA, either online through Business Registration Online or by paper Form RC1/RC151, accompanied by foreign entity formation documents, proof of a Canadian address for service or authorized Canadian representative, and partner information. If the partnership is a GST/HST registrant, the BN is combined with a GST/HST program account (RT0001). Alberta imposes no separate provincial sales tax registration. The LP files annual Form T5013 (Statement of Partnership Income); income is allocated to partners who pay federal and Alberta income tax at the partner level. GST/HST registration is mandatory once taxable supplies in Canada exceed the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold, unless the partnership is engaged exclusively in zero-rated export supplies and can obtain a refund of input tax credits through a voluntary registration.

Non-resident partners remain taxable in Canada on their allocable share of Canadian-source business income attributable to a permanent establishment in Alberta. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — Under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and the Alberta Partnership Act, a limited partnership is fiscally transparent; no entity-level income tax is imposed except in the limited circumstances of an entity-level GST/HST obligation and T5013 informational filing. Non-resident partners remain taxable in Canada on their allocable share of Canadian-source business income attributable to a permanent establishment in Alberta. Tax-exempt zones: none exist in Canada.

Available election forms

Canada does not offer a US-style check-the-box classification election. For partnerships, relevant statutory elections include: (i) section 97(2) rollover election on eligible property transfers to the partnership; (ii) partnership fiscal-period elections under section 249.1; (iii) non-resident partner election under subsection 216(1) to file a net-basis return for Canadian-source rents or royalties; and (iv) voluntary GST/HST registration election for non-resident suppliers. Hybrid mismatch arrangements are addressed by mandatory rules under sections 18.2 and 18.3 of the Income Tax Act (BEPS Action 2), with no elective opt-out.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the GST/HST small-supplier registration threshold is reached when total consideration from worldwide taxable supplies (excluding substantial capital property sales) exceeds CAD 30,000 in any calendar quarter or any set of four consecutive calendar quarters immediately preceding a supply in Canada. Non-resident partnerships carrying on business in Canada, or supplying container leasing services that are not zero-rated, must register when this threshold is exceeded; voluntary registration may be elected to offset GST paid on Canadian overheads.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Schedule VI, Part V to the Excise Tax Act, a supply of tangible personal property (including cargo containers) is zero-rated for GST/HST purposes when the property is exported by the supplier or by the customer as part of the supply, or where the supplier delivers the property to a common carrier for export from Canada. For intermodal container leasing, the lease of a freight container is zero-rated if the container is exported, or if the supply is made to a non-resident of Canada and the container is not used in Canada. Documentary proof must demonstrate that the container left Canada within a reasonable time following the supply and that the supplier had no indicia that the goods were consumed in Canada. Where the lessee is a non-resident and the container remains outside Canada throughout the lease, the supply qualifies for the zero-rated 'use outside Canada' exemption. Input tax credits on Canadian overheads allocable to zero-rated supplies are recoverable via the ordinary GST/HST refund mechanism.

  • Canadian Customs Export Declaration (Form B13/A50 equivalent or CBSA electronic export declaration through CARM/CCS/EDI)
  • Commercial intermodal bill of lading or through-transport document showing the container is exported from Canada to a foreign destination
  • Inward remittance certificate or equivalent proof of foreign exchange receipt from the non-resident lessee (e.g., SWIFT MT103, bank wire advice, treasury confirmation)

Canada has no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance system, no digital stamping or central electronic-invoicing clearance platform, and no mandatory API-based e-invoice clearance for GST/HST. However, registrants must issue prescribed GST/HST invoices containing the supplier's business number, invoice date, description, consideration, and GST/HST rate or zero-rating indication. Export declarations must be filed electronically with the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) through the relevant electronic system such as CARM or approved EDI/CCS before or on export. Books and records, including e-documents, must be retained for at least six years. CRA accepts electronic returns via NETFILE/XML and may impose penalties for failure to issue proper invoices or filing returns electronically when required.

Fraudulent or inadequately documented export zero-rating claims expose the LP to CRA reassessment with arrear interest, recapture of input tax credits, denial of refunds, and cancellation of GST/HST registration. Penalties include a 5% late/incorrect filing penalty plus 1% per month to 12 months, and a 50% gross negligence penalty under section 280 of the Excise Tax Act. In cases of misrepresentation, fraud, or neglect, the normal four-year reassessment period is extended by eight years and may be unlimited where misrepresentation is fraudulent. Criminal prosecution can result in fines up to CAD 1,000,000 and imprisonment. The LP should maintain a complete export-evidence chain (export declaration, B/L, freight invoices, bank receipt, container tail numbers) to withstand CRA/CBSA audit and include contractual clawback/indemnity provisions governing export documentation deficiencies.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely floating, fully convertible, and subject to no exchange-control statutes, licensing regimes, investment-screening approvals, or remittance restrictions. Canada accepts International Monetary Fund Article VIII obligations and adheres to the OECD Code of Liberalisation of Capital Movements, so both current-account and capital-account flows (capital calls, LP distributions, lease receivables, interest, dividends) are unrestricted. The only practical constraints are fiscal: an Alberta limited partnership must obtain a Canada Revenue Agency Business Number, non-resident limited partners are taxable on allocable Canadian-source business income under Part I of the Income Tax Act, and certain categories of cross-border payments (interest, rents, royalties, management fees) attract Part XIII withholding at 25% unless reduced by an applicable income tax treaty. No central-bank or foreign-exchange-administration clearance is required for an inbound capital injection or for profit remittance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable: Canada imposes no statutory surrender ratio, no prescribed conversion timeline, and no mandatory repatriation requirement for export or leasing proceeds. Container-leasing receipts denominated in USD, EUR, CNH, or other currencies may be retained indefinitely in a foreign-currency operating account at a Canadian bank or deployed directly for offshore trade payables. The only binding obligations are FINTRAC reportability for electronic funds transfers and cross-border cash movements at or above CAD 10,000, the record-keeping and identity-verification duties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and its Regulations, and ordinary OSFI prudential expectations on counterparty and currency-risk management. None of these creates a conversion or surrender duty.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), operating with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as prudential regulator of federally regulated deposit-taking institutions and with the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as the AML/ATF supervisor; the Department of Finance Canada administers the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act. Canada maintains no dedicated Foreign Exchange Administration.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for operational payment overlays, Low as a substitute for a core treasury bank. Payment service providers active in Canada must be registered as PSPs with the Bank of Canada under Part 1 of the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) and, where acting as money services businesses, registered with FINTRAC; however, an electronic-money institution is not a bank, cannot accept deposits by way of business, cannot provide capital-escrow or deposit-type liquidity, and cannot issue documentary credits. A PSP may lawfully provide virtual-account rails, receivables concentration, FX conversion, and small-value cross-border payables processing, all subject to the same CAD 10,000 FINTRAC EFT-reporting and record-keeping duties. Recommended architecture: Schedule I bank account as the principal treasury and escrow vehicle, with the EMI retained strictly as a payment-rail overlay for low-value operational disbursements.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound partner contributions are wired in CAD, USD, or EUR via SWIFT MT103 (or MT202 COV for upstream interbank transfers) to the account of the Alberta limited partnership operated by its General Partner at a Canadian Schedule I bank, using the bank's New York or London correspondent. The beneficiary field must carry the LP's account number and the reference field (field 70) must identify the transfer as 'CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION — ALBERTA LIMITED PARTNERSHIP — CAPITAL CALL', with the corresponding capital-call notice and subscription agreement retained in the KYC and treasury file. Every transfer at or above CAD 10,000 is reported to FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA's EFT-reporting rules and carries full originator name, address, and account data. A PSP may lawfully provide virtual-account rails, receivables concentration, FX conversion, and small-value cross-border payables processing, all subject to the same CAD 10,000 FINTRAC EFT-reporting and record-keeping duties. Recommended architecture: Schedule I bank account as the principal treasury and escrow vehicle, with the EMI retained strictly as a payment-rail overlay for low-value operational disbursements.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

0% at the Alberta limited partnership level where the partnership is fiscally transparent; taxable income is allocated to partners. Corporate partners are generally subject to combined federal and Alberta rates of 23% for general business income and 11% for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation small-business income, subject to applicable limits and facts.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding generally applies to taxable Canadian-source dividends paid or credited to non-residents. A Canadian limited partnership normally distributes partnership allocations rather than dividends; the correct withholding regime depends on the legal payment and income characterization. Treaty rates commonly reduce Canadian corporate dividends to 5% for a qualifying corporate parent and 15% in other treaty cases, subject to the applicable treaty, ownership tests, limitation provisions and beneficial-ownership requirements.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose one universal transaction-value threshold that eliminates the arm's-length requirement. Contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is required for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions, subject to statutory exceptions. Form T106 reporting is generally subject to the aggregate non-arm's-length non-resident transaction threshold of CAD 1,000,000, with detailed reporting requirements and exceptions under the Income Tax Act. Canadian Local File and Master File concepts are generally implemented through contemporaneous documentation expectations; Country-by-Country reporting applies to qualifying multinational enterprise groups with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone economic-substance regulation equivalent to a separate ESR regime. Substance is assessed through the Income Tax Act, transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, beneficial-ownership principles, treaty principal-purpose tests, specific anti-avoidance provisions and judicial analysis of the true legal and commercial arrangement. The LP should maintain Canadian commercial rationale, genuine leasing operations, asset-management capability, decision-making records, arm's-length contracts, adequate capitalization, reliable books and records, and evidence that risks and functions are actually controlled by the entities claiming the related returns.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years. Civil reassessment, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties and repeated-failure penalties may also apply.

Director personal liability: An LP has no directors in the corporate sense, but general partners, managing partners and responsible officers may incur personal criminal or civil exposure where they participated in, authorized or acquiesced in the conduct. Corporate limited liability is not a shield against personal fraud or criminal acts.
Transfer-Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: CRA may make a transfer-pricing adjustment, impose Part XVI.1 consequences, assess arrears interest and impose a penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions for a transfer-pricing penalty are met and reasonable efforts are absent.

Director personal liability: Responsible partners or officers may face personal exposure for knowing participation in fraud or deliberate misrepresentation. Ordinary transfer-pricing errors generally create entity and partner tax liability rather than automatic personal imprisonment.
Treaty Abuse and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny the reduced treaty rate, assess the unpaid 25% withholding, interest and penalties, and apply the general anti-avoidance rule or treaty anti-abuse provisions.

Director personal liability: General partners and officers who knowingly certify false treaty information may face civil assessments, director or responsible-person liability where statutory conditions are met, and criminal exposure for fraud or tax evasion.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Part XIII Tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be liable for the unremitted tax, interest and applicable failure-to-withhold penalties. CRA may assess the payer even where the non-resident recipient did not receive the expected net amount.

Director personal liability: For an applicable corporate payer, directors may face statutory director liability for unremitted source deductions after the statutory prerequisites are satisfied. For an LP, the general partner and responsible persons may be pursued under applicable partnership and tax-law rules.
Partnership Return and Information-Return Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Daily late-filing penalties, fixed information-return penalties, reassessment of partners, denial of unsupported deductions, interest and gross-negligence penalties may apply.

Director personal liability: Limited partners are generally not personally liable solely because of filing failures if they remain passive and preserve limited-liability status. General partners and persons directing the filing may be exposed for statutory, contractual, negligent or criminal conduct.
GST/HST and Tax-Collection Failure

Statutory sanction: Assessment of uncollected tax, interest, late-filing penalties, gross-negligence penalties and denial of input tax credits. Knowingly issuing false invoices or claims may create criminal exposure.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors of a corporate general partner may face statutory director liability after CRA enforcement prerequisites are met. General partners and persons involved in intentional misappropriation may face personal civil or criminal liability.
Shell Company, Sham Leasing and Economic-Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: Denial of deductions, recharacterization of payments, transfer-pricing adjustments, withholding assessments, GAAR application, penalties, interest and possible loss of treaty benefits.

Director personal liability: Canadian law does not impose an automatic corporate-veil piercing rule for tax substance disputes. Courts may nevertheless impose personal liability for fraud, deceit, knowing participation, breach of fiduciary duty or statutory source-deduction liability. Travel bans are not an automatic Canadian tax sanction; imprisonment may follow only from applicable criminal conviction.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Sanctions Breach

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, reporting restrictions, seizure or forfeiture, and criminal penalties under applicable Canadian AML, Criminal Code and sanctions legislation. Penalties depend on the regulated status of the business and the specific offence.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners and officers may be personally liable for knowing participation, authorization or acquiescence. Criminal offences may result in fines and imprisonment. No automatic travel ban applies, but court orders and enforcement measures may restrict movement in particular cases.
Loss of Limited-Partnership Protection

Statutory sanction: The partner may lose limited-liability protection under applicable Alberta partnership law, become exposed to partnership obligations, and face contractual, tax and insolvency consequences.

Director personal liability: The exposed limited partner may become personally liable for partnership debts and obligations. Mere ownership, consultation or protective rights do not automatically create personal liability, but conduct must be reviewed against the Alberta Partnership Act and the partnership agreement.
