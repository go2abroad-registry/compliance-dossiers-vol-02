Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — Business Registration Online (BRO) and My Business Account (MyBA) portal; Alberta Corporate Registry (Service Alberta) for the statutory registration of the Limited Partnership

E-government filing portal

A non-resident establishing an Alberta Limited Partnership must: (1) register the LP with the Alberta Corporate Registry under the Partnership Act, RSA 2000, c L-8, filing the required LP declaration and obtaining the Alberta registration/file number; (2) apply to the CRA for a federal Business Number (BN) and a Goods and Services Tax/Harmonized Sales Tax (GST/HST) account (account prefix RT), plus any payroll (RP) account, using Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1; the general partner must identify all non-resident partners and the foreign address of the partnership; (3) if the LP is a non-resident person carrying on business in Canada, comply with CRA non-resident registration requirements, including the appointment of a Canadian representative for service of documents where applicable; (4) receive the CRA Confirmation of Registration and use the BN in all GST/HST filings, export documentation, platform onboarding, and customs processes; (5) maintain CRA records and books for at least six years.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — the Alberta Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent under the Income Tax Act, R.S.C. 1985, c.1 (5th Supp.), s. 96; the partnership itself is not an income taxpayer and each partner, including non-resident partners, is taxable on its allocated share of income. For GST/HST purposes, the LP is a deemed 'person' under the Excise Tax Act, s. 123(1) as extended to partnerships, and may itself be a registered supplier carrying on a commercial activity in Canada.

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a US-style check-the-box regime; tax classification follows the facts and statute. Available statutory elections for the LP class include: (i) Income Tax Act s. 97(2) and s. 85(2) rollover elections on a tax-deferred transfer of eligible property into the partnership; (ii) Income Tax Act s. 249.1 election to adopt a non-calendar-year partnership fiscal period; (iii) Excise Tax Act Part IX elections relevant to cross-border and zero-rated supplies, including simplified documentation elections for exporters and the election to self-assess/rebate GST/HST on imported services; and (iv) for US-resident partners, the US federal check-the-box election (Form 8832) may independently determine the LP's US classification. Hybrid mismatch treatment is mandatory under Canada's implementation of OECD BEPS Action 2 rules and does not require or permit an election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual taxable supplies made in Canada (small supplier threshold, Excise Tax Act s. 148(1)(d)); registration is mandatory for a non-resident or domestic person carrying on a commercial activity in Canada whose taxable supplies exceed the threshold. Once registered, the LP must charge, collect, and remit 5% GST on its taxable supplies of prepared meals and delivery-support services unless a zero-rated export supply applies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is claimable when the ghost kitchen/cloud kitchen makes a qualifying export supply of goods under Excise Tax Act ss. 21-23 and Schedule VI, Part I, or a service supplied to a non-resident recipient under Part V, provided the supplier is the exporter-in-fact: title and risk of loss must pass to the foreign recipient, the goods must be physically shipped from Canada to a destination outside Canada, and contemporaneous commercial evidence must prove the foreign destination and settlement. Merely supplying goods through a domestic delivery aggregator to a foreign delivery address, without proper export evidence, does not qualify for the 0% rate.

  • Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) validated export declaration (B13A / Canada Automated Export Declaration (CAED) electronic export report), including the courier/consolidator proof of export where applicable
  • Commercial invoice with applicable Incoterms showing that title and risk of loss transferred to the foreign recipient
  • Bill of Lading, air waybill, or carrier export manifest evidencing final destination outside Canada
  • Foreign purchase order or food-delivery platform settlement order (e.g., UberEats Marketplace Export Settlement, DoorDash Export Reconciliation) attaching the foreign jurisdiction
  • Proof of foreign currency settlement, including the inward remittance certificate or platform payout record referencing the foreign bank account
  • CRA zero-rating schedule or export declaration certificate prepared at the time of supply, if requested by the CRA auditor

Canada has no real-time fiscal clearance mandate, no continuous transaction control (CTC) system, and no digital stamping requirement. E-invoicing is voluntary under the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing framework (Peppol BIS 3.0). However, the LP must issue GST/HST compliant invoices containing all prescribed information under Excise Tax Act s. 221 and retain electronic books, records, and source documents for at least six years under ETA s. 286; CRA may require electronic records to be made available in readable format during audit.

The CRA may deny zero-rating retroactively and reassess the supply at 5% GST with statutory interest if export documentation is missing, incomplete, or contradicted by the transactional evidence. A false zero-rating claim can attract civil penalties for false statements and negligence under Excise Tax Act ss. 280 and 285, the extension of the normal four-year reassessment period where misrepresentation is attributable to carelessness, neglect, or fraud (Income Tax Act s. 152(4)), and criminal tax evasion prosecution under Income Tax Act s. 239 and the corresponding Excise Tax Act offence provisions. The LP and its general partner remain jointly and severally liable for GST/HST collections and may be required to refund uncollected GST.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible. Canada maintains no meaningful distinction between current-account and capital-account convertibility and imposes no exchange-control approvals for foreign direct investment or repatriation of capital and after-tax profits. An Alberta Limited Partnership may receive capital contributions from non-residents, hold foreign-currency accounts in Canada, and remit distributions and profit shares abroad without central bank licensing. The only structural hurdles are Canadian withholding taxes under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, GST/HST compliance, and FINTRAC mandatory reporting of large cross-border electronic funds transfers equal to or exceeding CAD 10,000 or equivalent.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable in Canada. There is no statutory timeline for export proceeds surrender and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. Export proceeds may be retained in CAD or any foreign currency. However, all receipts from goods or services, including delivery-platform payouts from foreign platform entities, must be included in the partnership’s taxable income and, where applicable, reported for GST/HST. Any wire transfer equal to or above the prescribed FINTRAC threshold must be reported by the financial institution.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as central monetary authority; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential banking supervision; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for anti-money laundering and cross-border currency transaction reporting. Canada does not maintain a separate foreign-exchange control administration.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium. Canada has no bank authorization regime for offshore EMI or fintech-issued IBAN accounts; only banks and provincial credit unions may accept deposits. Offshore payment fintechs may act as payment service providers or money services businesses but must comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), and must register with FINTRAC when carrying on MSB activity in Canada. Virtual IBAN and platform payout services are viable as supplementary settlement conduits for UberEats and DoorDash payouts, but they must not be the sole treasury infrastructure. The Alberta LP should maintain primary operating and escrow balances at a Schedule I bank and use fintech rails only for pass-through disbursements with zero float retention.

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Not applicable to a Canadian limited partnership (LP) that is treated as a partnership for Canadian income tax purposes. The LP generally computes income and allocates each partner’s share under the Income Tax Act; partners are taxed on their allocated shares. Alberta corporate income tax rates apply to taxable Alberta corporations, not to the LP as such.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

A partnership distribution is not, merely by being distributed, a dividend paid by a corporation. A non-resident partner is generally taxed on allocated partnership income under the applicable Canadian rules; withholding depends on the income’s character and source. If a Canadian corporation separately pays a dividend to a non-resident, the general Part XIII rate is 25%, subject to reduction under an applicable tax treaty.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a universal Local File/Master File filing threshold of the type used in some jurisdictions. Contemporaneous documentation under Income Tax Act section 247 is required for relevant non-arm’s-length cross-border transactions, with a statutory penalty safe-harbour condition generally tied to records being made by the documentation deadline and provided within three months of a CRA request. T106 reporting generally applies where its CAD 1 million aggregate transaction threshold and other conditions are met. Country-by-Country Reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated group revenue of at least CAD 1.125 billion in the preceding fiscal year, subject to the statutory rules.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the UAE ESR. Canadian tax consequences may nevertheless depend on the actual legal and economic arrangements, including transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, specific anti-avoidance provisions, beneficial ownership, treaty anti-abuse rules, and factual residence or permanent-establishment analysis. Maintain evidence that related-party charges reflect services actually performed and arm’s-length terms.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Failure to file partnership information returns or partner slips

Statutory sanction: Late-filing and information-return penalties, reassessments, interest, and possible penalties for knowingly or negligently making false statements. The amount depends on the return, the number of slips, duration, and conduct.

Director personal liability: An LP has a general partner rather than corporate directors as its managing person. The general partner may face contractual, statutory, or tax-related liability according to its legal form and the facts. Limited partners are not automatically liable for partnership tax debts merely because they are partners, but their protection depends on applicable Alberta law and their conduct.
Failure to withhold or remit non-resident tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for unremitted withholding, interest, and penalties under the Income Tax Act. Treaty relief does not excuse failure to establish entitlement or meet applicable administrative requirements.

Director personal liability: Where the payer is a corporation, director liability for unremitted source deductions may arise under statutory conditions, including due diligence defences. An LP’s general partner or responsible persons may face liability under the applicable statutory and entity rules. Criminal liability requires the relevant statutory elements and proof.
Transfer-pricing non-compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust income, impose interest, and assess a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the applicable net transfer-pricing adjustment where statutory thresholds and conditions are met. Treaty and competent-authority procedures may be available to address double taxation.

Director personal liability: No automatic veil-piercing or imprisonment arises solely from a transfer-pricing adjustment. Personal exposure requires a separate statutory basis or evidence of conduct meeting the elements of a civil or criminal offence.
Tax evasion, false statements, or deliberate concealment

Statutory sanction: Civil penalties, interest, reassessment beyond ordinary limitation periods in specified circumstances, and prosecution may apply. Tax evasion on conviction can result in fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years under the Income Tax Act; additional offences and sanctions may apply on the facts.

Director personal liability: A general partner, officer, or other individual may be prosecuted where the statutory elements and evidence establish personal participation or authorization. Imprisonment is not automatic; civil restitution, travel bans, and veil-piercing are not automatic tax penalties.
Undisclosed permanent establishment or Canadian business activity

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of Canadian tax, interest, failure-to-file penalties, and possible withholding or information-reporting liabilities may result. Treaty relief depends on the relevant treaty and facts.

Director personal liability: No automatic personal liability follows solely from a permanent-establishment finding. Liability requires an applicable statutory basis, such as a director-liability provision, or personal involvement in an offence.
Unreported sales, payroll, GST/HST, or platform settlement discrepancies

Statutory sanction: Assessments for tax, GST/HST, source deductions, interest, and applicable penalties may apply. GST/HST registration and collection obligations depend on the taxable supplies, registration thresholds, and business facts.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner may be subject to statutory liability for specified unremitted amounts if the legal conditions are met. For an LP, exposure depends on the general partner’s legal form, the applicable statute, and the individual’s conduct.
Money laundering, proceeds of crime, or deceptive ownership and payment flows

Statutory sanction: Criminal Code offences may result in significant fines, forfeiture, and imprisonment, depending on the offence and circumstances. FINTRAC obligations apply only to persons and activities covered by the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act; a ghost-kitchen operator is not automatically a reporting entity solely because it accepts delivery-platform payouts.

Director personal liability: Individuals may be prosecuted for personal participation, authorization, or other conduct meeting the offence elements. A corporate role alone does not establish criminal liability; travel restrictions and imprisonment require lawful process and applicable statutory grounds.
