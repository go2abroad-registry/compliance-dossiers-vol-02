Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); registration, filing and account-administration portal: CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) via https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/registering-your-business.html. Alberta has no provincial sales tax authority with respect to this stage; the Canada-Alberta Tax Collection Agreement delegates provincial corporate tax collection to the CRA, while the Alberta Corporate Registry (CORES) maintains the underlying entity registration records.

E-government filing portal

1) Characterize the Alberta Limited Partnership: fiscally transparent for income tax under ITA s.96, but a distinct 'person' for GST/HST; partnership-level registration, not partner-level registration, is the norm for the GST/HST account. 2) Determine compulsory registration: the LP must register for GST/HST once worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in any four consecutive calendar quarters (small-supplier threshold, ETA s.148); voluntary registration is available before the threshold including for non-resident partners. 3) Apply online via CRA BRO, or by paper Form RC1 Request for a Business Number, providing: LP legal name and Alberta registration number from CORES, fiscal year-end, principal business activity (NAICS 511210 / ISIC 6201 - Enterprise Cloud Software Applications Development), and for each general and limited partner - name, address and either Canadian SIN/BN or, for non-residents, a foreign tax identification number / Individual Tax Number (ITN) or passport data. 4) CRA issues a 9-digit Business Number (BN) and activates the GST/HST program account (suffix RT); if the LP later hires staff or forms a taxable corporation, add RP (payroll) and RC (corporate) accounts. 5) Post-registration: file GST/HST returns on the assigned frequency (monthly when annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 6 million; otherwise quarterly or annual); file the annual T5013 Partnership Information Return and issue T5013 slips to all partners. 6) For foreign-controlled LPs, documentary proof of central management and control (CMC) must be maintained because Canadian tax residency of the partnership follows CMC rather than the Alberta registered office address.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible (transparent partnership for ITA and Alberta income tax purposes under ITA s.96 and relevant Alberta provisions; the partnership is nonetheless a separate registrant/person for GST/HST; territorial scope: Canadian-resident partnership when central management and control is exercised in Canada; non-resident limited partners are not taxable merely by reason of passive investment, though Canadian-source income flows through to them).

Available election forms

No domestic 'check-the-box' election exists in Canada; partnership classification is statutorily transparent. Under US tax law the Alberta LP is an eligible entity (not a per se corporation under Treas. Reg. section 301.7701-2(b)(8)), so US partners may file Form 8832 and elect association; hybrid-mismatch consequences are mitigated by the Canada-US Income Tax Convention, Article IV(7). GST/HST elections available: (a) ETA s.156 election among closely related registrants to treat supplies as nil-consideration; (b) Quick Method election (ETA s.227) for simplified remittance by small registrants; (c) annual filing election for certain qualifying registrants; none of these alter the transparent income-tax classification of the LP.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (gross revenue from taxable supplies, including certain supplies deemed made in Canada, measured over four consecutive calendar quarters; the small-supplier threshold under ETA s.148(1)).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

SaaS exports to foreign B2B customers are zero-rated principally under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s.23 (supply of a service to a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST) if the service is not supplied in connection with real property in Canada, is not supplied in connection with tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance, and is not a supply of a service performed on goods in Canada; a separately characterized supply of computer software rights may also qualify under s.20 of the same Part. The non-resident recipient must not be a GST/HST registrant at the time tax becomes payable; a non-resident that carries on business in Canada may be deemed resident, in which case the supply is taxable in Canada. The supplier must retain documentary evidence of the recipient's non-resident status and of foreign delivery/use; no customs export declaration arises for pure cloud delivery, only for tangible media. Canada imposes no statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline; the 180-day figure in this dossier is the internal treasury benchmark for documenting consideration repatriation. The federal Digital Services Tax (enacted 2024, applying to very large groups with global revenue exceeding EUR 750 million and Canadian digital services revenue exceeding CAD 20 million) targets marketplace, advertising, social-media and user-data revenues and does not capture ordinary enterprise SaaS at this structure's scale.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Border Services Agency B13A) - required only when tangible media is exported; waived for pure digital SaaS delivery
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill - required only for physical media (e.g., installation servers, USB keys); not applicable to cloud-based delivery
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / SWIFT MT103 or equivalent bank credit confirmation evidencing FX repatriation from the non-resident customer within the treasury benchmark period
  • Signed enterprise SaaS master services agreement, order forms, SOW and service schedules identifying the non-resident customer's registered office, governing law and delivery locations
  • Non-resident status self-declaration (customer tax-residency certificate, W-8 style form, or equivalent) and written confirmation that the customer is not registered for GST/HST
  • Technical delivery evidence (account administration logs, cloud-metadata and IP geolocation outside Canada) to rebut any CRA challenge on place of supply

Canada does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance, API stamping, or digital seal mandate for B2B e-invoicing. Electronic invoices are admissible if they respect ETA s.286 record-keeping (books and records kept in Canada or remotely accessible to the CRA; six-year retention). The federal government is progressively requiring PEPPOL BIS 3.0 e-invoicing for B2G contracts, but B2B SaaS invoices remain commercially flexible. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically via GST/HST NETFILE (mandatory for registrants above the electronic filing threshold), and large registrants face electronic remittance requirements.

CRA may reassess an improperly zero-rated supply as taxable at 5% GST with prescribed-rate interest; the standard reassessment window is four years from the filing date (ETA s.298), but misrepresentation, neglect or fraud extends reassessment indefinitely. Civil penalties: ETA s.280-282 failure-to-remit penalties with monthly accruals; ETA s.285 false-statement/gross-negligence penalty equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the tax sought, in addition to the unpaid tax and interest. Criminal exposure: ETA s.327 tax evasion prosecution with fines of up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment. In an export zero-rating audit, CRA tests true recipient residency, the contractual nexus to Canadian property, and whether the service was actually consumed outside Canada; where the 'in connection with Canadian property' exceptions apply, the clawback converts the entire fee to taxable supply, with interest and penalties running from the original filing date. No separate foreign-exchange repatriation penalty exists under the ETA; the 180-day benchmark is used only as an internal documentary-control standard.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada provides unrestricted current and capital account convertibility consistent with IMF Article VIII obligations. The Canadian dollar is freely floating and fully convertible. Non-resident limited partners may repatriate capital and remit profits without exchange control approval, subject only to standard banking due diligence and, where applicable, withholding tax requirements under the Canada-Alberta income tax framework. There are no restrictions on dividend distributions, interest payments, or liquidation proceeds to non-residents, and no pre-clearance is required for cross-border fund movement.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no mandatory export proceeds surrender, conversion, or repatriation timeline. Export receipts denominated in foreign currency may be retained overseas, held in Canadian dollar deposit accounts, or converted through any authorized financial institution without Bank of Canada approval. Reporting obligations are limited to anti-money laundering thresholds under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), which require financial institutions to report inbound or outbound individual transactions of CAD 10,000 or more to FINTRAC.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (with the Department of Finance Canada as policy authority; Canada maintains no separate foreign exchange control administration)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium as a secondary payment facilitator, Low as a primary treasury bank. Offshore digital EMI platforms (e.g., Wise, Stripe Treasury, Payoneer) are not authorized to take deposits in Canada under the Bank Act and cannot provide full Canadian depository coverage. To operate in Canada, they must be registered as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA. For the Alberta Limited Partnership, such platforms may be used only for non-depository payment settlement, vendor disbursements, and cross-border payroll; the primary operating and capital escrow accounts must remain at a Schedule I Canadian chartered bank. Legal constraints: unregistered offshore EMIs are prohibited from soliciting Canadian clients; any use of an EMI without FINTRAC registration would generate an AML/CFT compliance deficiency.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Undisclosed limited partners or opaque beneficial ownership chain.

Root cause: Undisclosed limited partners or opaque beneficial ownership chain.

Defense evidence: Complete Limited Partnership agreement, register of limited partners, corporate organizational chart, OSF or Alberta Corporate Access System extract confirming registration, and individual identification (government-issued photo ID and proof of address) for every partner holding 10% or more of the partnership. For corporate partners, provide certified articles of incorporation, register of directors, and beneficial ownership disclosure in line with FINTRAC's Annex 1 requirements.
Non-resident partners domiciled in high-risk jurisdictions or subject to sanctions.

Root cause: Non-resident partners domiciled in high-risk jurisdictions or subject to sanctions.

Defense evidence: Tax residence certificates, evidence of substantive business operations, updated sanctions screening results, no ultimate beneficial owner nexus to sanctioned jurisdictions, and a completed bank-specific enhanced due diligence questionnaire. Include source of wealth statements and notarized confirmation of fund origin where aggregate capital contribution exceeds CAD 250,000.
Unclear or inconsistent source of initial capital injection.

Root cause: Unclear or inconsistent source of initial capital injection.

Defense evidence: Six months of bank statements for each contributing partner showing the source of funds, latest audited or reviewed financial statements, executed subscription agreements, capital call notices, and a formal source-of-funds declaration. For contributed assets or converted services, provide valuation reports and independent legal advice certificates.
Payment flow mismatch with the declared B2B enterprise SaaS business model.

Root cause: Payment flow mismatch with the declared B2B enterprise SaaS business model.

Defense evidence: Executed B2B software subscription agreements, invoicing trail (e.g., from Stripe Billing), merchant settlement statements, evidence of recurring revenue collection through Stripe or Wise, and detailed explanations for any large one-time, non-recurring transactions. Provide an annual projected cash-flow model aligned with the ISIC 6201 / NAICS 511210 classification.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Incoming capital contributions from non-resident limited partners should be routed via SWIFT MT103 over the correspondent banking network. Field 70 (Remittance Information) must clearly state 'LP CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION – ALBERTA LIMITED PARTNERSHIP' and refer to the specific capital call or subscription agreement. The beneficiary bank will require the partnership's OSF registration number and may request the supporting capital call documentation before crediting funds. For USD payments from US partners, use the bank's US intermediate/priority account (ABA routing number) to avoid foreign exchange conversion. No Canadian exchange control approval is required. Any single transfer of CAD 10,000 or more will be subject to the receiving bank's FINTRAC reporting under AML transaction thresholds, so the partnership should expect a written inquiry and provide the subscription documents promptly.Outbound disbursements for trade settlements, SaaS vendor procurement, affiliate payments, and profit distributions under B2B enterprise SaaS should be cleared via SWIFT MT103 for cross-border international payments, ensuring field 70 includes a narrative purpose code such as 'SOFTWARE LICENSING FEES' or 'PROFIT DISTRIBUTION TO LIMITED PARTNERS'. Domestic Canadian vendor payments should be cleared through Payments Canada using the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) for high-value same-day transactions or the Real-Time Rail (RTR) for immediate low-value payments. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium as a secondary payment facilitator, Low as a primary treasury bank. Offshore digital EMI platforms (e.g., Wise, Stripe Treasury, Payoneer) are not authorized to take deposits in Canada under the Bank Act and cannot provide full Canadian depository coverage. To operate in Canada, they must be registered as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA. For the Alberta Limited Partnership, such platforms may be used only for non-depository payment settlement, vendor disbursements, and cross-border payroll; the primary operating and capital escrow accounts must remain at a Schedule I Canadian chartered bank. Legal constraints: unregistered offshore EMIs are prohibited from soliciting Canadian clients; any use of an EMI without FINTRAC registration would generate an AML/CFT compliance deficiency.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not itself subject to federal or Alberta corporate income tax. Corporate partners are generally subject to a combined federal and Alberta rate of 23% on general active business income. Eligible Canadian-controlled private corporations may qualify for a combined small-business rate of approximately 11% on eligible income within applicable business limits. Federal and Alberta rates and eligibility rules must be confirmed for the relevant taxation year.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The statutory Canadian Part XIII withholding rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is generally 25%. A treaty may reduce the rate, commonly to 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting ownership requirements, or to 15% for other qualifying treaty residents. A limited partnership distribution is not automatically a dividend; its Canadian tax treatment depends on the character of the underlying income, the partner's status, and the applicable partnership and withholding provisions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T5013 return is generally due within five months after the end of the partnership's fiscal period. A partnership fiscal period is generally limited to 12 months and commonly ends on December 31, subject to statutory exceptions. Corporate partner T2 returns are generally due six months after the corporation's taxation year-end, with tax balances generally due two months after year-end or three months for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no universal statutory monetary threshold that automatically triggers a prescribed OECD-style Local File or Master File for every taxpayer. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation supporting arm's-length pricing for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. T106 reporting commonly applies where the aggregate amount of reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceeds CAD 1 million, subject to detailed statutory rules. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational enterprise groups with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year. The CRA may require documentation within three months of a written request.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a separate set of broadly applicable Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to certain offshore ESR regimes. Canadian analysis relies on legal and economic substance, the arm's-length principle, specific anti-avoidance provisions, the general anti-avoidance rule in section 245, sham and beneficial-ownership principles, specified anti-hybrid rules, thin-capitalization rules, foreign-affiliate rules, and permanent-establishment concepts. A SaaS LP should maintain genuine commercial functions, personnel or appropriately controlled service providers, decision-making, intellectual-property development or licensing evidence, customer and vendor contracts, risk control, capitalization, and contemporaneous pricing support. Substance does not replace arm's-length pricing or source-of-income analysis.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and deliberate false reporting

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act section 239, conviction may result in a fine ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to two years on summary conviction, or a fine ranging from 100% to 200% and imprisonment of up to five years on indictment. Administrative gross-negligence or false-statement penalties may also apply, commonly 50% of the understated tax or overclaimed amount, subject to the statutory rules.

Director personal liability: A director or officer may face criminal liability where personal participation, authorization, acquiescence, or wilful blindness is established. Directors of corporate remitters may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts under applicable statutory provisions, subject to due-diligence defenses and limitation periods. Canadian law does not impose an automatic travel ban or automatic veil piercing solely because a tax debt exists.
Improper partnership classification or sham LP

Statutory sanction: The CRA may recharacterize the arrangement, deny losses or deductions, apply the general anti-avoidance rule, assess tax, interest, transfer-pricing adjustments, withholding, and civil penalties, and refer deliberate conduct for prosecution under the Income Tax Act.

Director personal liability: General partners may have unlimited contractual and statutory exposure for partnership liabilities. Limited partners risk loss of limited-liability protection if they improperly participate in management under applicable partnership law or if the structure is otherwise invalid. Individuals involved in fraud may incur personal civil restitution and criminal liability.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: The CRA may make a section 247 transfer-pricing adjustment, impose a 10% transfer-pricing penalty on qualifying adjustments not reasonably documented, deny deductions, increase withholding, and assess interest. T106 failures may generate separate penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal exposure where they knowingly approve false records, participate in fraud, or fail to remit amounts for which statutory director liability applies. Ordinary commercial transfer-pricing disagreement does not by itself create criminal personal liability.
Failure to withhold or remit non-resident tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may owe the unremitted tax, interest, late-remittance penalties, information-slip penalties, and potentially a penalty for failure to deduct. Treaty relief cannot generally be applied retroactively without adequate entitlement evidence and prescribed procedures.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporation may be assessed personally for certain unremitted source deductions and GST/HST under statutory director-liability rules, subject to due diligence and limitation provisions. An LP's general partner and persons controlling payment processes may face contractual, civil, or criminal exposure for deliberate misconduct.
GST/HST and digital-services non-compliance

Statutory sanction: The Excise Tax Act may impose assessed GST/HST, interest, late-filing and late-remittance penalties, input-tax-credit denial, gross-negligence penalties, and prosecution for deliberate evasion or false records.

Director personal liability: Directors of corporate entities may be jointly and severally liable for certain unremitted GST/HST. General partners may be exposed under partnership law. Criminal liability requires the applicable statutory mental element and evidence of personal involvement or authorization.
AML, sanctions, and beneficial-ownership concealment

Statutory sanction: Applicable offences under the Criminal Code, Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, and sanctions legislation may result in asset restraint or forfeiture, administrative monetary penalties, substantial criminal fines, and imprisonment. Ordinary SaaS operations are not automatically a FINTRAC-regulated money-services business, but regulated status must be assessed based on actual payment activities.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct, authorize, facilitate, or knowingly benefit from laundering or sanctions violations may face personal prosecution, imprisonment, forfeiture, civil recovery, and director or officer disqualification consequences. Travel restrictions may arise from court orders, bail conditions, immigration consequences, or sanctions, but are not an automatic consequence of every tax or AML breach.
Corporate records and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: The entity may face provincial corporate or partnership compliance measures, federal tax reassessments, information demands, penalties, loss of evidentiary credibility, and potential prosecution where records are falsified or intentionally destroyed.

Director personal liability: The general partner, its directors, officers, and controlling persons may face personal exposure for intentional misconduct, fraudulent transfers, statutory remittance liabilities, or criminal obstruction. Limited liability is not protection for fraud, bad faith, or personal guarantees.
Artificial profit repatriation and treaty abuse

Statutory sanction: The CRA may deny treaty benefits, apply the principal-purpose or limitation-on-benefits provisions where applicable, recharacterize payments, impose withholding and transfer-pricing assessments, apply the general anti-avoidance rule, and levy penalties and interest.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly implement sham or fraudulent repatriation arrangements may face civil recovery and criminal liability. A good-faith treaty position supported by contemporaneous legal and factual analysis does not automatically create personal liability, but inaccurate certifications can materially increase exposure.
