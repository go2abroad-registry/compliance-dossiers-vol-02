Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – Business Registration Online (BRO) portal (www.canada.ca/business-registration)

E-government filing portal

A non-resident establishing an Alberta limited partnership first registers the LP with the Alberta Corporate Registry (Service Alberta) via CORPORATE ACCESS to obtain an Alberta registration number. The partnership then registers with the CRA for a nine-digit federal Business Number (BN) and the relevant program accounts: GST/HST (RT), Import/Export (IM/EX), and, if Canadian employees are engaged, Payroll (RP). Non-residents may use CRA Form RC1B (Request for Business Number – Non-Resident) or the BRO electronic portal. The BN constitutes the statutory TIN for GST/HST and income tax remittance; the partnership itself is the registered person for GST/HST purposes, while partners report their shares of income at the partner level.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box election; limited partnerships are statutorily fiscally transparent. Available elections are administrative and narrow, including (i) designation of a partner as the GST/HST representative for the partnership under the Excise Tax Act; (ii) partnership fiscal-period election under the Income Tax Act where certain criteria are met; and (iii) application for voluntary GST/HST registration before reaching the mandatory threshold. Non-resident partners may also elect, where applicable, to file under alternative return mechanisms for Canadian-source business income rather than remaining subject to withholding.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

0.05%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

$30,000 CAD of worldwide taxable supplies, measured on the same basis as the CRA threshold; once a supplier exceeds $30,000 in taxable supplies in any 12-month period, mandatory GST/HST registration applies unless the supplier is a small supplier filing no election. Non-residents carrying on business in Canada are subject to the same threshold and may also voluntarily register.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of goods qualify for 0% GST where the goods are sold and exported by the supplier in accordance with the Excise Tax Act (typically sections 24 and 25 applicable to direct and indirect exports, including shipment to a non-resident buyer outside Canada). Supplies of services and intangible personal property (including AI video-generation software and SaaS) made to a non-resident customer are zero-rated under section 7(?) of Part V of Schedule VI, provided the supply is not a service performed in connection with real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance, and is not made to an individual resident in Canada. For digital supplies delivered electronically to a non-resident customer, the place of supply rules generally assign the supply outside Canada, supporting zero-rating.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form A8/A8A, automated G7, or Canada Export Declaration Summary Report)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing export from Canada to the non-resident destination
  • Commercial Invoice and sales contract identifying the non-resident buyer and the export terms
  • Carrier certificate or forwarding agent evidence confirming physical export
  • Payment and inward remittance evidence (bank receipts, SWIFT/credit card settlement records, Paypal/Stripe/Paddle transaction logs, or an Inward Remittance Certificate where the payment trail requires banking confirmation)

No real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control (CTC), e-invoicing pre-authorization, or digital stamping mandate exists federally or in Alberta. CRA accepts electronic invoices where books and records requirements are met. All GST/HST registrants must file returns electronically using GST/HST NETFILE or My Business Account unless a CRA-approved exemption applies; however, issuing invoices does not require API clearance or CRA stamping. Records must be kept in a readable digital format and be accessible to CRA on audit.

CRA may reassess within normal four-year audit limitation periods (or longer if misrepresentation is established) to disallow zero-rating, recover GST at 5% plus compound interest, and claw back refunds or input tax credits. Fraudulent or careless export claims attract penalties under the Excise Tax Act, including a false-statement penalty under s.285.1 (not less than $100 and up to 25% of the net tax sought to be evaded), failure-to-file penalties, and, for repeated or deliberate evasion, criminal prosecution under s.327 with fines up to 200% of evaded tax and imprisonment. The burden is on the supplier to maintain contemporaneous export and foreign-payment documentation.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital account; Canada observes Article VIII of the IMF Articles of Agreement and maintains no exchange control legislation restricting cross-border transfers, escrow funding, or profit repatriation. A non-resident limited partner's share of Alberta LP business income is taxable under Part I of the Income Tax Act (federal) and the Alberta Income Tax Act; distributions of business income are not subject to Part XIII withholding, whereas interest, management fees, rents, and royalties paid to non-residents attract Part XIII withholding of 25%, subject to tax-treaty relief under the Canada-US Treaty or other applicable DTAs. No statutory approvals are required for profit remittance, and there is no minimum-stay or holding-period obligation on CAD funds of non-residents.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline, mandatory conversion ratio, or surrender obligation on export receipts; software and SaaS license proceeds may be retained indefinitely in foreign-currency bank accounts or converted at arm's-length market rates. The only applicable reporting obligations are FINTRAC currency-transaction reports for cash receipts exceeding CAD 10,000 and CRA information-return thresholds on foreign holdings; neither requires mandatory conversion or surrender of export proceeds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), acting with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential supervision of Schedule I banks and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for anti-money-laundering surveillance; Canada maintains no separate foreign-exchange control administration.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium feasibility with material legal constraints. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer) operate in Canada only as FINTRAC-registered money services businesses and may offer multi-currency payment accounts and virtual IBANs, but they are not chartered banks: balances are uninsured e-money rather than CDIC-protected deposits, they cannot hold statutory capital escrow, and their ability to open a true onshore corporate account for an Alberta LP is limited to settlement and collection rails. Such platforms are recommended for operational receivables (Stripe and Paddle settlement aggregation) and small-value cross-border disbursements, but not as the primary treasury or escrow repository.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital contributions from non-resident partners are routed by SWIFT MT103 or ISO 20022 wire from the partner's home banking institution, using the LP's legal name in the beneficiary line, the full Alberta registered address, and the Canadian 9-digit transit and account number. The purpose-of-payment field should cite ISO 20022 purpose code 'SUBS' (subscription) for partnership-unit subscriptions or 'INTC' (intercompany) for affiliated holding-vehicle contributions, augmented by the narrative 'Capital Contribution to Canadian Limited Partnership'. Canada imposes no pre-approval, registration, or FX conversion requirement on such flows. Verification applied at onboarding and on each receipt includes the executed capital call, source-of-funds evidence, and the bank's sanctions screening (OFAC and CBSA criteria); funds must settle into a CDIC-insured Schedule I bank account and, where the treasury policy requires, be segregated between the Capital Escrow Account and the Operational Account.Outbound settlements and operating disbursements will be cleared through (i) SWIFT MT103 for international vendor, contractor, and API-infrastructure payments (e.g., cloud GPU providers); (ii) the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) and ACSS electronic funds transfer for domestic CAD payments; (iii) Fedwire and CHIPS via the Canadian bank's US correspondent for USD payments; and (iv) commercial letters of credit (UCP 600) for material equipment or licensing procurements exceeding CAD 100,000. Reinstatement requires formal application and payment of restoration fees, while the LP is not in good standing and partners may face loss of limited-liability protection for obligations arising after dissolution.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Canada: 15% federal general corporate income tax rate. Alberta: 8% general provincial corporate income tax rate. Combined general rate: 23%. Eligible Canadian-controlled private corporation active business income may generally qualify for a 9% federal rate and a 2% Alberta rate, producing an 11% combined rate, subject to statutory limits and eligibility conditions.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Canada's statutory Part XIII withholding tax rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is 25%. Under an applicable income tax treaty, the rate is commonly reduced to 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting the ownership threshold, and to 15% for other qualifying corporate or individual portfolio holdings. The Canada-United States treaty commonly provides 5% for qualifying direct corporate ownership and 15% for other dividends, subject to treaty conditions. A limited partnership distribution is not automatically a dividend; classification depends on the legal character of the payment and the payer.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For Canadian non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents, T106 reporting is generally triggered when the applicable aggregate transaction category exceeds CAD 1 million. Canadian contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is required under section 247 of the Income Tax Act when non-arm's-length cross-border transactions occur; there is no general safe-harbour threshold below which the documentation obligation automatically disappears. A Country-by-Country report and master-file/local-file documentation generally apply to constituent entities of multinational enterprise groups meeting the consolidated revenue threshold of EUR 750 million, subject to Canadian implementation rules and filing deadlines.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a single standalone Economic Substance Regulation regime equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is assessed through the Income Tax Act, treaty anti-abuse rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, beneficial-ownership principles, specific anti-avoidance provisions, transfer-pricing rules, and common-law principles. A Canadian LP should maintain genuine commercial activity, decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational capacity, books and records, control over IP and risks, bank accounts, contracts, and evidence that allocations reflect functions, assets, and risks. The principal-purpose test in applicable treaties may deny benefits where obtaining the benefit was one of the principal purposes of an arrangement, subject to treaty wording.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Assessment of unpaid tax, interest, civil penalties including gross-negligence penalties that can reach 50% of the understated tax or overstated credits, and potential prosecution. Tax evasion convictions may result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to 5 years under the Income Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible officers may face criminal liability where they authorized, participated in, or acquiesced in the conduct. Corporate status does not protect intentional personal wrongdoing. Directors may also face derivative civil claims and reassessment of personal benefits. Travel bans are not a routine tax penalty but may arise from criminal bail, court orders, immigration consequences, or enforcement proceedings.
Failure to Withhold or Remit Part XIII Tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the unremitted amount, arrears interest, and applicable failure-to-withhold penalties. Directors may be personally liable for unremitted source deductions under the director-liability provisions where statutory conditions are met.

Director personal liability: Directors may face joint and several liability for unremitted amounts, interest, and penalties after the CRA's statutory collection conditions are satisfied. Criminal liability may arise for deliberate obstruction or fraudulent withholding conduct.
Transfer-Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust taxable income under section 247, deny unsupported deductions, impose withholding consequences, and apply a penalty generally equal to 10% of the transfer-pricing adjustment where the statutory conditions for the penalty are met. Recharacterization, interest, and foreign-tax mismatch may result.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability for source deductions and may be exposed to civil claims for knowing misconduct or breach of fiduciary duties. Deliberate false documentation can support criminal tax charges.
Shell Company and Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: Treaty benefits may be denied under the principal-purpose test, limitation-on-benefits provisions, beneficial-ownership rules, or the Canadian general anti-avoidance rule. The CRA may reassess tax, withholding, interest, penalties, and reportable or notifiable transaction consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may incur civil liability for losses caused by unlawful distributions or misrepresentations. Criminal exposure may arise if the structure is used to commit tax fraud, money laundering, or document fraud.
AML, Proceeds of Crime, and Sanctions

Statutory sanction: Potential reporting, registration, record-keeping, and suspicious-transaction obligations may arise under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and related legislation. Violations can result in administrative monetary penalties, seizure, forfeiture, regulatory enforcement, and criminal fines or imprisonment. Sanctions violations may trigger substantial penalties under Canadian sanctions legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, and officers may be prosecuted for knowing participation, facilitation, or wilful blindness. Courts may impose probation, forfeiture, and imprisonment. Travel restrictions may arise from criminal process or court orders, but are not automatic for every AML breach.
Improper Partnership Allocation and Distribution

Statutory sanction: CRA reassessment, denial of allocations, partner-level tax, withholding tax, interest, penalties, and possible application of partnership anti-avoidance or recharacterization rules. Alberta corporate and partnership law may impose additional remedies for unlawful distributions.

Director personal liability: A limited partner is generally protected from partnership debts if it does not participate in management beyond permitted limits. The general partner and persons exercising management control may face personal liability for partnership obligations, fraudulent conduct, breach of fiduciary duty, and unlawful distributions.
Corporate Records, Beneficial Ownership, and Filing Failure

Statutory sanction: Administrative penalties, loss of good standing, inability to obtain certificates, CRA reassessments, information demands, and prosecution for obstruction or false records may result.

Director personal liability: The general partner, directors of corporate partners, and responsible officers may face personal penalties or criminal liability for false filings, obstruction, or failure to remit source deductions. A corporate veil is not a defence to fraud, bad faith, deliberate torts, or statutory director-liability provisions.
