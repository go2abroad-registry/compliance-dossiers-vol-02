Canada Revenue Agency (CRA). Primary portals are Business Registration Online (BRO), My Business Account, Represent a Client, and GST/HST NETFILE. Alberta entity formation and partnership registry matters are administered separately by Alberta Corporate Registry under Service Alberta and Red Tape Reduction; Alberta has no general provincial sales tax.

E-government filing portal

A BN is the principal Canadian business identifier; an LP does not automatically receive a federal income-tax TIN merely because it is formed under Alberta law. 3. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a general US-style check-the-box election. A valid Alberta limited partnership is ordinarily fiscally transparent for Canadian income-tax purposes, subject to partnership characterization, allocation, anti-avoidance, and non-resident-partner rules. Income and loss are generally allocated to partners rather than taxed at the partnership level, although the partnership may have filing, withholding, information-reporting, and tax-computation obligations. Relevant elections may include a subsection 97(2) tax-deferred rollover for qualifying transfers of property to a Canadian partnership, subsection 85 elections where the statutory conditions are met, foreign-affiliate and treaty-related elections at partner level, and elections concerning GST/HST methods or reporting periods. These are not entity-classification elections. Canada has mandatory hybrid-mismatch rules and anti-hybrid denial provisions; there is no elective hybrid-mismatch regime that converts an LP into a corporation or generally overrides Canadian partnership characterization.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies over the applicable threshold period, generally measured by the person’s total taxable supplies, including zero-rated supplies, subject to the small-supplier rules and associated-person aggregation. The threshold is generally tested over a single calendar quarter or the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters. A non-resident carrying on business in Canada may be required to register even where the ordinary small-supplier threshold analysis does not eliminate the obligation. Voluntary registration is permitted in many cases and normally creates collection, return, recordkeeping, and input-tax-credit obligations.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods may generally be zero-rated where they are supplied for export and the statutory export conditions are satisfied, including evidence that the goods were exported from Canada. Services supplied to a non-resident may be zero-rated where the service falls within the exported-service provisions and is not excluded by a specific statutory exception. For data-pipeline, web-scraping, and proxy-API services, treatment depends on the contractual recipient, recipient status, place of supply, actual use or consumption, and whether the service is performed in respect of Canadian real property, tangible personal property, or another excluded matter. A non-resident customer alone is not conclusive. Canadian use, a Canadian establishment of the customer, intermediary arrangements, or an excluded service can prevent zero-rating. Supplies to Canadian customers generally attract 5% GST where the LP is registered. A registrant may generally claim input tax credits for eligible GST incurred in commercial activities and may receive a refund or net refund where eligible zero-rated exports create excess credits, subject to documentary substantiation and filing compliance.

  • Export declaration or other CBSA export evidence, where goods are exported and an export declaration is legally required
  • Commercial invoice, purchase order, contract, and delivery or fulfilment records identifying the customer, destination, consideration, and goods
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, courier record, or equivalent carrier evidence, where applicable to exported goods
  • Proof of delivery outside Canada and customs import evidence in the destination jurisdiction, where available
  • For offshore API or digital services: customer contract, customer legal-status and address evidence, service-usage or access records, server or delivery records, payment records, and evidence supporting non-resident status and non-Canadian use
  • Banking or payment records supporting receipt of consideration; an inward-remittance certificate is not generally a Canadian statutory GST document
  • GST/HST invoices, general-ledger records, input-tax-credit invoices, and reconciliation schedules linking zero-rated revenue to the relevant contracts and transactions

Canada has no general federal real-time GST invoice-clearance, government e-invoicing, or mandatory digital tax-stamping system for ordinary commercial supplies. GST registrants must issue compliant documentary evidence when required, retain records, and show prescribed information such as supplier identity and BN/GST account number, invoice date, recipient information where required, description, consideration, and GST/HST amount or rate. Electronic invoices and electronic records are generally acceptable if they preserve authenticity, integrity, accessibility, and audit readability. CRA filings may be made electronically through GST/HST NETFILE, My Business Account, or other authorized channels. Sector-specific electronic procurement requirements, payment-platform reporting, customs systems, and foreign customer mandates may apply independently. The LP should retain API contracts, usage logs, billing records, customer-location evidence, payment records, and service-delivery records for its web-scraping and proxy-API supplies.

Canada generally imposes no mandatory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds. CRA may reassess GST/HST where zero-rating is unsupported, the customer is not a qualifying non-resident, the service is consumed in Canada, an exclusion applies, or records are inadequate. The reassessment may impose GST/HST on the supply, deny or reduce input tax credits, require repayment of excess refunds, and add arrears interest. Misrepresentations or false statements may attract statutory penalties, including gross-negligence penalties generally equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, where the applicable statutory conditions are met. Failure-to-file, failure-to-remit, repeated-failure, and documentary penalties may also apply. Fraudulent conduct may lead to prosecution under the Excise Tax Act, Income Tax Act, or Criminal Code. Export documentation fraud can additionally trigger Canada Border Services Agency enforcement, assessment, seizure, administrative monetary penalties, and customs-related penalties. The LP should preserve relevant records for the statutory retention period, commonly six years from the end of the relevant year, subject to longer periods where required by an objection, appeal, reassessment, or CRA direction.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible under IMF Article VIII for both current and capital account transactions. There are no general exchange controls, no prior approval requirements for foreign capital inflows or outflows, and no restrictions on profit or dividend repatriation. Legal entities may maintain foreign-currency bank accounts in Canada and remit dividends, partnership distributions, or branch profits freely, subject only to Canadian corporate and non-resident withholding tax obligations and applicable anti-money-laundering reporting. The practical hurdles are not convertibility or exchange-control restrictions but rather bank compliance under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) for all cross-border wires and source-of-funds verification.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory export-proceeds surrender rules in Canada. Exporters are not required to convert foreign-currency receipts into Canadian dollars, nor are they required to remit or repatriate such proceeds within any prescribed timeline. Any conversion of export earnings is voluntary and governed by corporate treasury policy, commercial banking agreements, and ordinary foreign-exchange market practice.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); in Canada there is no dedicated foreign-exchange control agency, and the Department of Finance Canada retains policy oversight for exchange arrangements and sanctions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low-to-Medium viability as a standalone treasury rail. An unregulated offshore electronic-money institution (EMI) cannot operate as a Canadian deposit-taking bank or provide Canadian clearing accounts. At best, a foreign EMI may act as a payment-services intermediary if structured through a FINTRAC-registered Money Services Business, but it cannot hold funds in a manner equivalent to a Schedule I bank account. For a cross-border capital injection and trade settlement program, the recommended path is a Canadian Schedule I bank account with ancillary use of licensed payment-fintech and virtual-asset providers for specific collections or disbursements.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Capital contributions from foreign investors should be routed using SWIFT MT103 / MT202 messages through the foreign investor's bank to the Alberta LP's Schedule I bank account. The wire must show the exact legal name of the LP as beneficiary, the beneficiary bank's SWIFT/BIC, the LP account number, and a clear capital-investment purpose code such as 'equity participation' or 'capital contribution' consistent with Bank of Canada balance-of-payments reporting practices. For conventional wires exceeding CAD 10,000, the receiving bank will file an Electronic Funds Transfer report and may require a completed FINTRAC-required source-of-funds questionnaire. If funds are held temporarily in USDC or other stablecoin, they should be converted to fiat through a licensed Canadian virtual-asset dealer or a regulated foreign exchange provider and then wired into the LP's bank account; direct stablecoin settlement into a bank account is not a permitted clearing rail in Canada.Settlement of trade and operating disbursements should use SWIFT MT103 for individual cross-border payments, MT202 COV for correspondent-cover payments where the originating bank is different from the beneficiary bank, and commercial letters of credit under UCP 600 with bank-issued SWIFT MT700/MT710 through the bank's trade-finance desk. For Canadian-dollar high-value clearing, the Payments Canada Lynx rail provides finality; for foreign-currency settlements, the LP must rely on the Schedule I bank's correspondent banking network. Low-value CAD disbursements can use Payments Canada Automated Clearing Settlement System or wire-like EFT rails, subject to available banking products. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined general corporate rate (Federal Part I 15% + Alberta 8%). The Alberta Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent; the rate applies to partners on allocated income, not to the LP itself.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax (ITA s.212(2)) applies to dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation. LP profit distributions are not dividends: a non-resident partner's share of LP Canadian-source business income is taxed under Part I through the T5013 allocation, and repatriation of previously taxed profits is generally not subject to further Part XIII withholding.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: T106 Information Return required where the aggregate value of reportable non-arm's-length cross-border transactions exceeds C$1,000,000 in the fiscal period; contemporaneous Local File documentation is expected for intercompany transactions above C$1,000,000 (CRA TPM-09); Country-by-Country Report (Form RC4648) and Master File are required where the MNE group's consolidated revenue is C$750,000,000 or more; penalties under ITA s.247(3) apply for adjustments without reasonable efforts, plus s.247(7) documentation penalties for failure to provide requested records.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations statute for LPs, but substance is enforced through multiple doctrines: the arm's-length requirement of ITA s.247; the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s.245); the treaty Limitation-on-Benefits provisions; and the 'Canadian partnership' requirements of ITA s.96. The CRA will recharacterize a conduit or shell LP if there is no real commercial activity. The enterprise must maintain an Alberta office, employ technical and management staff, own or license actual software and API infrastructure, execute customer and vendor contracts, and operate bank accounts in its own name.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: On indictment under ITA s.239(1): fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded plus imprisonment for up to 5 years; on summary conviction: fine plus imprisonment for up to 2 years. Gross negligence under ITA s.163(2) carries a 50% penalty on the understated tax, plus prescribed interest compounded daily.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers of the corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable under ITA s.227.1 for unremitted source deductions and Part XIII amounts; CRA may issue a s.160 assessment against assets transferred to non-arm's-length persons; courts will pierce the limited partnership veil where fraud or sham is proven; criminal conviction triggers immediate removal from directorship and court-ordered travel restrictions pending sentencing.
Transfer Pricing and Documentation Failures

Statutory sanction: CRA may reallocate income under ITA s.247(2) at full arm's-length pricing; penalty under s.247(3) of up to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where no reasonable efforts were made; failure to provide records under s.247(7) triggers monthly penalties; double taxation and Part XIII recharacterization of interest or royalties may follow.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner face personal assessments for unremitted Part XIII tax on mispriced cross-border payments; s.160 clawbacks may attach personal assets; deliberate non-compliance converts the civil adjustment into a tax evasion prosecution, with imprisonment exposure of up to 5 years.
Shell Company, Conduit, and GAAR Abuse

Statutory sanction: GAAR (ITA s.245) denies the tax benefit and imposes a 25% penalty on the benefit (ITA s.163(7)); treaty benefits are denied under Limitation-on-Benefits; recharacterization as a deemed corporation under Part I could subject worldwide income attribution to Canadian tax; the Alberta Registrar may strike the LP for non-compliance; interest and compounded penalties apply to all reassessments.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorized conduit transactions are liable for civil restitution to the CRA up to the tax benefit conferred; corporate veil may be pierced for fraudulent conveyances; personal bankruptcy proceedings may be initiated for unpaid GAAR assessments; travel bans are available where large assessments remain outstanding and asset dissipation is imminent.
AML / FINTRAC Non-Compliance (Virtual Currency Payments)

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties up to C$1,000,000 for a first violation and up to C$2,000,000 for a subsequent violation; suspension or revocation of MSB registration; stop orders against processing virtual currency payments; reputational debarment from banking and Stripe payment partners.

Director personal liability: Officers and directors who directed, authorized, or acquiesced in the violation are personally liable to FINTRAC penalties; individuals may be subject to fines up to C$500,000 per violation; knowingly laundering the proceeds of cybercrime through the payment rails triggers the Criminal Code offence of laundering proceeds of crime, carrying imprisonment of up to 10 years and mandatory asset forfeiture.
