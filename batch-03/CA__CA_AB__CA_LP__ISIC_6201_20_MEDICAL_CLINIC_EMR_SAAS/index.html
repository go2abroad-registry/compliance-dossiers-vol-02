Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC), GST/HST portal: My Business Account (CRA BizPort), GST/HST Web Services API, and My Payment Service; provincial counterpart for Alberta: Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration (ATRA) for specific provincial taxes, although GST/HST is administered federally.

E-government filing portal

For a non-resident or foreign-owned Alberta Limited Partnership, the Canadian tax identifier framework requires the partnership itself to obtain a Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) business number (BN) as a 'Business Account' if it has a GST/HST registration obligation or payroll account. However, because a limited partnership is a pass-through entity, the CRA generally attributes partnership income to its partners; a foreign entity with no Canadian permanent establishment must nevertheless consider whether the partnership is carrying on business in Canada and whether the partnership must register for GST/HST under the Excise Tax Act (ETA). Procedure: (i) submit Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number (BN) and Certain Program Accounts' or use CRA's online 'Register a business account' service, providing partnership name, Alberta registration number, business office address in Alberta, and names of general partners; (ii) provide non-resident partner disclosure and CRA Form RC460 'GST/HST and QST Registration (Non-Resident)' where required; (iii) obtain a separate GST/HST program account (RT) if the partnership's taxable supplies in Canada exceed $30,000 CAD in any single calendar quarter or immediately upon first taxable sale in Canada (head-in-the-sand rule), or voluntarily if engaged in commercial activity and seeking input tax credits (ITCs); (iv) register for Alberta corporate income tax (Albertan provincial corporate tax) only at the partner level if any partner is a corporation; the Alberta LP itself is not a taxpayer for Alberta corporate income tax unless it is a 'designated limited partnership' with a corporate general partner—in which case the ATRA requires a partnership registration number and annual Alberta Corporate Tax Return (AT1) for the deemed corporate partner; and (v) where the non-resident LP has no Canadian-resident general partner, retain registered agent for service in Alberta and file Alberta Partnership Return (Form ATK7630) for reporting if required.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Under the Income Tax Act (Canada), an Alberta Limited Partnership is by default a fiscally transparent partnership (not a taxpayer at the entity level); CRA administers a mandatory accrual system, not an elective check-the-box regime. Special elections: (i) Partnership fiscal period non-calendar-year election under subsection 249.1(4) and section 96(1.11), allowing a year-end other than December 31 if all partners are U.S. or foreign entities with tax years ending on a common date; (ii) election under subsection 97(2) for property contributed to the partnership to be disposed of at agreed amount; (iii) election under subsection 99(2) on partnership dissolution; (iv) joint election under subsection 152(4) to waive reassessment or carry-back; (v) election under subsection 216(1) for non-residents earning Canadian rental income to file a Canadian return on net basis; (vi) hybrid mismatch elections limited under OECD BEPS Action 2 as implemented through subsection 18.4(3) and section 18.2 (anti-hybrid rules), which may disallow deductions on hybrid mismatches (e.g., where U.S. LLC treated as corporation for U.S. purposes and branch for Canadian purposes); no 'check-the-box' is available under Canadian law. A limited partnership that is a 'Canadian partnership' under section 102 may be designated as a 'business investment company' for certain loss restriction rules, but not as a tax election.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

An Alberta LP supplying EMR SaaS to non-resident clients can zero-rate the supply under the Excise Tax Act if the supply is (i) a 'service' rendered to a non-resident person (other than a registrant) and is not supplied to an individual who is in Canada at the time the service is performed, or is not in connection with real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada that is not a supply of an exported service; under paragraph 7(a), Schedule VI, a supply of a service to a non-resident is zero-rated unless the service is 'in connection with real property situated in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time the service is rendered, or a service rendered to an individual while the individual is in Canada'. EMR SaaS is generally a digital service supplied remotely; thus, zero-rating applies if the recipient is non-resident and the service is not performed in Canada in connection with Canadian property. The supply must also be 'made outside Canada' or an 'exported service' under ETA section 144. Additionally, if the non-resident recipient is registered for GST/HST, the supply can be zero-rated under paragraph 7(b) if the service is for consumption exclusively outside Canada. Software licenses supplied by way of download or electronic transmission to a non-resident may be zero-rated as 'intangible personal property' supplied to a non-resident under Part V, Schedule VI. If the supply is made to a Canadian-resident medical clinic, the 5% GST applies; however, qualifying cloud-based EMR services may be exempt if supplied to a 'hospital authority', 'public institution', or 'prescribed entity' under section 21, Part II, Schedule V if the recipient certifies the software is for use in a health care facility and is of a prescribed kind (e.g., EMR software systems certified under provincial health information standards in Alberta). For zero-rating, the supplier must obtain documentary evidence of the recipient's non-resident status (e.g., foreign business registration, passport, or a signed declaration Form GST 224 'Non-Resident Status Declaration'), and the service must not be delivered to a Canadian address or used in Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA B13A / G13 Aggregate Export Declaration, or Canada Border Services Agency proof of export)
  • Commercial invoice or supply contract stating the service is supplied to a non-resident
  • Non-Resident Status Declaration (CRA Form GST 224) or equivalent documentary evidence (foreign business registration, director's declaration, certificate of non-residence)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank SWIFT/MT103, wire transfer advice, or payment records from Stripe Billing / Bank Transfer evidencing receipt in Canadian bank account or credited to the partnership)
  • Software delivery logs or IP geolocation records verifying remote access by non-resident users
  • GST/HST return and Schedule showing zero-rated supplies (Line 103 if electronically filed)

Canada does not operate a mandatory real-time electronic invoicing clearance platform comparable to Italy's Sistema di Interscambio or Brazil's NF-e. CRA mandates that all registered suppliers issue and retain valid GST/HST invoices or simplified receipts (for supplies under $30 CAD); invoices may be issued electronically in EDI, XML, PDF, or other machine-readable formats. Since 2022, CRA accelerated audit use of GST/HST electronic data and requires prescribed invoice information to support ITCs. There is no digital stamping, but CRA has published 'Electronic Payment and Reporting Standards' and GST/HST Web Services API specifications allowing filers to submit returns via EDI, the GST/HST NETFILE (XML), or the CRA My Business Account portal. Alberta has no provincial sales tax (Alberta does not participate in HST); therefore only the federal 5% GST applies on qualifying supplies made in Alberta. Medical clinic EMR/SaaS services generally constitute 'software as a service' taxable at 5% unless the supply is made to a non-resident person who is not registered for GST/HST and not receiving the supply in Canada, or the supply is an 'exported service' that qualifies for zero-rating under section 9, Schedule VI to the ETA. Invoices must clearly show supplier BN, date, consideration, tax rate, and GST amount; foreign suppliers making cross-border digital supplies to Canadian consumers may be subject to the simplified GST/HST registration regime for non-resident suppliers effective July 1, 2021, with a $30,000 CAD threshold, requiring annual returns and no ITCs (final consumer supplies).

CRA may reassess and deny zero-rating where the non-resident status or consumption-outside-Canada condition is not substantiated. Penalties and clawbacks: (i) uncollected GST/HST is assessed under ETA section 298, with interest compounded daily at prescribed rates (currently average prescribed rate plus 4% for large corporations; for non-corporations 4% plus regular rate); (ii) 6% penalty on any net tax remittance deficiency mitigated by defense; (iii) 10% penalty for knowingly making a false statement or omission under ETA subsection 285.1(1); (iv) if the exporter or supplier knowingly misrepresents the export, fines up to the greater of $5,000 CAD and 50% of the tax sought to be evaded, plus up to 5 years imprisonment under ETA section 328 and Criminal Code fraud provisions; (v) CRA can claw back ITCs on exported supplies and apply Section 296 (waiver of interest/penalties only in exceptional circumstances); (vi) for cross-border digital services, if the non-resident is later found to be in Canada or the service is used in Canada, the LP is liable for unremitted GST/HST including penalties under subsection 280(1) (failure to register), 1% of gross revenue for failure to file, and 5% plus 1% per month for late filing up to 12 months. Additionally, the Excise Tax Act contains anti-avoidance rules (section 274) that can recharacterize transactions to prevent 'export zero-rating' abuse, and the CRA's GST/HST Memory Aid requires exporters to retain evidence for six years; failure to maintain adequate export records triggers 10% penalty (minimum $100 CAD) under subsection 285(1). Provincial clawbacks: Alberta does not impose provincial sales tax, so no provincial clawback except for corporate income tax adjustments if the LP's income from exports is reallocated to Alberta under treaty/PE rules.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely convertible under both capital and current account transactions. Canada has no general restrictions on currency conversion, dividend remittances, or capital repatriation for residents or non-residents. Profit remittances are permitted without prior government approval, but cross-border wire transfers and large cash transactions are subject to FINTRAC reporting and Canadian sanctions screening. Non-resident partners may repatriate earned income or distributions, subject to Canadian tax withholding, and banks may require a completed NR6 or waiver for specific withholding tax arrangements. No exchange control approval is required for investment inflows or outflows.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no mandatory surrender or conversion requirements for export proceeds. Exporters may hold foreign currency in Canadian bank accounts or offshore accounts without any statutory timeline for repatriation. There is no mandatory conversion ratio. However, Canadian residents must file Form T1135 (Foreign Income Verification Statement) if the aggregate cost of specified foreign property exceeds CAD 100,000, which includes foreign currency held in foreign accounts. For the purpose of the EMR SaaS entity, subscription revenue received from US or foreign customers may be retained in USD or converted to CAD at the entity's discretion.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); the Department of Finance Canada oversees exchange control policy, but there is no dedicated foreign exchange administration.

Airwallex Fit: High

Moderate feasibility. Foreign fintech entities (e.g., Airwallex, Wise, Payoneer) are not Canadian chartered banks and cannot take deposits unless registered as MSBs and authorized to operate in Canada. They may be used as auxiliary payment service providers (PSPs) for inbound subscription collection and outbound disbursement, but core treasury liquidity and capital escrow should reside in a Schedule I Canadian bank. Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Capital injections from non-resident partners must be sent via SWIFT MT103 to the LP's Canadian bank account, using a clear beneficiary name and the specific purpose of payment: 'Capital Contribution to Limited Partnership' or 'Equity Investment for Software Operations'. For wires exceeding CAD 10,000, banks may require pre-verification of the relationship and source of funds. Routing should be from the partner's own bank account; never accept triangular payments from third parties. If the contribution is paid in USD, a USD-denominated account at the Canadian bank is advisable. All capital injections should be documented with a subscription agreement and director approval (if applicable). Ensure the bank's funds received via large-value transfer system (LVTS) or SWIFT are not delayed by providing prompt responses to AML inquiries and submitting a copy of the executed partnership agreement and KYC pack in advance.For operating disbursements: use SWIFT MT103 for international wire payments and Canadian EFT (via Payments Canada) for domestic CAD payments. For recurring SaaS vendor payments (e.g., cloud hosting, development services), commercial letters of credit are generally not required; SWIFT MT103 or ACH/SEPA/EFT rail is sufficient. For settlement with suppliers in the US, a USD account at a Schedule I bank with US correspondent clearing (e.g., via BMO's US network or RBC's RBC Bank) allows efficient USD-to-USD transfers without conversion. For tax-related payments to the CRA, use the standard Canadian banking web or payroll remittance file. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Moderate feasibility. Foreign fintech entities (e.g., Airwallex, Wise, Payoneer) are not Canadian chartered banks and cannot take deposits unless registered as MSBs and authorized to operate in Canada. They may be used as auxiliary payment service providers (PSPs) for inbound subscription collection and outbound disbursement, but core treasury liquidity and capital escrow should reside in a Schedule I Canadian bank. Alberta requires MSB registration with FINTRAC for money services businesses; the entity itself does not need to be a fintech. Cross-border payment providers offering electronic money services are permitted, but due diligence and sanctions screening remain the responsibility of the Canadian entity. For a low-risk SaaS business, pairing a Schedule I bank with a fintech provider for customer-facing card payments is optimal.

Stage 06

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined federal (15%) and Alberta provincial (8%) general corporate rate; a Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent and pays no entity-level corporate income tax, so the rate is borne by its partners at their applicable corporate or personal marginal rates.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian corporation to non-resident shareholders. A Limited Partnership does not pay dividends; profit distributions to partners are return of capital and arise under the partnership agreement, and are not subject to Part XIII dividend withholding.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Within five months after the partnership fiscal year-end (for a calendar-year LP, by May 31). Partner T5013 slips must be furnished by March 31 (or two months after a differentiated fiscal year-end).

Deadline: · Frequency:

Six months after the corporate partner's fiscal year-end (e.g., June 30 for December 31 year-end).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate annual value of reportable non-arm's length cross-border transactions triggers mandatory Form T106 filing for Canadian tax purposes; contemporaneous Local File and Master File documentation is required for all reportable transactions, and CRA may request it regardless of size. A CAD 750,000,000 global consolidated revenue threshold triggers CBCR filing, which is not applicable to most EMR/HealthTech LPs.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain separate Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) comparable to UAE/offshore centres; however, the GAAR, the substance-over-form doctrine, and treaty anti-abuse provisions (including the principal purpose test for treaty benefits) require genuine entrepreneurial risk and functional activity. The Alberta LP must be Canadian-established, maintain substantive management, and have an economic nexus in Alberta; a pass-through LP that merely routes EMR/clinical software licensing royalties through a non-resident partner without employees, contracts, or decision-making autonomy will be attacked by the CRA.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Willful Understatement

Statutory sanction: Gross negligence penalty equal to 50% of the understated tax (ITA s. 163(2)); criminal conviction for tax evasion can impose a fine of 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment of up to 5 years. CRA may also order disgorgement and compound interest at the prescribed daily rate.

Director personal liability: Directors and controlling general partners are jointly and severally liable for unremitted wage withholdings, GST, and section 227.1 amounts; knowingly signing false T5013/T1/T2 returns can lead to civil restitution, director penalty orders, and a criminal record that blocks cross-border travel and banking in the US.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply GAAR to ignore the LP structure or re-characterize the non-resident partner's income as Canadian-source; a 25% GAAR penalty on the tax benefit under ITA s. 245, plus 40-100% penalty for intentionally misrepresenting facts. The LP can be assessed for unpaid Canadian corporate tax as if it were a corporation.

Director personal liability: Courts may pierce the limited liability shield under the Alberta Business Corporations Act where the LP is an unregistered sham; directors face assessments for partnership debts, equitable restitution to the CRA, and a Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) travel prohibition request pending outstanding tax judgments.
AML / FINTRAC Compliance Failure

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalty under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) of up to CAD 1 million per violation (or double the benefit for repeated violations); criminal prosecution can add imprisonment and seizure of the LP's bank accounts. Failing to file Large Cash Transaction and Suspicious Transaction Reports separately triggers per-instance penalties.

Director personal liability: A responsible individual (director, general partner, or compliance officer) can be fined up to CAD 500,000 and imprisoned for up to 5 years; FINTRAC may also impose personal prohibition orders preventing the individual from operating any licensed money services or healthcare-adjacent payment platform.
Transfer Pricing / Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Under ITA s. 247(4), a penalty equal to 10% of the transfer-pricing adjustment (with no downside offset) is assessed if the taxpayer fails to make or obtain reasonable arm's length documentation; CRA also disallows corresponding deductions and adds daily accrued interest. T106 non-filing attracts CAD 100 per day per month.

Director personal liability: Directors who approved the mispricing are jointly liable for the unpaid assessment where the LP is insolvent; CRA may seek a court order recovering the penalty from the director's personal assets, and the director may be barred from exiting Canada pending payment under the Tax Court of Canada's urgent-asset freeze.
