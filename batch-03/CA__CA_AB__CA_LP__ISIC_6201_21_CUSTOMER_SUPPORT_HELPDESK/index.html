Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) with business registration via the Business Registration Online (BRO) portal and My Business Account (MNB); Alberta corporate income tax is administered federally by CRA under the Alberta Corporate Tax Act collection agreement; provincial registration of the Limited Partnership is handled by the Alberta Corporate Registry under the Partnership Act.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity obtains a federal Business Number (BN) through CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) or paper Form RC1. The Limited Partnership must appoint a general partner or authorized representative and file Form RC59C or RC59 with the CRA. Procedure: (1) apply for the BN as a partnership adding the GST/HST account (RT prefix) if taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 or if voluntary registration is elected; (2) add an RP payroll account if Canadian employees are engaged; (3) register the LP with the Alberta Corporate Registry under the Partnership Act (Alberta LLP/LP registration form and fee); (4) file the T5013 partnership information return annually (LP itself is not a taxpayer; income flows to partners); (5) non-resident partners must supply their tax identification data or T2042 documentation and remain subject to relevant withholding rules under Part XIII or Part I of the Income Tax Act.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — an Alberta Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent under the Income Tax Act; no entity-level federal or Alberta corporate income tax is imposed on the partnership itself; each partner reports their allocable share of income and loss under ITA section 96. For GST/HST purposes, however, the partnership is treated as a separate person and is the remitter of GST/HST on taxable supplies. Territorial scope: Canadian-resident partners are subject to taxation on their worldwide partnership income; non-resident partners are subject to Canadian tax only on Canadian-source partnership income. Alberta imposes no provincial sales tax or provincial partnership-level tax, but a non-resident partner may face Alberta Part I tax via the federal collection agreement.

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box elective classification regime; classification follows legal form, and an LP is inherently flow-through. Available elections relevant to this structure include: (1) partnership fiscal period election under ITA section 249.1; (2) optional section 216 return for certain non-resident incomes; (3) GST/HST Quick Method of Accounting election (GST/HST Memorandum 10.1); (4) GST/HST annual filing election; (5) section 18.2(9) and 18.3(4) transitional elections for hybrid mismatch basis/asset-liability timing mismatches under Canadas hybrid mismatch rules; (6) deemed liability election under section 150(2.1). Hybrid mismatch rules generally operate as default denial rules rather than elective outcomes for cross-border arrangements involving tax-indifferent entities.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 annual worldwide taxable supply threshold: registration becomes mandatory when taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter or in the aggregate over the four immediately preceding calendar quarters (ETA section 240). Voluntary registration is available below this threshold, subject to CRA acceptance, and foreign non-residents may be required to register without regard to the threshold in certain circumstances involving supplies made in Canada through digital platforms or Canadian sales channels.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to: (1) exports of tangible goods under ETA section 21 where the supplier in Canada transfers ownership to a non-resident and either exports the goods or causes their removal from Canada; (2) exported goods later imported for further processing under ETA section 22; (3) supplies of services to non-residents under ETA section 23 where the service is not performed in Canada and is not consumed or used in Canada; this includes remote multi-channel customer support/helpdesk SaaS services delivered entirely to foreign clients, as recognized in CRA policy guidance for cross-border service outsourcing; and (4) supplies of intangible personal property to non-residents under ETA section 25.1. Eligibility conditions require documentary evidence of the customer non-resident status, evidence of overseas delivery of the service, and contractual terms confirming the benefit is received exclusively outside Canada. Exclusions apply if the service relates to Canadian real estate, tangible personal property situated in Canada, or Canadian employees, or if the non-resident customer intercepts the benefit in Canada.

  • Commercial invoice including seller and purchaser details, HS classification, value in CAD, and Incoterms
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill issued by the carrier evidencing export date, destination country, and consignee
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank evidence of payment (SWIFT MT103, Stripe Billing, or PayPal settlement report for SaaS subscriptions)

No statutory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, digital stamping, or mandatory API pre-clearance regime exists in Canada. E-invoicing is voluntary; CRA accepts electronic records and EDI-based documentation under the Electronic Records and Books and Records requirements. The Pan-Canadian PEPPOL standard is being phased in for suppliers invoicing the Government of Canada (starting fiscal 2026), but private-sector B2B or B2C e-invoicing is not federally mandated. Taxpayers must retain invoices, contracts, and supporting records for six years from the end of the record period.

The CRA may deny zero-rating, reassess at the standard 5% GST/HST rate, and charge quarterly prescribed interest if export documentation is insufficient or non-contemporaneous. Civil penalties include: (1) a 25% penalty under ETA section 285 for false statements or omissions in a return where tax was understated or an input tax credit or refund overstated; (2) penalties up to 200% of the net tax or refund under ETA section 280 for willful evasion; (3) criminal prosecution with fines of up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment of up to two years for tax evasion offences; and (4) retrospective clawback of input tax credits attributed to improperly claimed zero-rated exports. CRA audit practice requires the exporter to retain the complete documentation bundle for six years, and recovery can extend from the date of the first non-compliant export to the full assessment period permitted under the normal reassessment limitation period or longer where misrepresentation or fraud is established.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canadian Dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current and capital accounts under a free-floating exchange-rate regime. Canada does not operate exchange controls, a surrender requirement, or an investment motive based FX licensing regime under the Currency Act or the Bank of Canada Act. The relevant restriction is the Investment Canada Act (ICA), which requires notification and, for acquisitions above Enterprise Value thresholds (C$1.343bn for WTO investors in 2024; C$929m for Canadian businesses with high enterprise value), a pre-closing net benefit review for foreign investments acquiring control of a Canadian business. Inbound capital injection into, and outbound profit/capital repatriation from, an Alberta limited partnership is unhindered; however, a non-resident limited partner withdrawing tax-basis capital or receiving distributions in excess of their contributed adjusted cost base (ACB) may trigger Part XIII withholding tax at 25% (subject to treaty reduction, e.g., 15% dividends exemption, or 0% for certain capital dispositions), and Canadian-source business income of a non-resident LP is subject to Part I tax on a net basis via the deemed-carrying-on-business rules in the Income Tax Act (ITA) s. 2(3). Commercial reasonableness: there is no mandatory FX approval for USD/CAD in-bound or out-bound flows, but all wires must pass normal FINTRAC reporting thresholds (>C$10,000 daily incoming or a large transaction report where applicable).

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no export proceeds surrender obligation: the Income Tax Act and Currency Act contain no provision mandating repatriation, conversion, or compulsory sale of foreign exchange to the central bank. Export proceeds and SaaS subscription revenue denominated in USD/EUR/Foreign currency may be retained offshore or in a Canadian-dollar or foreign-currency account (such as an RBC USD 'Swap' account or TD foreign-currency savings) without restriction. For trades, the currency exchange may be arranged freely via a chartered bank, an authorized foreign exchange dealer (MSB registered with FINTRAC), or an a securities dealer; for financial statement purposes, ASC/PFRS translation gains and losses must be recognized under IAS 21.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), established under the Bank of Canada Act (1934); prudential supervision of banks and federally regulated financial institutions is the mandate of the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); conduct and market integrity supervision of securities market participants is the mandate of the Canadian Securities Administrators and self-regulatory organizations

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Limited Partnership: generally no entity-level Canadian income tax; income and losses are allocated to partners. Alberta corporate benchmark: 15% federal general rate plus 8% Alberta general rate, for a combined 23%. Eligible Canadian-controlled private corporations may generally access a combined 11% small-business rate on qualifying active business income within applicable limits.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding applies to taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. A Canadian LP does not ordinarily pay dividends; distributions are generally partnership distributions and must be analyzed as returns of capital, allocations of income, interest, royalties, or other payments. Treaty treatment depends on the partner's residence, legal classification, beneficial ownership, and applicable treaty article.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Generally due within five months after the partnership fiscal period-end where all partners are corporations, and otherwise within three months after the fiscal period-end. Certain partnership structures and filing circumstances can impose the five-month deadline. Partnership information slips are generally due by the last day of the third month after the partnership year-end.

Deadline: · Frequency:

An LP ordinarily does not file T2 or AT1 because it is not a corporation. A corporate partner, incorporated general partner, or incorporated subsidiary must generally file within six months after its taxation year-end. Any balance is generally payable two months after year-end, or three months for qualifying CCPCs subject to statutory conditions.

Deadline: · Frequency:

For annual filers, the deadline depends on the registrant's reporting period and whether the registrant is an individual or other person. Monthly and quarterly returns are generally due one month after the reporting period. GST/HST balances are generally due with the return.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general transaction-value threshold that eliminates the contemporaneous documentation requirement under section 247(4) for non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents. Form T106 generally applies where the aggregate value of reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceeds CAD 1,000,000, subject to detailed statutory rules. The transfer-pricing penalty threshold is generally met where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of the taxpayer's gross revenue, unless reasonable efforts and contemporaneous documentation requirements are satisfied.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone United Kingdom-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Substance is assessed through section 245 GAAR, section 247 transfer-pricing rules, treaty anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership analysis, sham and agency doctrines, permanent-establishment rules, and general deductibility requirements. The LP should maintain Canadian commercial activity proportionate to the business claimed, including decision-making, personnel or contracted operational capability, customer-support controls, technology management, risk assumption, records, and governance. A legal entity with no credible functions, assets, or risks may face denied deductions, recharacterization, withholding, treaty denial, or GAAR application.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalty generally up to 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed amount, and prosecution under the Income Tax Act or Excise Tax Act. Criminal convictions can result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years, subject to the applicable offence.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible officers may face prosecution, personal liability for unremitted source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts, civil recovery, director-liability assessments, and imprisonment after conviction. Travel bans are not an automatic tax penalty but may arise from criminal process, bail conditions, arrest warrants, or immigration consequences.
Failure to Withhold and Remit

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unremitted amount, 10% penalty for failure to deduct, 20% for repeated failure, arrears interest, denial of treaty relief, and possible director-liability assessment. Regulation 105 failures can also result in payer liability and penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally under applicable statutory director-liability provisions where the corporation or responsible entity failed to remit source deductions or specified indirect taxes and statutory due-diligence defenses are unavailable.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Primary and secondary transfer-pricing adjustments, denial of deductions, withholding tax, interest, and a penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where statutory penalty conditions apply. Contemporaneous documentation and reasonable-efforts defenses must be assessed.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for a transfer-pricing adjustment, but personal exposure can arise from knowing participation in fraud, obstruction, false statements, criminal tax offences, or misuse of corporate assets.
Shell Company and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits, GAAR reassessment, withholding at domestic rates, interest, penalties, beneficial-ownership challenges, and possible reassessment of the actual recipient or permanent establishment.

Director personal liability: Individuals may face civil recovery, prosecution for fraud or tax evasion, director disqualification under applicable corporate law, and imprisonment upon conviction. Corporate veil piercing is exceptional and fact-specific, not automatic.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, regulatory enforcement, seizure and forfeiture, and Criminal Code or Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act prosecution. Criminal penalties can include substantial fines and imprisonment, depending on the offence.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and beneficial owners may face personal criminal liability, asset restraint and forfeiture, prohibition orders, and imprisonment. A payment-platform or SaaS business may have reporting-entity obligations depending on its actual services, payment processing role, and activities.
GST/HST Fraud and Improper Input Tax Credits

Statutory sanction: Assessment of tax, interest, penalties for false statements or gross negligence, denial of input tax credits, registration consequences, and prosecution under the Excise Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal assessment for certain unremitted GST/HST amounts and criminal liability for deliberate fraud or false records.
Payroll and Employment-Source-Deduction Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of source deductions, CPP and EI, penalties, interest, payroll audits, and possible employment-law claims. Deliberate failure to deduct or remit can lead to prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted payroll source deductions where statutory conditions apply and may face criminal sanctions for intentional evasion.
Recordkeeping and Obstruction

Statutory sanction: Document-production orders, compliance orders, monetary penalties, adverse reassessments, contempt consequences, and criminal charges for obstruction or false statements.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors, officers, and agents may be prosecuted personally and may face fines or imprisonment. Travel restrictions may arise only through criminal or court processes, not merely from an ordinary late filing.
