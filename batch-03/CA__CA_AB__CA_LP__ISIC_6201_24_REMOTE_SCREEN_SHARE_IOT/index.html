E-government filing portal

Foreign or non-resident entities establishing a Limited Partnership in Alberta must register with the CRA to obtain a Business Number (BN) and GST/HST number. Non-residents register via Form GST400 or the My Business Account portal if making taxable supplies in Canada. Required documents include LP incorporation papers, business plan, and proof of activities. Registration is mandatory upon exceeding the GST threshold or carrying on business in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

GST is zero-rated on exports of goods and on qualifying services supplied to non-residents (e.g., remote desktop access software performed outside Canada). Eligibility requires the supply to be exported, the recipient to be a non-resident, and proper substantiation of export and payment.

  • Customs Export Declaration (if goods involved)
  • Bill of Lading or equivalent shipping proof
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or proof of non-resident payment

No real-time electronic invoicing, API integration, or digital stamping mandated for GST. All registrants file GST/HST returns electronically through the CRA My Business Account portal on a quarterly or monthly basis based on volume. Compliance relies on accurate record-keeping and timely remittance of any collected GST.

Fraudulent zero-rating claims trigger full GST reassessment, interest, penalties up to 50% of the evaded amount, and potential criminal prosecution. Retrospective clawback applies with liability for unremitted GST plus administrative penalties.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully and freely convertible for both current-account and capital-account transactions. There are no exchange controls, no profit-remittance licensing, and no registration gates for inbound foreign direct investment into an Alberta Limited Partnership. The only material friction is tax-based: non-resident Limited Partners are subject to Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (Canada) on distributions of passive Canadian-source income (25% statutory, often treaty-reduced), whereas active business income allocated through the pass-through LP to non-resident partners is taxable on a net basis under sections 2(3) and 115 if the LP carries on business in Canada. Repatriation of liquidation proceeds and capital repayments is unrestricted, provided the LP files accurate T5013 schedules and the partners maintain Canadian tax compliance. Cross-border remittance from SaaS subscription revenue collected via Stripe and Paddle is treated as ordinary export sales income and is fully convertible without Bank of Canada intervention.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender, repatriation, or conversion obligations on export receipts. An Alberta LP deriving ISIC 6201 software-licensing revenue from non-residents may hold 100% of foreign-currency proceeds indefinitely in domestic or offshore accounts; there is no mandatory conversion ratio, no regulated timeline for repatriation, and no requirement to sell foreign exchange to the central bank. The only compliance triggers are FINTRAC reporting of cross-border currency movements of CAD 10,000 or more, and PCMLTFA record-keeping for large cash transactions. Optional hedging of export receivables in the interbank FX market is unrestricted.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) under the Bank of Canada Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. B-2, with exchange and monetary policy administered jointly with the Department of Finance Canada, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Canada maintains no dedicated foreign-exchange-control administration and no Currency Act exchange-control approval regime.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium, with strict compliance conditions. Offshore digital EMIs and payment facilitators (Wise Platform, Airwallex, Payoneer, Currencycloud, Multri) are legally viable for FX conversion, multi-currency wallets, and disbursement execution, but they are not Schedule I banks under the Bank Act, cannot accept retail deposits in Canada, and cannot be used as a substitute for a Canadian chartered-bank account. If the EMI's services create a Canadian-nexus payment service, the EMI or the LP's Treasury function may trigger FINTRAC money-services-business registration and PCMLTFA reporting duties. Optimal architecture: (1) a domestic Schedule I operating account for CRA payroll/GST-HST remittances and Alberta LP statutory obligations; (2) Stripe/Paddle merchant settlement accounts for global SaaS collections; and (3) an EMI only as an auxiliary cross-border disbursement and FX clearing rail, never as the primary settlement account.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound routing for capital injections into the Alberta LP: (1) each non-resident partner sends a SWIFT MT103 / ISO 20022 payment to the LP's CAD or USD account at a Schedule I Canadian bank, with the narrative 'LP capital contribution - partnership agreement dated [DD-MM-YYYY]' and, where the receiving bank requires a purpose code, the ISO treasury category 'Equity Capital' or 'Direct Investment'; (2) USD-denominated injections should settle through the Canadian bank's correspondent network using CLS-eligible same-day processes to eliminate double FX conversion; (3) the bank will apply PCMLTFA large-value and sanctions screening; and (4) the LP must record the inflow as equity in the partnership books, not as debt, to avoid recharacterization risk under section 212 and thin-capitalization rules of the Income Tax Act. If any injection is structured as a partner loan, arm's-length interest rates must apply and NR4/NR3016 withholding filings may be triggered. Stripe and Paddle settlement funds must never be routed to a capital-escrow account; they must land only in the designated operational 'merchant settlement' ledger.For an ISIC 6201 SaaS LP, outbound disbursement rails include: (1) Payments Canada EFT/ACH and e-Transfer for domestic small-value payments, including CRA remittances (GST/HST, payroll source deductions) and Alberta corporate registry filings; (2) SWIFT MT103 direct transfers for cross-border supplier, cloud-infrastructure, and reseller payments above CAD 50,000 or in foreign currency, converted at same-day interbank rates; (3) commercial letters of credit are not standard for software licensing but an irrevocable confirmed LC advised through a Calgary or Toronto correspondent is permissible where an OEM or enterprise reseller requires trade instruments; (4) Stripe and Paddle payout rails using domestic ACH or locale-specific payout methods for recurring merchant settlement into the LP's operating account; and (5) non-cash wire transfers are not subject to FINTRAC currency-reporting thresholds, but all cross-border disbursements remain subject to sanctions screening and transaction monitoring. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

0% at the Alberta limited partnership entity level where the partnership is fiscally transparent and does not itself earn income subject to entity-level corporate income tax. Income is allocated to partners. A taxable Canadian corporation carrying on the same business is generally subject to a combined federal and Alberta rate of 23%: 15% federal plus 8% Alberta general rate. The combined small-business rate can be approximately 11%: 9% federal plus 2% Alberta, subject to eligibility and the applicable business limit.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. A limited partnership does not ordinarily pay corporate dividends. Distributions of partnership income are analyzed under partnership allocation rules, Canadian-source income rules, and any applicable withholding obligations rather than automatically as dividends.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T5013 return is generally due by the last day of the third month after the partnership's fiscal period. For a December 31 year-end, the usual deadline is March 31. Partnership information slips must generally be provided to partners and filed with the CRA by the same deadline. A partnership information return may be mandatory where, among other circumstances, the partnership has a corporate or trust partner, has a non-resident partner, exceeds applicable revenue or expense thresholds, holds significant assets, or is otherwise required by the Income Tax Act.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Part XIII tax is generally due by the 15th day of the month following the month in which the amount was paid or credited. NR4 information returns and slips are generally due by March 31 of the year following the calendar year in which the amounts were paid or credited.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For Canadian cross-border non-arm's-length transactions, contemporaneous documentation is generally required under Income Tax Act section 247. A detailed documentation obligation is generally triggered where the aggregate value of the relevant non-arm's-length cross-border transactions, excluding certain remuneration and specified items, exceeds CAD 1,000,000 for the year. Canada does not generally impose a separate statutory Local File/Master File filing regime in the same form as many OECD jurisdictions. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone economic substance regulations equivalent to the United Kingdom or certain other jurisdictions. Economic substance remains relevant through the federal general anti-avoidance rule, the transfer-pricing arm's-length requirement, treaty beneficial-ownership analysis, treaty anti-abuse and principal-purpose provisions where applicable, sham-transaction principles, debt characterization, foreign-affiliate rules, and source-of-income analysis. A remote-support software LP should maintain Canadian decision-making evidence, personnel or outsourced capabilities, product-development records, IP ownership and licensing records, customer and vendor contracts, cybersecurity controls, bank records, and commercially coherent profit allocation.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, criminal conviction can result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to two years, in addition to assessment of tax, interest, civil penalties, and recovery costs. Fraud may also be prosecuted under the Criminal Code, with materially higher potential imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, officers, and persons who authorized, acquiesced in, or participated in the conduct may face personal criminal liability. Corporate or partnership form does not protect deliberate tax fraud. Courts may impose restitution or fines and, under criminal justice processes, release or sentencing conditions can restrict travel; a general automatic tax-debt travel ban does not ordinarily apply.
Failure to File Partnership or Corporate Returns

Statutory sanction: Administrative late-filing penalties, daily T5013 penalties where applicable, 5% plus 1% per month late T2 penalties subject to statutory limits, prescribed interest, loss of discretionary relief, and possible demand-to-file or prosecution proceedings.

Director personal liability: Directors may become personally liable for unremitted source deductions and certain other amounts under statutory director-liability rules if the legal conditions are met, including expiry of the applicable limitation period and failure to exercise due diligence.
Unremitted Non-Resident Withholding Tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the tax that should have been withheld, arrears interest, failure-to-withhold penalties, late-remittance penalties, and information-return penalties. CRA may deny treaty-rate treatment and assess the domestic 25% rate.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors may face personal assessment for certain unremitted amounts where statutory director-liability provisions apply. Intentional evasion may create criminal exposure.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may make a transfer-pricing adjustment under section 247, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the adjustment in qualifying cases, deny deductions, recharacterize transactions, assess interest, and apply the general anti-avoidance rule where relevant.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face civil or criminal exposure where records were knowingly falsified or tax evasion was intentional. Ordinary transfer-pricing adjustments ordinarily remain liabilities of the taxpayer rather than automatic personal liabilities.
Shell Company, Sham, or Treaty-Conduit Structure

Statutory sanction: CRA may invoke the general anti-avoidance rule, deny treaty benefits, recharacterize income, assess withholding tax, impose penalties and interest, and apply beneficial-ownership and anti-abuse rules. Transactions may also trigger mandatory disclosure or reportable-transaction obligations where statutory conditions are met.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly implement sham arrangements may face civil reassessment, third-party civil claims, professional sanctions, and criminal tax-fraud exposure. Corporate separateness will not protect fraudulent conduct.
AML, Proceeds of Crime, and Sanctions

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and sanctions legislation can produce compliance orders, administrative monetary penalties, asset restraint or forfeiture, prosecution, significant fines, and imprisonment. The precise regime depends on the entity's regulated status and activity.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, compliance personnel, and partners may face personal criminal liability for knowing participation, wilful blindness, sanctions violations, or obstruction. Assets may be frozen or forfeited, and criminal bail or sentencing conditions can restrict travel.
Permanent Establishment and Unreported Canadian Activities

Statutory sanction: CRA may assert Canadian residence or a Canadian permanent establishment, assess Canadian income tax, GST/HST, payroll obligations, withholding tax, interest, penalties, and provincial tax. Treaty protection depends on the relevant treaty tests and factual substance.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may face personal source-deduction liability and criminal exposure for intentional concealment. Ordinary residence or permanent-establishment disputes generally do not by themselves create personal liability.
Payroll, Contractor, and Misclassification Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: The payer may owe unremitted deductions, employer contributions, interest, penalties, reassessments, and employment-insurance or workers' compensation amounts. Gross-negligence or failure-to-remit penalties may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted source deductions under the Income Tax Act and related legislation where statutory conditions are satisfied, including failure to exercise due diligence.
Books, Records, and Electronic Evidence Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA may impose penalties, issue compliance demands, disallow deductions, extend reassessment periods in cases of misrepresentation attributable to neglect or fraud, and prosecute obstruction or record-keeping offenses.

Director personal liability: Individuals who destroy records, make false statements, or obstruct an audit may face personal civil penalties and criminal prosecution, including imprisonment in serious cases.
