Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC), including the My Business Account portal (canada.ca); Alberta Ministry of Treasury Board and Finance and the Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration (ATRA) for Alberta non-income taxes, with Alberta corporate income tax administered by CRA under the Alberta Tax Collection Agreement; Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for customs and export controls.

E-government filing portal

A foreign participant establishing an Alberta limited partnership should: (1) register the LP under the Partnership Act (Alberta) and maintain an Alberta registered office for the general partner; (2) apply to CRA for a Business Number (BN) using Form RC1 or Business Registration Online (BRO); the BN is the statutory TIN and may be expanded with program accounts, including RT (GST/HST), RP (payroll), RC (corporate tax) and RD (import/export); (3) recognize that for income tax the LP is fiscally transparent and files only an information return (T5013), with each partner's TIN disclosed in CRA schedules; and (4) register under the Excise Tax Act (ETA) s. 240 for GST/HST only where the LP makes taxable supplies in Canada and is not a small supplier (worldwide taxable consideration over C$30,000 in the current and four preceding calendar quarters). A pure-equity holding SPV whose income consists solely of dividends and share-disposition gains is not engaged in a commercial activity and is not required to hold a GST/HST account. A non-resident partner's participation may cause the LP to be a non-resident partnership under ITA s. 102, triggering Part XIII Canadian-source withholding for allocated Canadian dividends and specific T5/T5013 disclosure obligations.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not permit a US-style check-the-box classification election. An Alberta LP is fiscally transparent under Income Tax Act s. 96. Elections available to an equity-holding SPV include: (a) the s. 97(2) partnership rollover election for transfers of capital property to the LP; (b) the s. 85 rollover election on transfers of corporate shares to a corporation; (c) the s. 216(1) election for a non-resident partner to file a net-basis return on Canadian rental income; and (d) hybrid-mismatch designations under ITA ss. 18.2 and 18.3 (effective July 1, 2022) requiring contemporaneous documentation to prevent deduction-without-inclusion outcomes for foreign limited partners. Under the ETA, a partnership is a person for GST/HST; elections affecting 'selected financial institution' status may apply where financing activities are conducted.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000 in total consideration from worldwide taxable supplies realized in a current calendar quarter and the four preceding calendar quarters (small-supplier threshold, ETA ss. 123(1) and 148). A pure-equity holding SPV receiving only dividends and capital gains normally has no taxable supplies and no mandatory registration obligation; any in-scope management or support services supplied to affiliates would count toward the threshold and trigger registration once exceeded.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Schedule VI to the ETA, supplies of goods exported by the supplier are zero-rated at 0% if the goods are shipped to a non-resident and exported as soon as reasonably possible after the supply. Services supplied to non-residents are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI where the service does not relate to real property situated in Canada, does not relate to tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance, and is not a personal service rendered to an individual physically present in Canada. For a pure-equity holding SPV, dividends and dispositions of shares are exempt financial services (Schedule V, Part VII), not exports, and do not trigger zero-rating or input tax credit entitlement.

  • CBSA-approved export declaration (Form A1 paper export declaration or electronic CERS/cargo control record) evidencing physical export from Canada
  • Commercial invoice and underlying supply contract identifying the non-resident purchaser and foreign place of supply
  • Bill of lading (ocean, air, rail or road) or courier/freight-forwarder manifest showing the foreign destination
  • Payment evidence in foreign currency (SWIFT MT103, wire transfer advice, or bank inward remittance statement) for the exported goods or zero-rated services
  • Non-resident status evidence (certificate of residence, foreign registry extract, or statutory declaration) supporting zero-rated services
  • Reconciliation schedules linking export and payment dates to the GST/HST return and underlying CRA books and records

Canada does not impose real-time fiscal clearance, API pre-authorization, or digital stamping for GST/HST. Registered persons must issue compliant tax invoices (ETA s. 223) showing the supplier's BN, date, description and tax amounts, and must keep books and records in machine-readable form. Canada is implementing the PEPPOL-based Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Standard on a voluntary, phased basis; electronic invoicing is required for suppliers to the federal government through the PSPC 'Simplify' e-procurement platform. No additional fiscal clearance mandate applies in Alberta.

Canada has no statutory exchange-control repatriation deadline; the 180-day benchmark is used by CRA as an evidentiary norm for confirming that export consideration was received and retained. CRA may reassess within the normal four-year period, and without time limit where misrepresentation, fraud or wilful default is established; disallowed zero-rating re-imposes the 5% GST plus interest from the original supply date. Penalties for fraudulent zero-rating include ETA false-statement and gross-negligence penalties (up to the greater of C$500 and 50% of the net tax involved), ETA s. 327 criminal penalties, and ITA s. 163(2) gross-negligence penalties of 50% of the understated tax, together with criminal prosecution under ITA ss. 238 and 239.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital account. Canada maintains no exchange control regime under the Currency Act or Bank of Canada Act; cross-border transfers of funds, dividend repatriations, and capital redemptions are free from prior central bank approval, but are subject to tax compliance (withholding tax on non-resident distributions) and mandatory FINTRAC reporting for transactions of CAD 10,000 or more. Profit remittance hurdles are limited to statutory withholding tax obligations and administrative filing requirements under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and Canada-Alberta tax information exchange agreements.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. There is no statutory surrender or mandated conversion of export proceeds in Canada. For the limited partnership engaged in pure equity holding (ISIC 6420), no export receipts arise; general trade settlement proceeds are freely held in CAD or foreign currency without any compulsory repatriation timeline.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (central monetary and exchange rate authority) in coordination with the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for anti-money laundering/anti-terrorist financing reporting and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential bank regulation

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility: Medium as primary treasury rail, High as supplementary clearing rail. Canada does not issue dedicated 'electronic money institution' licences in the EU sense; off-shore EMIs (e.g., Wise, Revolut) may operate as Money Services Businesses registered with FINTRAC. Legal constraints: such entities cannot hold deposits in a Canadian chartered bank context, do not provide the same Level 1 clearing membership or CDSX direct participation, and are generally not acceptable for capital escrow or intercompany loan servicing by a non-resident-owned LP. They are viable for small-value operational disbursements but must not be used to circumvent regulated banking channels or tax reporting under FATCA/CRS.

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

An Alberta limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not itself subject to Canadian corporate income tax. A Canadian-resident corporate partner is generally subject to 15% federal corporate income tax plus 8% Alberta general corporate income tax, for a combined general rate of 23%. Eligible Alberta small-business corporations may generally receive a combined rate of approximately 11% on qualifying active business income.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The Canadian domestic withholding tax rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is generally 25%. Treaty rates commonly reduce the rate to 5% for a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting ownership and entitlement conditions and 15% for other qualifying shareholders. The Canada-United States treaty commonly provides a 5% rate for a company beneficially owning at least 10% of the voting stock and a 15% rate in other ordinary cases, subject to treaty conditions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Generally due within 5 months after the end of the partnership fiscal period. A partnership fiscal period normally cannot exceed 12 months. The return is generally required where prescribed filing conditions are met, including certain partnerships with corporate, trust, or non-resident partners, or where the partnership has specified income, losses, or other reporting characteristics.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Amounts withheld must generally be remitted by the 15th day of the month following the month in which the amount was paid or credited. NR4 information returns and slips are generally due by the last day of March following the calendar year.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a universal monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is unnecessary. Canadian taxpayers must make reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length terms and maintain contemporaneous documentation. Form T106 generally applies where aggregate reportable non-arm's-length cross-border transactions exceed CAD 1,000,000 for the year, subject to the statutory rules and exclusions. Canada has OECD-aligned country-by-country reporting for constituent entities of multinational enterprise groups meeting the EUR 750 million consolidated revenue threshold. Canada does not generally impose a separate mandatory Master File and Local File filing regime identical to jurisdictions using those labels, although CRA may require comprehensive supporting documentation.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance remains relevant through the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse and principal-purpose concepts where applicable, beneficial-ownership analysis, transfer-pricing rules, thin-capitalization rules, interest-limitation rules, corporate residence and central-management-and-control principles, and the requirement that transactions be legally and commercially supportable. A passive holding LP should maintain genuine governance, documented investment rationale, books and records, partner resolutions, bank-account controls, and evidence that allocations and distributions reflect the partnership agreement and actual commercial arrangements.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, criminal tax evasion can result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment of up to 5 years, or both. Civil reassessment, gross-negligence penalties, arrears interest, and penalties for repeated failures may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and other individuals who authorize, assent to, or participate in misconduct may face criminal prosecution and personal fines or imprisonment. Limited liability does not protect a person from personal participation in fraud or criminal conduct.
Gross Negligence and Inaccurate Returns

Statutory sanction: A gross-negligence penalty is generally 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit attributable to the conduct, in addition to reassessed tax and interest.

Director personal liability: Personal liability is not automatic for ordinary corporate income tax errors, but individuals may be liable where they personally participated in false reporting, fraud, or a statutory source-deduction obligation.
Failure to File T5013 or T106

Statutory sanction: Administrative late-filing penalties, commonly calculated at CAD 25 per day subject to statutory minimums and maximums, may apply. Additional penalties may apply for repeated failures, failure to provide information, false statements, or gross negligence.

Director personal liability: The general partner and persons responsible for compliance may face enforcement action where they directed or knowingly permitted non-compliance. Personal liability depends on the applicable statutory provision and the individual's conduct.
Improper Non-Resident Withholding

Statutory sanction: CRA may assess the payer for the unremitted withholding tax, applicable penalties, and arrears interest. Incorrect treaty claims may be reassessed at the 25% domestic rate, subject to available relief and statutory rules.

Director personal liability: Directors may face derivative liability for source deductions in statutory circumstances, particularly where the corporation failed to remit amounts withheld or required to be withheld. A general partner may also face contractual or statutory exposure depending on the partnership structure.
Transfer-Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust income under section 247, deny or reduce deductions, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the relevant adjustment where statutory conditions are met, and assess interest.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners are not ordinarily personally liable merely because a transfer-pricing adjustment is made, but personal exposure may arise from deliberate fraud, false records, or participation in abusive arrangements.
General Anti-Avoidance Rule and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny the tax benefit under the Income Tax Act general anti-avoidance rule, apply treaty anti-abuse principles where relevant, reassess tax, and impose interest and applicable penalties. The absence of a standalone violation does not prevent GAAR application.

Director personal liability: Civil tax liability generally rests with the taxpayer, but individuals may face penalties or criminal consequences for fraudulent implementation, false evidence, or deliberate concealment.
Shell Company, Beneficial Ownership, and Residence Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Treaty benefits may be denied, withholding may be reassessed at domestic rates, and anti-avoidance or beneficial-ownership rules may apply. False statements can result in civil penalties and criminal prosecution.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly submit false ownership or residence information may face personal civil and criminal exposure. Travel restrictions are not an automatic Canadian tax sanction but may arise from criminal proceedings, bail conditions, or immigration measures.
Anti-Money Laundering and Sanctions

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, Criminal Code, and Canadian sanctions legislation may support transaction blocking, forfeiture, administrative monetary penalties, and criminal fines or imprisonment. FINTRAC obligations apply principally to regulated reporting entities, but all persons remain subject to criminal and sanctions laws.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, beneficial owners, and officers may face personal criminal liability for knowing participation, facilitation, or laundering. Imprisonment, asset forfeiture, account freezing, and court-imposed travel restrictions may arise in criminal cases.
Limited Partnership Liability and Improper Management

Statutory sanction: Under Alberta partnership law and the partnership agreement, a limited partner may risk loss of limited-liability protection if it participates in control beyond permitted statutory limits. The LP, general partner, or relevant partner may also face civil claims, insolvency proceedings, and contractual damages.

Director personal liability: A general partner is generally liable for partnership obligations. Limited partners may incur personal liability to the extent they lose statutory protection, personally guarantee obligations, commit torts, or participate in fraud. Piercing the corporate veil is exceptional and fact-dependent; civil restitution and imprisonment require separate legal grounds and are not automatic.
