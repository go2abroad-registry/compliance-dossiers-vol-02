Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — federal tax administration via 'My Business Account' and Business Registration Online (BRO) portal at canada.ca; Alberta corporate income tax is administered by CRA under the Canada–Alberta Tax Collection Agreement through the Alberta Ministry of Finance (Alberta has no provincial sales tax and no separate provincial VAT/GST registration portal).

E-government filing portal

For a non-resident-owned Alberta Limited Partnership carrying on business in Canada: (1) register the Alberta LP with the Government of Alberta's Companies Office (via an authorized Corporate Registry service provider) to obtain the provincial registration number; (2) apply online through CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) or by filing Form RC1 to obtain the 9-digit federal Business Number (BN) and open a GST/HST program account (RT); (3) a non-resident partner that neither carries on business in Canada nor has a permanent establishment in Canada may nevertheless be required to file a non-resident GST/HST return or seek simplified registration where the LP makes zero-rated supplies; (4) CRA issues the BN and GST/HST account (14-character RT program account) immediately through BRO, while Form RC1 processing takes 4–6 weeks; (5) the LP itself, not the limited partners, holds the BN for GST/HST purposes; a corporate general partner may require its own BN for corporate income tax and payroll accounts if it has employees in Canada.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — an Alberta Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax purposes: the LP is not a taxpayer, derives no taxable income itself, and each partner is taxed on its allocable share of partnership income; the LP files an information return (T5013). For GST/HST purposes, however, the partnership is treated as a separate 'person' under the Excise Tax Act and may be a GST/HST registrant distinct from its partners. — For a non-resident-owned Alberta Limited Partnership carrying on business in Canada: (1) register the Alberta LP with the Government of Alberta's Companies Office (via an authorized Corporate Registry service provider) to obtain the provincial registration number; (2) apply online through CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) or by filing Form RC1 to obtain the 9-digit federal Business Number (BN) and open a GST/HST program account (RT); (3) a non-resident partner that neither carries on business in Canada nor has a permanent establishment in Canada may nevertheless be required to file a non-resident GST/HST return or seek simplified registration where the LP makes zero-rated supplies; (4) CRA issues the BN and GST/HST account (14-character RT program account) immediately through BRO, while Form RC1 processing takes 4–6 weeks; (5) the LP itself, not the limited partners, holds the BN for GST/HST purposes; a corporate general partner may require its own BN for corporate income tax and payroll accounts if it has employees in Canada.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — an Alberta Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax purposes: the LP is not a taxpayer, derives no taxable income itself, and each partner is taxed on its allocable share of partnership income; the LP files an information return (T5013). For GST/HST purposes, however, the partnership is treated as a separate 'person' under the Excise Tax Act and may be a GST/HST registrant distinct from its partners.

Available election forms

Canada has no 'check-the-box' classification election comparable to the U.S.; entity classification follows legal form. An Alberta LP is flow-through by statute. Available statutory elections/mechanisms relevant to a global IP licensing LP: (i) partnership fiscal-period election under subsection 34(1) (or section 34.1) of the Income Tax Act to use a non-calendar year-end where corporate or non-resident partners hold the preponderant interest; (ii) non-resident partners may elect to file an NR6 undertaking and apply treaty-reduced Part XIII withholding rates on Canadian-source royalty allocations flowing through the LP, subject to filing prescribed forms and obtaining a CRA waiver; (iii) limited partners may claim capital cost allowance and Canadian tax credits through the transparent structure; (iv) Canada's hybrid mismatch rules (implementing OECD BEPS Action 2) may disallow deductions or require withholding adjustments where a counterparty jurisdiction treats the LP as opaque or a deductible royalty payment gives rise to a double-dip outcome; these rules operate as automatic integrity recharacterizations, not as elective regimes, and there is no 'protective' check-the-box election available under Canadian federal law.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the GST/HST small-supplier threshold under the Excise Tax Act is C$30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; a person carrying on a commercial activity in Canada that exceeds this threshold must register for GST/HST, although a non-resident making only zero-rated supplies who does not carry on business in Canada may register voluntarily to claim input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

The supply of a patent right (intangible personal property) by the Alberta LP to a non-resident licensee is zero-rated under section 7 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act when: (i) the recipient is a non-resident person who is not registered under the ETA; (ii) the patent or trademark right is not for use in Canada and is not exploited in Canada; (iii) the LP retains contemporaneous evidence of the non-resident status of the licensee, the foreign destination/use of the licensed IP, and the arm's-length royalty quantum. Related services supplied to the non-resident licensee (e.g., technical support, sub-licensing administration) are likewise zero-rated under section 9 of Part V of Schedule VI if the services do not relate to real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property located in Canada. The zero-rate does not apply where the recipient is a registered GST/HST registrant or where the IP is in fact exploited in Canada; in that case, GST at 5% applies.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Export Declaration B13A) — required only where the patent is embodied in tangible goods or a physical medium crossing the Canadian border
  • Commercial Invoice stating the royalty stream, licence territory, and explicit zero-rating basis under Schedule VI
  • Executed global IP licence / sub-licence agreement (original or certified copy) evidencing the non-resident licensee and restricted jurisdiction
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) issued by the partnership's Canadian bank, or equivalent SWIFT/MT103 credit advice, evidencing the actual receipt of foreign royalty payments to the LP's Canadian account
  • Foreign tax residence certificate of the licensee (e.g., IRS Form 6166, HMRC Certificate of Residence) or Form W-8BEN-E where the licensee is the counterparty, together with any Part XIII waiver or treaty certificate
  • CRA GST/HST registration confirmation (RT account) and tax returns showing the zero-rated treatment of the export supply

Canada imposes no statutory real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or pre-clearance API mandate for federal GST/HST e-invoicing. CRA accepts electronic records and EDI/e-invoicing voluntarily, with no requirement for a third-party clearance portal. Invoices must contain the prescribed particulars under CRA GST/HST Memorandum 14-1: supplier's Business Number, date, recipient's name, description of the supply, and the tax status/amount (GST rate applied or zero-rated indicator). For B2B royalty licensing, a compliant invoice with a zero-rate supply code and reference to the licence agreement suffices; CRA does not operate a government-mandated fiscal stamping regime as of the current effective tax period.

CRA may reassess an LP's zero-rating within the normal 4-year limitation period, extended to 7 years in cases of negligence or misrepresentation, and without time limit for fraud. If zero-rating is denied or a supply is recharacterized as taxable in Canada, the LP is liable for the unreported GST (5% of the supply value) plus daily simple arrears interest from the original reporting period and a penalty under subsection 280(1) of the Excise Tax Act equal to the greater of C$250 and 5% of the net tax understated; gross negligence penalties under subsection 285.1(1) may reach 50% of the understated amount. Knowingly providing false information or issuing a false invoice can subject the LP, its general partner, and its directors/officers to criminal prosecution and uncapped administrative penalties. For Canadian-source royalties mischaracterized as exempt from Part XIII withholding, the LP may also be assessed under subsection 227(9) of the Income Tax Act for the gross royalty amount not withheld, plus interest and penalties, without regard to any tax subsequently paid by the non-resident recipient.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for both current and capital account transactions, consistent with Canada's IMF Article VIII obligations. Royalty, dividend, interest, and licensing remittances to or from non-residents are not subject to exchange control or prior currency approval. Profit remittance is freely permitted at the currency level; the only substantive hurdles are Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (25% statutory, often reduced by treaty or statute), transfer-pricing documentation, and any Investment Canada Act national-security or notifiable-acquisition review if the arrangement includes acquisition of a Canadian business.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory repatriation timeline, and no conversion ratio. Export receipts and royalty/patent-licensing proceeds may be retained indefinitely in foreign-currency accounts at Canadian chartered banks. FINTRAC large-transaction reporting above CAD 10,000 and cross-border electronic funds-transfer reporting apply, but these are informational/AML obligations, not exchange-control conversion mandates.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (central bank); foreign-exchange policy and capital-flow administration are managed by the Department of Finance Canada, with AML/CFT supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). No dedicated foreign-exchange control agency exists.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low-to-Medium. Canada does not have an EU-style EMI licence. Foreign digital banks and payment service providers may operate only as money-services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC and, in some cases, regulated by provincial consumer-protection and remittance rules. Such platforms are feasible for small royalty collections and low-value disbursements but are not viable for capital-escrow accounts, high-value SWIFT settlement, or core treasury clearing because they lack access to the Lynx high-value payment system and are not federally regulated deposit-taking institutions under the Bank Act.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and does not pay corporate income tax at entity level. Income is allocated to partners. For a taxable Canadian corporation carrying on ordinary business in Alberta, the combined general federal and Alberta corporate income tax rate is 23%: 15% federal plus 8% Alberta. The combined small-business rate is generally 11%: 9% federal plus 2% Alberta, subject to eligibility and business-limit rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The statutory Canadian Part XIII withholding rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is 25%. Under an applicable income tax treaty, the rate is commonly reduced to 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting the ownership threshold, and to 15% for other qualifying beneficial owners. A limited partnership distribution is not automatically a dividend; its characterization depends on the legal payer, the partnership's tax classification, the underlying income, and applicable treaty provisions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For Canadian transfer-pricing purposes, contemporaneous documentation is required under Income Tax Act section 247 where non-arm's-length cross-border transactions are relevant, subject to statutory exceptions. Form T106 reporting generally applies where the total reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000 in the reporting period, subject to applicable rules. Canada does not use a universal filing threshold that automatically requires an OECD-style Local File and Master File for every taxpayer. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational enterprise groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a general standalone Economic Substance Regulation regime equivalent to the regimes in certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is nevertheless material under section 245 of the Income Tax Act general anti-avoidance rule, transfer-pricing rules, treaty beneficial-ownership and anti-conduit principles, principal-purpose or limitation-on-benefits provisions, partnership allocation rules, and judicial doctrines concerning sham transactions and abusive avoidance. An IP holding partnership should maintain evidence of genuine commercial functions, decision-making, personnel or outsourced capability, control over economically significant risks, development and enhancement activities, valuation support, licensing administration, enforcement decisions, and arm's-length contractual conduct. Mere legal title, pass-through invoicing, or circular funding is insufficient substance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act section 239, conviction can result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to two years, or both, in addition to reassessed tax, interest, and civil penalties. False statements or omissions may also attract gross-negligence penalties generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, officers, and persons who directed or participated in the conduct may face prosecution and personal criminal exposure. Civil liability may arise for restitution, fines, reassessed source deductions, and derivative claims. Canada has no automatic general tax-related travel ban for directors, but courts may impose release conditions or enforcement measures in specific criminal proceedings.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust income under section 247, deny or reduce deductions, recharacterize amounts, and impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions for the penalty are met and reasonable efforts were not made. Interest applies to resulting tax balances.

Director personal liability: The primary assessment is generally against the taxpayer or partnership members, but directors and managing persons may face civil claims, gross-negligence penalties where personally involved, and criminal exposure if the conduct was knowingly fraudulent. The corporate veil is not automatically pierced merely because transfer pricing is incorrect.
Improper Treaty Relief and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny reduced withholding, assess the unpaid Part XIII tax, impose arrears interest, apply penalties for failure to deduct or remit, and invoke treaty anti-abuse rules and the general anti-avoidance rule. The normal payer penalty for failure to deduct or remit source deductions can be 10% of the amount not deducted or remitted, increasing to 20% in prescribed repeat-failure circumstances.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible officers may become personally liable for unremitted source deductions under Income Tax Act section 227.1 where statutory conditions are met. Personal criminal liability requires proof of the relevant offence; treaty disagreement alone does not create imprisonment.
Unremitted Source Deductions

Statutory sanction: The payer remains liable for the amount that should have been withheld, together with prescribed interest and applicable penalties. Information-return failures can generate separate penalties. CRA may issue assessments and pursue collection against the payer.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions under section 227.1 after the statutory collection conditions are satisfied, subject to the due-diligence defence. Personal assets may be exposed; imprisonment is generally associated with a separate wilful offence rather than the assessment alone.
Partnership Filing and Allocation Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA may impose late-filing and information-slip penalties, reassess partnership income and partner allocations, deny unsupported losses or deductions, and charge interest. Deliberate misrepresentation may trigger gross-negligence penalties or prosecution.

Director personal liability: A limited partnership has general partners and limited partners rather than directors in the corporate sense. The general partner and persons controlling filing and allocation decisions may face contractual, fiduciary, tax, and criminal exposure. Limited liability does not protect a person who personally committed fraud or statutory wrongdoing.
Shell Company and Lack of Commercial Substance

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include denial of treaty benefits, transfer-pricing adjustments, GAAR reassessment, withholding at statutory rates, deduction denial, partnership recharacterization, beneficial-ownership challenges, and interest and penalties. A shell structure is not unlawful solely because it has limited personnel, but sham or abusive arrangements are high-risk.

Director personal liability: General partners, directors of corporate partners, and controlling persons may face civil claims, tax indemnity claims, director-liability exposure, and criminal prosecution if the structure is used for fraud, evasion, money laundering, or knowingly false filings. Mere low substance, without unlawful conduct, does not by itself establish veil piercing or imprisonment.
Anti-Money Laundering and Sanctions Compliance

Statutory sanction: Reporting entities subject to the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act may face FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, public naming, and criminal prosecution. Criminal money-laundering and terrorist-financing offences can carry substantial fines and imprisonment, including imprisonment for up to 10 years or life for the most serious offences. Sanctions breaches may attract criminal and administrative consequences under Canadian sanctions legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and compliance personnel may be personally prosecuted where they authorized, participated in, or knowingly facilitated misconduct. Corporate status does not shield personal criminal conduct. Asset seizure, forfeiture, prohibition orders, and imprisonment may apply in appropriate cases.
Unlicensed or Unauthorized IP Rights Activity

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include injunctions, damages, accounting of profits, contractual termination, invalid sublicenses, administrative costs, and tax reassessments where royalty deductions or withholding classifications depend on invalid rights. Canadian federal IP statutes and provincial commercial laws may provide additional remedies.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may face personal liability for knowing infringement, fraudulent representations, breach of fiduciary duty, or misuse of partnership property. Ordinary corporate or partnership liability protection generally remains available for good-faith commercial errors.
Books, Records, and Document Retention Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA may impose record-keeping and information penalties, extend audit exposure where records are inadequate, deny deductions or treaty reductions, and draw adverse evidentiary inferences. Records must generally be retained for at least six years from the end of the relevant tax year, subject to longer periods for unresolved objections, appeals, or capital property.

Director personal liability: Responsible officers and general partners may face compliance orders, civil claims, and prosecution where records were deliberately destroyed or falsified. Imprisonment requires proof of a statutory criminal offence and is not an automatic consequence of ordinary retention failures.
