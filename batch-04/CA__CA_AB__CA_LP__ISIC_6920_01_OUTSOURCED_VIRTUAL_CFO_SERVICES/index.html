Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box election. A Canadian LP is statutorily fiscally transparent under ITA s. 96 for income tax; for GST/HST, the Excise Tax Act treats the partnership as a distinct 'person' (s. 123(1)(a), s. 240). Available elections include: ETA s. 156 (election to deem a taxable supply between closely related corporations), ETA s. 211 (election for associates), and ETA s. 273 (election for qualifying partnerships to treat certain financial services as taxable). ITA hybrid mismatch rules (ss. 18.4–18.6 and related provisions) deny deductions and apply adjustments to hybrid instruments and entities, but do not permit the LP to elect out of pass-through status.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — small supplier threshold measured on worldwide taxable supplies in a single calendar quarter or over the last four consecutive calendar quarters; exceeding CAD 30,000 in any such period makes GST/HST registration mandatory; voluntary registration is available below the threshold

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7, a supply of a service is zero-rated where: (i) the service is supplied to a non-resident person; (ii) the non-resident is not registered under the GST/HST system; and (iii) the service is not in respect of real property situated in Canada, not in respect of tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time the service is performed, and not otherwise treated as being supplied in Canada for a non-resident. Fractional CFO advisory and multicurrency ledger bookkeeping rendered remotely to foreign clients through Stripe Billing and Wise qualify as zero-rated exported services provided the client is a non-resident non-registrant and the services do not pertain to Canadian situs property. If the non-resident client is GST/HST registered, or the services relate to Canadian real or tangible property, GST at 5% must be charged instead.

  • Service contract / client engagement letter identifying the non-resident recipient and the scope of advisory and bookkeeping services
  • Invoice bearing the statement 'Zero-rated export — Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7' and the CRA Business Number
  • Non-resident status evidence (foreign certificate of incorporation, business license, registered office address in a foreign jurisdiction, or comparable documentation for individual clients)
  • Foreign-currency remittance ledger from Stripe Billing / Wise showing transaction IDs, payout dates, gross amounts, and applied foreign-exchange rates
  • Customs Export Declaration (required only for any incidental physical goods; not applicable to pure virtual CFO services)

Canada does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping regime for GST/HST. CRA mandates electronic (NETFILE) GST/HST filing for registrants with annual taxable supplies above CAD 1.5 million and requires machine-readable books and records upon audit. Pan-Canadian e-invoicing operates on the PEPPOL/BAS standard as a voluntary framework; Alberta has not enacted mandatory e-invoicing or clearance requirements. Payment processing via Stripe Billing and Wise does not trigger GST/HST clearance obligations.

CRA may reassess a zero-rating claim within the normal four-year reassessment period under ETA s. 298, and there is no limitation period where fraud or misrepresentation is shown. A false zero-rating or rebate claim attracts a penalty of 25% of the tax at issue under ETA s. 285, together with arrears interest under ETA s. 280; a gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understated income may also apply under ITA s. 163(2). Criminal tax evasion under ETA s. 327 carries fines of up to 200% of the GST evaded and/or imprisonment. Although no statutory GST/HST FX repatriation deadline exists, the 180-day mark is an internal compliance benchmark; if foreign-currency proceeds remain offshore without business purpose, CRA may challenge the arrangement under ITA s. 247 (transfer pricing) and general anti-avoidance rules, and the zero-rating documentation must remain contemporaneous and verifiable to withstand audit scrutiny.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully and freely convertible on both current account (cross-border sale of virtual CFO, bookkeeping, and advisory services; partnership distributions) and capital account (capital contributions, portfolio and direct investment). Canada has accepted IMF Article VIII obligations and imposes no approval queues, conversion ratios, surrender mandates, or balance-of-payment restrictions. Profit remittance for a non-resident limited partner of an Alberta LP is unrestricted from an exchange-control standpoint, but is subject to tax compliance: the general partner must apply Part XIII withholding tax of 25% on amounts constituting Canadian-source income of the non-resident partner unless reduced by treaty (e.g., 15% or 5% under the Canada-US Treaty, as applicable, or 0% for business profits attributable to a Canadian permanent establishment only where no share of LP income is separately taxable); distributions of capital are non-taxable and remittable without withholding. Practical hurdles are banking KYC, FINTRAC EFT reporting, CRS/FATCA self-certification, and the LP's obligation to keep books in CAD under subsection 261(2) of the Income Tax Act because an elective functional currency is generally unavailable for a pass-through limited partnership.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender, conversion, or repatriation obligation on export receipts or foreign-currency earnings; there is no timeline and no mandatory conversion ratio. The virtual-CFO business may retain USD, EUR, and GBP receivables in foreign-currency accounts and convert or deploy them freely. The applicable compliance rails are: (i) FINTRAC large-EFT reporting for every electronic funds transfer of CAD 10,000 or more in a 24-hour period; (ii) FINTRAC cross-border currency declaration for physical currency movements of CAD 10,000 or more; (iii) functional-currency record-keeping under subsection 261(2) of the Income Tax Act, which requires the LP's Canadian books and records to be maintained in CAD while permitting a parallel management ledger in USD; (iv) transfer-pricing documentation under section 247 for any cross-border payment to a related foreign service provider; and (v) no CBSA export declaration applies to pure services, but if the LP later exports embedded software or digital IP, Canada's goods-export reporting regime under the Customs Act would be triggered.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), as the central monetary authority and operator of the Lynx high-value real-time gross settlement system, together with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as prudential regulator of Schedule I banks, and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as the financial-intelligence unit administering the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Canada operates a liberalised exchange-control framework under the Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52), the Bank of Canada Act, and the Payment Clearing and Settlement Act; there is no dedicated foreign-exchange administration agency analogous to SAFE in China, and no discretionary exchange-licensing regime. The federal Minister of Finance retains residual policy authority over international financial relations; Alberta-specific prudential and banking matters have no provincial dimension except the existence of ATB Financial as a provincial Crown entity and the Alberta Partnership Act / Limited Partnership Act (RSA 2000, c. L-15) governing the LP treasury functions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM-HIGH only as a supplementary rail, not as the primary bank. Wise (Wise Canada, Inc.) is registered with FINTRAC as a money services business (MSB), holds e-money licences in the EU/UK, and can legally hod multi-currency wallet balances and execute cross-border payments for the Alberta LP; however, it is not a Schedule I deposit-taking institution, does not participate directly in the CPA/Lynx CAD clearing system, and accounts held with it are not CDIC-insured. Stripe Billing is viable exclusively as a merchant-collection rail for fractional-CFO subscription fees from Canadian, US, and EU clients; Stripe Payments Canada, Ltd. is a FINTRAC-registered MSB operating under a sponsor-bank arrangement. Legal constraints: (1) any EMI/MSB must be FINTRAC-registered before onboarding the LP; (2) the general partner must maintain the LP's primary operational account, CRA tax-remittance direct-debit capability, and payroll clearing account at a Schedule I bank because government remittances and payroll direct-deposit rails are not available through Wise or Stripe; (3) the bank's own AML policy may require the LP to disclose and justify the use of EMI accounts to avoid perceived 'warehousing' of funds; and (4) prudent treasury procedure is to keep EMI balances minimal and sweep settlement funds to the Schedule I bank within 24 to 48 hours.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant protocol: non-resident limited partners transmit SWIFT MT103 wires to the LP's operational account at a Schedule I Canadian bank, with the beneficiary legal name exactly matching the Alberta LP registration and a field-70 narrative stating 'Capital contribution - Alberta Limited Partnership' to prevent recharacterisation as income. Because Canada does not maintain a statutory purpose-code regime, the narrative, the correspondent routing (e.g., RBC's US correspondent RBC Bank (Georgia), N.A.; BMO's US branch BMO Harris N.A.; TD's TD Bank, N.A.), and the sender's CRS/FATCA self-certification are the only verification anchors. The GP must: (1) confirm with the bank whether the wire triggers FINTRAC large-EFT reporting at the CAD 10,000 threshold; (2) record the contribution in the LP capital-account ledger with a dated partner receipt; (3) ensure the sender's bank performs its own source-of-funds verification to avoid a correspondent-bank recall or sanctions-flag; (4) obtain from each contributing partner the relevant tax self-certification before wiring (US W-9 or W-8BEN, Canadian NR-series, or EU CRS form); and (5) rely on SWIFT MT103 field 33B for currency and field 72 for regulatory codes. Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

0% at the Alberta limited partnership level where the partnership is fiscally transparent and does not elect or qualify as a corporation; taxable income is allocated to partners. Canadian-controlled private corporation partners may generally face combined federal and Alberta rates of 11% on eligible active business income within the small-business limit and 23% at the general corporate rate, subject to applicable conditions.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding applies to taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. A partnership does not pay dividends in the corporate-law sense. LP distributions are analyzed as allocations or distributions of partnership property and may involve withholding under other provisions rather than dividend withholding.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For Canadian transfer-pricing documentation, contemporaneous documentation is generally required where a Canadian taxpayer has transactions with non-arm's-length non-residents. Canada does not impose a universal CAD threshold for preparation of a Local File. The T106 reporting threshold is generally more than CAD 1 million of aggregate reportable non-arm's-length transactions. A corporation with gross revenues of at least CAD 10 million and significant cross-border related-party transactions may be required to provide documentation on demand under the detailed transfer-pricing rules. Canada generally has no separate statutory Master File requirement; Country-by-Country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million, or the applicable Canadian equivalent.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone economic-substance regulation equivalent to the UAE ESR. Substance is enforced through the general anti-avoidance rule, sham and beneficial-ownership doctrines, treaty anti-abuse provisions, principal-purpose or similar treaty rules where applicable, transfer-pricing rules, corporate-residence principles, and factual determination of where services, personnel, management, risk control, and decision-making actually occur. A virtual CFO LP should maintain real Canadian business records, client contracts, workpapers, time records, decision authority evidence, banking records, and commercially supportable intercompany pricing.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties generally equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, and criminal conviction under the Income Tax Act. Criminal tax evasion may attract a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to 2 years on summary conviction or up to 5 years on indictment.

Director personal liability: General partners, managing partners, officers, and directors may face criminal prosecution where they authorized, acquiesced in, or participated in the conduct. The corporate veil is not automatically pierced, but personal liability may arise for direct wrongdoing, statutory director liability, trust amounts, source deductions, GST/HST, obstruction, proceeds-of-crime offences, and restitution orders. Courts may impose probation, asset restraint, restitution, and imprisonment; travel restrictions may arise through bail, enforcement, or immigration processes but are not an automatic tax penalty.
Failure to Withhold, Remit, or File Information Returns

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for unremitted amounts, interest, late-remittance penalties, and information-return penalties. Directors may become jointly and severally liable for certain unremitted source deductions and GST/HST under applicable statutory rules.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible officers may incur personal statutory liability where they failed to exercise due diligence. Liability is not eliminated by insolvency of the LP or by delegating bookkeeping to an external accountant.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: CRA may make a transfer-pricing adjustment, impose a 10% transfer-pricing penalty on the adjusted amount where statutory conditions are met, deny deductions, recharacterize payments, apply withholding tax, and impose interest and foreign-reporting penalties.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors, general partners, and officers may face personal exposure for deliberate misrepresentation, tax-evasion participation, or statutory remittance failures. Mere transfer-pricing disagreement normally creates entity-level tax exposure rather than automatic personal liability.
Shell Company, Sham, or Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty benefits, recharacterize income, apply the General Anti-Avoidance Rule, assess withholding and income tax, impose penalties and interest, and challenge the existence or commercial substance of the arrangement.

Director personal liability: General partners and controlling persons may face civil restitution, director or officer liability for statutory amounts, and criminal exposure if the structure was used knowingly for fraud, evasion, money laundering, or proceeds concealment. Corporate separateness does not protect individuals from their own fraudulent or criminal conduct.
AML, Proceeds of Crime, and Suspicious Payment Activity

Statutory sanction: Potential FINTRAC compliance violations, administrative monetary penalties, transaction restrictions, reporting failures, account closure, seizure, and criminal prosecution under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and Criminal Code. Professional-accounting or financial-service obligations may apply depending on the services actually provided.

Director personal liability: Individuals may face criminal fines, forfeiture, restitution, and imprisonment for laundering or knowingly dealing with proceeds of crime. Travel restrictions may arise from arrest, bail, forfeiture, or court orders; they are fact-dependent and not automatic.
Unauthorized Practice or Misrepresentation of Professional Services

Statutory sanction: Provincial professional-regulatory sanctions, injunctions, administrative penalties, civil damages, contract disputes, and possible offence proceedings may apply. Bookkeeping and management consulting are distinct from regulated public accounting, assurance, insolvency, legal, and investment-advisory activities.

Director personal liability: Individuals who personally make unauthorized representations or provide regulated services may be sued or prosecuted directly. Directors and partners may also face restitution, regulatory discipline, and loss of professional status where applicable.
GST/HST and Source-Destination Misclassification

Statutory sanction: Assessment of unremitted GST/HST, denied input tax credits, interest, late-filing and gross-negligence penalties, and potential director liability for collected but unremitted amounts.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible partners may be personally liable for statutory trust amounts and deliberate false claims. Personal criminal liability may arise for fraud or evasion.
Books, Records, and Beneficial-Ownership Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Document-production orders, denied deductions, penalties, reassessments, extended assessment periods for misrepresentation or fraud, and provincial corporate-registry or AML sanctions where applicable.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, and officers may be ordered to produce records and may face obstruction or contempt consequences. Personal criminal exposure can arise where records are destroyed, falsified, or concealed.
