Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency / Agence du revenu du Canada (CRA-ARC). Tax registration portals: Business Registration Online (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/registering-your-business/register.html) and My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/e-services-businesses.html). Alberta corporate income tax is administered by the CRA under the Federal-Provincial Tax Collection Agreement; the Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration (https://www.alberta.ca/tax-and-revenue-administration) separately collects provincial levies such as fuel tax, tobacco tax and the carbon levy.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Register the Limited Partnership with the Alberta Corporate Registry under the Partnership Act (Alberta); no minimum capital is required and non-resident ownership is permitted. Step 2: Submit Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number' or use Business Registration Online to obtain the 9-digit CRA Business Number (BN), which is the national statutory tax identifier. Step 3: Open the GST/HST (RT) account for indirect tax and, if goods will be exported, the RM import/export account. Step 4: Non-resident general and limited partners must provide certified copies of foreign registration certificates, registered office address, and foreign tax identification numbers; CRA requires a Canadian agent or representative for service of notices where no Canadian address exists. The Alberta LP registers with the Alberta Corporate Registry first, and CRA will not issue the BN before the provincial registry number is obtained.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible - A Canadian Limited Partnership created in Alberta is fiscally transparent for income tax purposes under section 96 of the Income Tax Act: the LP does not pay income tax, and each partner includes its allocated share of income and loss in its own tax return. The LP is nonetheless a 'person' and a registrant for GST/HST purposes, files its own GST/HST returns, and must file a T5013 Partnership Information Return with CRA. The limited partners enjoy limited liability, while the general partner (including a non-resident general partner) has unlimited liability and may create a Canadian permanent establishment. Alberta has no separate tax attribution because CRA administers provincial corporate tax on the same federal taxable income basis.

Available election forms

Canada has no U.S.-style Section 7701 check-the-box election; classification follows the Income Tax Act and the Alberta Partnership Act. Available elections relevant to the LP include: (i) the CRA administrative check-the-box policy (Income Tax Folio S4-F6-C2) under which a U.S. LLC partner may be recognized as flow-through or opaque by reference to its U.S. federal tax election; (ii) GST/HST voluntary registration and effective-date elections under section 240(2) of the Excise Tax Act; (iii) the nil-consideration election for closely related persons under section 273 ETA; (iv) the Quick Method election and change-of-use election under sections 225 and 225.1 ETA for qualifying registrants; and (v) for cross-border alignment, the 'specified similar partnership' rule in subsection 96(2) ITA may be used to match Canadian and foreign tax results. Hybrid mismatch and excessive-interest-and-financing (EIF) anti-avoidance rules apply automatically and provide no elective protection.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (C$30,000) - A person carrying on commercial activity in Canada, including an Alberta LP with non-resident partners, ceases to be a small supplier and must register for GST/HST once the total worldwide taxable supplies of the person and all associated persons exceed C$30,000 in a single calendar quarter or C$30,000 over four consecutive calendar quarters. Registration is voluntary below this threshold. A non-resident LP that merely holds passive investment interests without carrying on commercial activity in Canada is not required to register.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating of cross-border trade-policy advisory and government-affairs lobbying services (ISIC 7020 / NAICS 541820) is available under section 22 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act when the recipient is a non-resident person and: (i) the service is not in connection with real or tangible personal property situated in Canada; (ii) the recipient is not a GST/HST registrant and does not acquire the service for consumption, use or supply in Canada; and (iii) the service is not rendered to an individual present in Canada. The Alberta LP, as a GST registrant, reports such export services at 0% on its GST/HST return and may claim input tax credits on related overhead costs, provided it retains contemporaneous evidence of the foreign status of the client and of performance outside Canada. Goods exported from Alberta are zero-rated where export occurs within 60 days of sale and is supported by customs documentation. Canada imposes no statutory FX repatriation deadline, but a 180-day repatriation benchmark is the expected documentary standard; any longer deferral must be commercially justified and evidenced by payment terms in the client engagement.

  • CBSA Customs Export Declaration (Form A6) or approved alternative (e.g., G13 Streamlined Export Reporting for non-controlled goods) evidencing legal movement of goods out of Canada, where applicable
  • Original Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / marine or rail manifest bearing the LP Business Number and the overseas consignee details
  • Commercial invoice and signed engagement letter for the Government Affairs Lobbying services specifying the foreign client, scope, fee, currency and payment milestones

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or pre-clearance API mandate exists under Canadian GST law. GST/HST invoices may be issued in paper or any electronic format (PDF, EDI, PEPPOL) provided they contain the prescribed particulars: supplier Business Number, recipient name, date of supply, description of the supply, and tax amount. Mandatory electronic filing of GST/HST returns (NETFILE) applies where a registrant's total revenue exceeds C$1.5 million, and all remittances over C$10,000 must be made electronically through My Payment or participating financial institutions. E-invoicing under the Pan-Canadian Trust Framework and PEPPOL CIUS is voluntary; CRA may require digital audit files (e.g., Excel, ERP extracts) during an audit, but no statutory electronic invoice clearance system is in effect.

CRA may reassess within four years of the normal reassessment date, extended to seven years where negligence, carelessness or wilful default occurred, and without time limit in cases of fraud. If zero-rating is denied, the supply becomes taxable at 5% and CRA assesses the unpaid GST together with compounding prescribed arrears interest. Penalties include: (i) a failure-to-register or failure-to-remit penalty of the greater of C$250 and 3% of the unremitted net tax (ETA s. 280); (ii) a false-statement or false-omission penalty of the greater of C$100 and 30% of the tax sought to be evaded (ETA s. 285); and (iii) criminal prosecution under ETA s. 327, which carries substantial fines and imprisonment on conviction on indictment. Where export documentation is contrived or repatriation exceeds the 180-day benchmark without commercial justification, CRA may apply transfer-pricing penalties of 10% of the net adjustment under ITA s. 247(3), invoke the general anti-avoidance rule under ITA s. 245 with an additional 25% penalty, and refer the file to CRA's Offshore Compliance Division. Previously paid refunds are retroactively clawed back for all open assessment years, and the LP's entitlement to future zero-rating treatment may be suspended pending further audit.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has full current and capital account convertibility under Canadian law. There is no exchange-control regime, no foreign-investment screening for passive trade-policy and government-affairs advisory receipts, and no requirement for central-bank authorization to remit profits or distributions to non-resident partners. However, financial institutions must apply anti-money-laundering and know-your-client filters to all cross-border wires, and distributions to non-residents are subject to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (Canada) where applicable, which is a tax-remittance obligation, not a currency-convertibility restriction.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory export-proceeds surrender rules in Canada. Exporters and service providers are free to retain export and cross-border service receipts in any convertible currency, with no mandatory conversion timeline and no minimum conversion ratio. Firms must nevertheless maintain accurate accounting records for CRA tax reporting and FINTRAC wire-transaction reporting thresholds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). Canada maintains no dedicated foreign-exchange-control regulator; OSFI supervises deposit-taking institutions, FINTRAC administers AML/CFT, and the Bank of Canada acts as monetary and financial-stability authority. The Minister of Finance retains residual powers under the Currency Act and payment-system statutes.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low. Canada does not issue 'EMI' licenses as a standalone regulated category. Cross-border digital payment firms require registration as money service businesses with FINTRAC, but only federally incorporated Schedule I banks or provincial-licensed credit unions can offer full deposit-taking operating-account services. Offshore authorized EMI platforms cannot provide CAD operating accounts compliant for an Alberta limited partnership without either partnering with a Canadian bank or obtaining an MSB registration and adhering to FINTRAC reporting/receiving restrictions.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound routing for Alberta LP capital contributions uses SWIFT MT103 wires from the non-resident partner's bank to the LP's Tier-1 operational account in Canada. The wire must identify the LP as beneficiary, state the remitter as the partner in the same legal name as in the partnership agreement, and include a transmission reference describing the payment as 'capital contribution' or 'partner contribution'. Since Canada has no statutory FX purpose-code system, Canadian banks rely on their own remittance-purpose fields and SWIFT 'supplementary details'; these fields must be completed truthfully. Supporting corporate documents, partner-name matching, and sanctions/PEP screening are required before credit is made available. For subscriptions above CAD 10,000, banks will require an executed subscription agreement, source of wealth/source of funds documentation, and final beneficial-owner schedules.Outbound trade and advisory disbursements from an Alberta LP are settled through SWIFT MT103/MT202 messages for cross-border wire payments, Canadian-dollar EFT through the national Lynx high-value clearing system for domestic CAD payments, and USD payments through U.S. correspondent rails (Fedwire/CHIPS) paired with Canadian USD-clearing accounts. Commercial letters of credit are recommended for trade-related payments to vendors where documentary assurance is required; these are issued by the Tier-1 bank under UCP600. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No monetary threshold; applies to all related-party transactions with non-arm's length parties

Economic substance scope: No specific Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) in Canada; substance requirements enforced through general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) and business purpose doctrine

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction with fines up to $100,000 and/or imprisonment up to 2 years; civil penalties for gross negligence

Director personal liability: Directors and partners personally liable for unpaid taxes; potential criminal charges, restitution orders, and bans on leaving Canada
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of income, penalties up to 50% of tax due, and possible criminal prosecution under GAAR

Director personal liability: Piercing of corporate veil; personal liability for taxes, fines, and potential imprisonment for willful evasion
AML Violations

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to $10,000 per violation; possible license suspension or revocation

Director personal liability: Personal fines, civil suits, and criminal liability for willful AML breaches; travel bans possible
Late Filing and Underpayment

Statutory sanction: Interest on unpaid taxes (prime rate + 3-5%), penalties of 5-10% of tax due, and possible garnishment

Director personal liability: Joint and several liability for partners; personal payment demands, liens on assets, and potential criminal prosecution
