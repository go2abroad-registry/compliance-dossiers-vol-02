Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No statutory minimum paid-up capital is prescribed for a limited partnership in Alberta; a limited partner must make a contribution (which may be nominal, e.g., USD/CAD 1) as set out in the partnership agreement, and no escrow or blocked-account requirement applies under the Limited Partnership Act.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety: office premises must hold a Fire Safety Inspection certificate under the Alberta Fire Code (Safety Codes Act, RSA 2000, c S-1) where required by the landlord or municipality; structural load inspections are not applicable for a leasehold office. Occupational safety: maintain an OHS Program under the Occupational Health and Safety Act, RSA 2000, c O-2, including first aid and workplace hazard assessment, and obtain WCB-Alberta workers' compensation coverage if employees are hired.

Paid-in capital rule: No statutory minimum paid-up capital is prescribed for a limited partnership in Alberta; a limited partner must make a contribution (which may be nominal, e.g., USD/CAD 1) as set out in the partnership agreement, and no escrow or blocked-account requirement applies under the Limited Partnership Act.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a United States-style check-the-box election for an Alberta limited partnership. A Canadian partnership is generally fiscally transparent: income, loss, deductions, and credits are computed at partnership level and allocated to partners under the Income Tax Act, with partners generally taxed on their allocated shares. A partnership may be required to file a T5013 partnership information return, and a non-resident partner may have Canadian filing, withholding, treaty, and information-reporting obligations. Available elections are transaction-specific rather than entity-classification elections, including eligible partnership fiscal-period elections, GST/HST small-supplier or voluntary-registration choices, GST/HST elections for specified supplies or organizational structures where statutory conditions are met, and treaty-based relief claims. Anti-hybrid and foreign-affiliate rules may deny or recharacterize benefits where foreign tax treatment produces a hybrid mismatch; no general hybrid-mismatch election converts the partnership into a corporation.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of taxable supplies, generally measured on a worldwide basis for the supplier and associated persons, excluding zero-rated supplies from the calculation of tax charged but generally including their value for threshold purposes. The threshold is tested over a single calendar quarter and the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters under the small-supplier rules. Registration becomes effective under the applicable statutory timing rules when the threshold is exceeded, with voluntary registration generally available earlier.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada may generally be zero-rated where the statutory export conditions are satisfied, including delivery or transfer of possession outside Canada and retention of evidence establishing export. Commercial exports commonly require customs export reporting, transport records, commercial invoices, proof of delivery, and purchaser or destination information. Services supplied to a non-resident may be zero-rated only if the specific Exported Services provisions apply. For market-research, consumer-panel, SaaS, and platform services, the supplier must assess the customer's residence, the actual recipient, place of performance, and whether the service is directly related to Canadian real property, tangible personal property, an event in Canada, or another exclusion. Services performed in Canada are not automatically zero-rated merely because the customer pays from abroad. Certain advisory, research, data-processing, and electronically supplied services may qualify when supplied to a non-resident and not excluded by the legislation. Documentary support should include customer residency and beneficial-recipient evidence, contract and statement of work, deliverables, service-location analysis, payment records, and evidence that no excluded Canadian connection applies.

  • Canadian export declaration or applicable Canada Border Services Agency export-reporting record for reportable goods
  • Commercial invoice identifying the supplier, purchaser, goods, value, destination, and delivery terms
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, courier record, or equivalent transport document
  • Proof of delivery or importation in the foreign jurisdiction, where available
  • Purchase order, contract, customer-residency evidence, and payment records
  • For exported services, contract, statements of work, deliverables, recipient records, and evidence supporting the statutory exported-service category
  • Inward remittance or foreign-exchange evidence where relevant to commercial substantiation; Canada generally does not impose a universal inward-remittance-certificate requirement for GST zero-rating

Canada has no general real-time GST invoice-clearance, fiscal-stamping, or mandatory nationwide e-invoicing mandate for ordinary business-to-business supplies. GST invoices must contain prescribed information when the customer requires documentation to support an input tax credit, including supplier identity and GST registration number, invoice date, description, consideration, and tax amount or applicable rate. Electronic invoices and electronic books are generally acceptable if reliable, readable, accessible, and retained for the statutory record-retention period. CRA electronic filing and payment requirements may apply to registered businesses, and certain large corporations may be required to file electronically. E-invoicing, payment-platform reporting, and marketplace rules do not replace GST registration, collection, invoicing, or record-retention obligations.

Canada has no general statutory requirement to repatriate export proceeds within 180 days as a condition of GST zero-rating. The CRA may reassess tax where export or exported-service conditions are not proven, treating the supply as taxable at the applicable rate, recovering incorrectly claimed input tax credits or GST/HST rebates, and charging arrears interest. Penalties may include failure-to-file, failure-to-remit, gross-negligence penalties generally equal to the greater of CAD 500 and 25% of the understated tax or overstated credit, and penalties for false statements or omissions. Tax evasion or fraudulent documentation can lead to civil penalties, prosecution, fines, and imprisonment. A registrant must retain supporting records, generally for six years from the end of the relevant year, and must repay or correct amounts obtained through an invalid zero-rating or rebate claim.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible under the Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52) and no central bank licence or prior approval is required for capital-account or current-account transactions, profit distributions, or repatriations by limited partners of an Alberta limited partnership. Residual operational hurdles comprise: (i) Part XIII non-resident withholding tax at 25% (treaty-reducible) levied only on specifically enumerated payments such as royalties, interest, management fees and certain deemed distributions; capital contributions and returns of capital are not chargeable; (ii) allocation of Canadian-source active business income to non-resident partners under Income Tax Act sections 96 and 102, which may require each non-resident corporate partner to file a Canadian Part I return or claim treaty protection; (iii) FINTRAC electronic funds transfer (EFT) reporting for any cross-border remittance equivalent to CAD 10,000 or more aggregated over any 24-hour period; and (iv) blocking sanctions imposed under the Special Economic Measures Act. Profit remittance is otherwise unblocked; the practical structuring objective is to obtain treaty benefits (for example under Article VII of the 1980 Canada-United States Income Tax Convention as amended by the 2007 Protocol) and to avoid a hidden permanent establishment for foreign limited partners.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion ratio for export or service-export earnings, including cross-border market research fees; the applicable conversion ratio is zero and there is no statutory repatriation timeline. A digital platform under ISIC 7320 is not required to convert CAD receipts or foreign-currency sweep balances, and may retain foreign currency indefinitely. Mandatory compliance steps are limited to: (i) CBSA trade documentation only when goods are physically exported (generally not applicable to digital market-research services); (ii) FINTRAC EFT reporting for wire transfers of CAD 10,000 or more per 24-hour window; (iii) sanctions and consolidated-list screening; and (iv) presentation of the partnership's T5013 information return and financial statements measured in Canadian dollars under IFRS/IAS 21.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); Canada maintains no single SAFE-style foreign exchange administration. Oversight is functionally distributed among the Bank of Canada, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA), and Global Affairs Canada for sanctions administration under the Special Economic Measures Act and the United Nations Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

PayPal Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-High as a payout and mass-disbursement rail; Low as the principal treasury and custody account for the CA_LP. Canada does not issue a discrete electronic-money-institution licence; e-money issuance, stored-value balances, and payment initiation are regulated under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) administered by the Bank of Canada, which requires registration as a payment service provider and participation consent, with a transitional onboarding period extending into late 2025. Wise, PayPal (Mass Payout), and Airwallex each hold FINTRAC money-services-business registrations and can lawfully execute the platform's mass micro-payouts through SEPA, UK Faster Payments, ACH, FedACH, PIX, and local instant clearing. Legal constraints are: (i) a foreign EMI without a Canadian MSB registration and RPAA registration cannot hold CAD balances or directly access Canadian clearing; (ii) if the CA_LP itself initiates or controls transfers of monetary value to third-party panelists, it may be deemed a money-services business under the PCMLTFA and would require FINTRAC registration, a compliance program, and CAD 10,000 EFT reporting; and (iii) the recommended architecture is a Schedule I bank as principal treasury account and settlement anchor, with Wise or PayPal engaged strictly as executing sub-processors so that the LP's activity remains an ordinary payment for survey services rather than independent money transmission.

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not subject to entity-level federal or Alberta corporate income tax. Taxable income is allocated to partners. For a taxable Canadian corporation, the general combined federal and Alberta rate is generally 23% (15% federal plus 8% Alberta); the combined small-business rate is generally 11% (9% federal plus 2% Alberta) on qualifying active business income within applicable limits.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The Canadian statutory withholding rate on dividends paid or credited to non-residents is 25%. A limited partnership generally does not pay dividends; distributions are analyzed according to the character of the underlying partnership income, capital, or other payment. Treaty reductions commonly apply to qualifying beneficial owners. Under the Canada-United States treaty, the dividend rate is generally 5% for qualifying corporate ownership and 15% in other cases, subject to treaty conditions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

The partnership information return and related T5013 slips are generally due within five months after the end of the partnership's fiscal period. A partnership fiscal period generally cannot exceed 12 months, subject to statutory rules.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada generally requires contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions under Income Tax Act section 247. Canada does not use a single OECD-style local-file/master-file threshold for all taxpayers. T106 reporting is generally relevant where the aggregate amount of reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 1,000,000 or another statutory reporting condition is met. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to multinational enterprise groups meeting the CAD 750,000,000 consolidated-revenue threshold.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no general standalone economic-substance regulation equivalent to the United Kingdom or certain offshore economic-substance regimes. Canadian anti-avoidance controls include the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership requirements, transfer-pricing rules, thin-capitalization rules, specified foreign-affiliate rules, principal-purpose or similar treaty limitations where applicable, and judicial substance-over-form analysis. A limited partnership should maintain commercially credible functions, personnel, decision-making, records, contractual allocation of risks, and bank-account controls. Artificial fee, royalty, financing, or profit-allocation arrangements may be reassessed even if formally documented.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act section 239, conviction may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to two years, or both. Reassessed tax, gross-negligence penalties, interest, and civil collection also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, authorized representatives, and persons who participated in the conduct may face criminal prosecution and civil assessment. A limited partner is generally protected from partnership liabilities only if it does not take prohibited control actions; the general partner remains exposed under applicable partnership law. Travel restrictions are not automatic, but criminal proceedings, bail conditions, passport surrender, and enforcement measures may restrict travel.
Failure to Remit Payroll, Withholding, or Trust Amounts

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unremitted amount, prescribed interest, remittance penalties, and potential criminal prosecution. Income Tax Act section 227.1 may impose personal liability on directors for unremitted source deductions, subject to statutory due-diligence defenses and limitation rules.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable with the entity for qualifying unremitted amounts. General partners and persons exercising effective control may face analogous exposure depending on the applicable statute and facts. Personal assets may be subject to collection action; imprisonment requires a separate criminal conviction.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust income under Income Tax Act section 247, deny or reduce deductions, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the applicable adjustment where statutory conditions are met, assess withholding tax, and apply interest.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may face civil exposure for knowing participation, misrepresentation, or breach of fiduciary or statutory duties. Personal criminal liability generally requires proof of the applicable offence and mental element.
Treaty Abuse and Improper Withholding Reduction

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits, reassessment of withholding at 25%, interest, penalties, anti-avoidance reassessment, and potential prosecution for false statements or tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors, general partners, and signatories may face assessments, penalties, and criminal exposure where they knowingly participated in false certification or concealment.
Shell Company, Nominee Ownership, and Economic-Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: Possible denial of deductions, transfer-pricing adjustment, GAAR application, treaty-benefit denial, reassessment of source and permanent-establishment income, and penalties for inaccurate reporting.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is not automatically pierced merely because an entity is thinly capitalized or tax-efficient. Courts may impose personal liability where there is fraud, sham conduct, oppression, statutory liability, knowing participation, or misuse of the entity. General partners normally bear the primary partnership liability.
AML, Sanctions, and Payment-Rail Misuse

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties may reach statutory maximums of up to CAD 1,000,000 for businesses, depending on the violation and compliance category. Proceeds-of-crime, terrorist-financing, sanctions, and money-laundering offences may carry substantial fines, forfeiture, and imprisonment, including imprisonment of up to 10 years for indictable money-laundering offences under applicable Criminal Code provisions.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, compliance personnel, and beneficial owners may face personal criminal liability for knowing participation, facilitation, laundering, or sanctions evasion. Accounts may be frozen, assets seized, and travel restricted through bail, forfeiture, or other lawful enforcement processes.
GST/HST and Digital-Service Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Assessment of tax, interest, late-filing and late-remittance penalties, denial of input tax credits, gross-negligence penalties, and possible prosecution for tax fraud.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability for certain unremitted trust amounts and may face criminal exposure for deliberate fraud or obstruction. Insolvency does not automatically extinguish statutory trust obligations.
Records, Books, and Obstruction

Statutory sanction: Record-keeping orders, reassessment based on available information, penalties for failure to provide information, prosecution, contempt-related consequences, and adverse evidentiary inferences.

Director personal liability: Responsible officers, directors, general partners, and agents may be personally prosecuted for obstruction, false evidence, or destruction of records. Civil liability may arise for resulting tax, penalties, costs, or losses.
