Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National: Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), acting for the Minister of National Revenue; official portal: CRA My Business Account and Business Registration Online (www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/e-services-businesses.html). Provincial complement for Alberta: Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration (ATRA; tax.alberta.ca) and Alberta Corporate Registry for the limited partnership's legal registration; Alberta does not administer any provincial retail sales tax.

E-government filing portal

1) Confirm that the Alberta Limited Partnership is registered or extra-provincially registered under Alberta's Partnership Act or corresponding registry, and obtain the Alberta registry identification number. 2) Submit Form RC1, 'Request for a Business Number', to the CRA through Business Registration Online, by fax, or by mail; identify the limited partnership itself as the registrant and include the general partner and, where requested, the partner list. 3) The CRA will issue a nine-digit Business Number and assign program accounts as needed: e.g., GST/HST account suffix RT0001, payroll account suffix RP0001, and import/export account suffix RM0001. 4) For a non-resident limited partnership, engage or appoint a Canadian representative/agent and provide governing partnership documentation, Alberta LP registration evidence, and non-resident identification for each foreign partner; the CRA non-resident taxation unit will coordinate the GST/HST registration if the partnership is not otherwise resident. 5) Once the BN is issued, the LP must file GST/HST returns electronically, maintain its business registry data, and update the CRA of any change in partners or address.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible: under the Income Tax Act (ITA) s.96, the Alberta Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent for Canadian federal income tax and for Alberta provincial income tax; the LP itself is not a taxpayer and pays no entity-level income tax. Instead, partnership income, losses, credits, and cash allocations flow through and are taxed to the partners, including non-resident partners who may be subject to Part XIII withholding tax or branch tax on allocated Canadian-source amounts. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible: under the Income Tax Act (ITA) s.96, the Alberta Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent for Canadian federal income tax and for Alberta provincial income tax; the LP itself is not a taxpayer and pays no entity-level income tax. Instead, partnership income, losses, credits, and cash allocations flow through and are taxed to the partners, including non-resident partners who may be subject to Part XIII withholding tax or branch tax on allocated Canadian-source amounts. For GST/HST purposes, however, the LP is treated as a separate 'person' and must register, charge, collect, and remit GST/HST in its own name when engaged in a commercial activity. Alberta has no separate corporate or capital tax classification for an LP.

Available election forms

No U.S.-style check-the-box election exists in Canada. The flow-through classification of an Alberta limited partnership is statutory and cannot be elected away. Partnerships may use procedural elections such as a coordinated fiscal period under ITA s.249.1, and partners may enter into allocation agreements permitted by the partnership agreement, subject to the reasonableness and specific-allocation rules of ITA s.96. Canada also applies hybrid-mismatch rules under the Income Tax Act, including provisions directed at hybrid entities and hybrid arrangements; these rules operate automatically rather than by election. No direct statutory election is available to convert the LP into an entity taxed at partnership level for Canadian income tax purposes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: an entity that is not a 'small supplier' must register for GST/HST. The small supplier threshold is measured by worldwide GST/HST-taxable supplies (including Canadian taxable supplies) over the last four consecutive calendar quarters; exceeding CAD 30,000 in the relevant look-back period triggers mandatory registration. Non-residents carrying on commercial activity in Canada are subject to the same threshold, but imported goods into Canada are subject to GST at the border regardless of small-supplier status.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

For goods: zero-rating applies only where tangible personal property is supplied to a non-resident and is exported from Canada in accordance with Schedule VI, Part V of the ETA. The supplier must prove movement of goods from Canada to a foreign jurisdiction, either by delivering outside Canada or by transferring the goods to a common carrier for export. For services: consulting services in the medical device regulatory field (FDA 510(k) filing and CE Mark compliance) supplied to a non-resident manufacturer or distributor can be zero-rated where the services are not performed in Canada for an individual, do not relate to Canadian-situated real property, and are not in respect of tangible personal property situated in Canada for which the non-resident is acquiring services in Canada; evidence should show that the recipient is a non-resident and that the deliverables were transmitted or performed outside Canada, e.g., electronically or by international courier. The mere receipt of wire payment by the LP from abroad is not sufficient; the substance of the supply must be an export. For regulatory documentation and licenses, intangible personal property supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated when title is transferred outside Canada.

  • CBSA export declaration filed through the Canadian Export Reporting System / CBSA CARM, including B13A-form export data, for any tangible medical-device sample, prototype, or commercial goods shipped from Canada; retain the export report number and any controlled-goods permit if applicable.
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, or courier transport record showing consignor in Canada, consignee outside Canada, and actual movement from Canada.
  • Commercial invoice and packing list describing the goods, quantities, HS classification, and foreign destination.
  • Inward remittance certificate or bank wire/fx conversion record from a Canadian financial institution showing payment received from the non-resident customer or by a foreign entity.
  • Where available, the foreign customs import entry or destination-country customs record evidencing arrival of goods outside Canada.
  • For regulatory consulting services, retain executed consulting agreement, non-resident status evidence, detailed operational report, electronic delivery log, FDA/CE submission records, and proof of payment within the export substantiation file.

Canada does not currently mandate real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls, digital stamping, or government API approval for invoices. Electronic invoicing is permitted and the CRA accepts legible PDF, EDI, and e-invoice formats provided the invoice contains the prescribed information under the Input Tax Credit Information Regulations and the Excise Tax Act. Pan-Canadian e-invoicing, including the use of PEPPOL-compliant interoperability, is being promoted on an optional/voluntary basis rather than through a legal fiscal fixture. Registrants must retain invoices and electronic records in an accessible, auditable format.

CRA export audits focus on zero-rated services and goods; if the exporter cannot produce contemporaneous evidencing of non-resident recipients, actual export, foreign delivery, or payment, the CRA may deny zero-rating, reassess the supply as taxable at 5% GST, disallow related input tax credits, and charge interest. Penalties may include civil penalties under the Excise Tax Act for failure to collect or report tax and for false or misleading statements. Deliberate fraudulent zero-rating can give rise to criminal prosecution for tax evasion, substantial monetary fines, retroactive clawback of refunds and input tax credits, and imprisonment. The normal CRA assessment period for GST/HST is four years, but the limitation period is suspended or extended where misrepresentation, fraud, or failure to file applies.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full capital and current account convertibility under Article VIII of the IMF Articles of Agreement. There are no exchange control approvals for capital injection, income repatriation, or profit distributions. A Canadian Limited Partnership may freely distribute profits to non-resident limited partners, subject only to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax and any applicable income tax treaty relief. The sole compliance hurdles are documentary: valid NR4 or T2LP filings, beneficial ownership disclosure, and bank source-of-wealth verification.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no obligation to bring export proceeds onshore. Receipts may be retained in any freely convertible currency. FINTRAC reporting applies only where a currency transaction or cross-border transfer exceeds CAD 10,000, and no exchange control permit is required.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); operational banking and AML/ATF supervision by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); currency transaction reporting to FINTRAC

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium for ancillary settlement, Low for primary treasury jurisdiction. Foreign offshore digital EMI and non-bank payment service providers must be registered as Money Service Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC and must comply with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act if they solicit Canadian clients. They do not possess a Canadian Bank Act banking license, cannot accept deposits as a Canadian bank, and cannot supply insured clearing or credit. An offshore EMI may be used only as a pass-through prepaid settlement channel, not as a substitute for a prudentially regulated Canadian clearing account.

Stage 06

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal Part I general rate 15.0%, Alberta general corporate rate 8.0%, combined general rate 23.0%. For a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) eligible for the small business deduction, combined federal and Alberta small business rate is 11.0% on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income. An Alberta Limited Partnership is generally a flow-through entity; the partnership itself has no Part I/AT1 liability, but its members are taxed on allocated income.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% federal Part XIII withholding tax applies to dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident; however an Alberta LP is a pass-through partnership, so a distribution of allocated partnership income does not face Part XIII dividend withholding. If the arrangement is recharacterized as a dividend, royalty, interest, rent or management fee, the statutory Part XIII rate is 25%, reduced under an applicable DTA (e.g., Canada-US DTA: 5% for a corporate shareholder owning at least 10% of the voting stock, 15% for portfolio dividends).

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Due within five months after the partnership's fiscal period end. A non-resident partner that must report allocated Canadian-source income files a non-resident Part I return generally by the CRA due date for its own taxation year.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CRA administratively requires contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation for non-arm's-length transactions with a non-resident where the aggregate value of controlled transactions exceeds CAD 1,000,000 per counterparty or per fiscal year; OECD-aligned Local File/Master File expectations apply above that threshold. Country-by-country reporting is mandatory when consolidated group revenue is at least CAD 750,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations comparable to UAE-style ESR; however the CRA applies GAAR under Income Tax Act section 245, common-law substance-over-form, sham doctrine, beneficial-ownership analysis, and treaty Limitation on Benefits provisions. An Alberta LP providing FDA/CE regulatory consulting must maintain real office premises, board/partner decisions, employees, client contracts, time records, bank accounts, and economic risk in Alberta, otherwise treaty benefits may be denied and the arrangement recharacterized as a shell or conduit.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act subsection 239(1) makes tax evasion an either-way offence: on indictment, a fine of 100% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to 5 years; on summary conviction, similar fines or imprisonment not exceeding 2 years. Civil gross-negligence penalty under subsection 163(2) is 50% of understated tax, plus prescribed interest and late-filing penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and corporate general-partner officers who authorized false filings are jointly and severally liable for unremitted taxes and penalties under subsection 227.1(1); criminal restitution orders may include compensation to CRA, asset freezing, travel restrictions or arrest warrants, and imprisonment. Limited partners lose limited liability if they exercise control over the partnership's business.
Failure to File and Late Remittance

Statutory sanction: Federal late-filing penalty is 5% of unpaid tax plus 1% per complete month up to 12 months; T5013 late-filing penalties are CAD 25 per day per partner with a CAD 2,500 cap; prescribed interest is compounded daily. Alberta imposes equivalent provincial late-filing penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors who knew or ought to have known of non-filing or non-remittance can be personally assessed for the full amount of unremitted tax, interest and penalties. CRA may issue director liability assessments, garnish personal assets, and seek travel bans through criminal warrants where non-compliance is deliberate.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess the transaction to arm's-length terms under Income Tax Act subsection 247(2). Subsection 247(3) penalty applies at 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 and 10% of gross revenue; gross negligence can trigger a separate 50% penalty under subsection 163(2). Inadequate contemporaneous documentation destroys the reasonable-efforts defence.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who direct intentional transfer-pricing distortion can be held personally liable for penalties if they cause the corporation to act without reasonable grounds; criminal tax evasion charges may attach where documents are backdated or false. Directors may also face breach-of-duty civil restitution claims from shareholders or the CRA.
Non-Resident Withholding and NR4 Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: The Canadian payer is assessable under Part XIII for the full amount that should have been withheld, plus interest and penalties. Withholding failures can result in liability equal to 25% of the gross amount paid to the non-resident (treaty-reduced if the DTA is properly invoked). Criminal prosecution applies for wilful failure to report.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for all unremitted Part XIII amounts and related penalties under subsection 227.1(1); CRA can place liens on personal assets and may obtain court orders prohibiting departure from Canada where authorities believe a director may abscond with assets.
Shell Company, Sham, and GAAR Abuse

Statutory sanction: GAAR under subsection 245(2) denies the tax benefit without changing the underlying commercial consequences; subsection 245(4.1) imposes a penalty of 25% of the denied benefit. The sham doctrine permits CRA to reconstruct the true economic arrangement and assess the controlling person or corporate general partner. Penalties, interest, and reassessments can exceed 100% of the tax avoided.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners of the shell LP expose themselves to civil restitution, piercings of limited partnershipveil, personal assessments for entity-level taxes, and criminal liability for participating in a fake arrangement. Travel bans and extradition cooperation can be invoked in serious cross-border tax evasion cases.
