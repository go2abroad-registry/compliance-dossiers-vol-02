Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including the Business Registration Online (BRO) service and My Business Account portal. Alberta legal-entity registration is administered by Service Alberta and Red Tape Reduction through an authorized Alberta Corporate Registry agent; Alberta does not operate a separate value-added tax authority.

E-government filing portal

1. Register the Alberta limited partnership with an authorized Alberta Corporate Registry agent and obtain the provincial registration certificate and Alberta corporate access number. 2. Apply to the CRA for a Business Number (BN), generally using Business Registration Online where available or the CRA non-resident business registration process. 3. Register the applicable CRA program accounts, including GST/HST (RT), payroll (RP), import-export (RM), and non-resident withholding (NR), as required by the activities. 4. File Form RC1 or the applicable non-resident registration documentation where online registration is unavailable. 5. For a partnership carrying on business in Canada or required to report partnership information, obtain and use a partnership tax account and file the applicable T5013 partnership information return. 6. A foreign partner or foreign limited partner generally uses its own foreign tax identification number and may separately require a Canadian BN, GST/HST account, payroll account, import-export account, or non-resident withholding account. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no general United States-style check-the-box election for a Canadian limited partnership. A valid Alberta limited partnership is generally treated as a partnership for Canadian income-tax purposes, with partnership income calculated at the partnership level and allocated to partners under the Income Tax Act. Partnership classification is determined under Canadian law and applicable tax-treaty principles rather than by an elective entity-classification form. Available transactions may include a section 97(2) tax-deferred rollover of eligible property into a partnership, section 85 elections for transfers to a taxable Canadian corporation, and applicable partnership-level elections or designations under the Income Tax Act. Foreign classification elections generally do not bind Canada. Canadian anti-hybrid mismatch rules may deny deductions or impose inclusion consequences for specified hybrid arrangements; they do not provide a general hybrid entity election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies in a single calendar quarter or over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters for a person that is not a small supplier. The threshold generally applies to taxable supplies, including zero-rated supplies, subject to detailed statutory exclusions and aggregation rules. A non-resident may be required to register earlier where it carries on business in Canada, makes taxable supplies in Canada, or otherwise has Canadian GST/HST obligations. Voluntary registration is generally available if the statutory conditions are met.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada may generally be zero-rated where the statutory export conditions are satisfied and the supplier retains evidence that the goods were shipped or delivered outside Canada. Services supplied to a non-resident may be zero-rated only where the Excise Tax Act and its regulations specifically apply, including the relevant non-resident-recipient, use, place-of-supply, and exclusion conditions. For CRO services, the supplier should verify the sponsor's non-resident status, the contractual recipient and ultimate beneficiary, where the services are performed and used, and whether the services relate to tangible personal property, real property, financial services, or another excluded category. Services performed in Canada for a non-resident are not automatically zero-rated. Exporters may recover eligible GST/HST through input tax credits and, where applicable, a rebate or refund mechanism, provided tax was properly accounted for and supporting records are retained.

  • Export declaration or applicable Canadian export reporting record, including the required Canadian Export Reporting System filing where applicable
  • Commercial invoice identifying the supplier, purchaser, goods, value, currency, and destination
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, courier record, or other transport document showing shipment from Canada to a destination outside Canada
  • Proof of delivery or importation at the foreign destination, where available
  • Purchase order, contract, and customer records establishing the non-resident purchaser and export terms
  • For zero-rated services: executed service agreement, tax invoice, evidence of the customer's non-resident status, description of the services, delivery and performance records, and evidence supporting the statutory zero-rating provision
  • Bank receipt, payment record, or other commercial evidence linking consideration to the exported goods or services where relevant; an inward remittance certificate is not generally a Canadian statutory prerequisite
  • Accounting records, GST/HST return workpapers, input-tax-credit support, and correspondence sufficient to establish the applicable zero-rating and destination conditions

Canada has no general nationwide real-time GST clearance, mandatory fiscal-device, or government e-invoicing mandate for ordinary private-sector GST/HST transactions. GST/HST registrants must issue documentation containing the prescribed information, including supplier identity and GST/HST registration number where required, invoice date, description, consideration, tax rate, and tax amount or an acceptable tax-inclusive indication. Electronic invoices and electronic records are generally acceptable if they preserve authenticity, integrity, accessibility, and auditability. CRA filing and payment may be completed electronically through CRA online services. Federal procurement may impose separate electronic invoicing requirements for government suppliers. Alberta has no general private-sector real-time invoice-clearance system.

CRA may reassess an incorrectly zero-rated supply as taxable, recover improperly claimed input tax credits or rebates, assess the unremitted GST/HST, and charge arrears interest from the applicable statutory date. Penalties may apply for late filing, late remittance, false statements, omissions, gross negligence, or repeated non-compliance; gross-negligence penalties can be substantial and fraud may trigger criminal prosecution, fines, and imprisonment under the Excise Tax Act and related legislation. Customs export-reporting violations may result in administrative monetary penalties and other customs enforcement. Records supporting zero-rating and input tax credits must generally be retained for six years from the end of the relevant year, subject to statutory extensions and litigation or objection requirements. The partnership should maintain contemporaneous evidence for each export or offshore-service transaction and should not rely solely on customer location, foreign-currency payment, or an inward remittance as proof of zero-rating.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully liberalized foreign exchange regime with unrestricted convertibility for both current account transactions and capital movements. Profit remittances to foreign investors, shareholders, or service providers are permitted without prior approval or surrender obligations, subject only to standard corporate tax compliance and FINTRAC reporting.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender or conversion of export proceeds is required under Canadian law. Foreign currency receipts from international clinical trial contracts may be retained in offshore accounts. All transactions remain subject to FINTRAC anti-money laundering and counter-terrorism financing reporting obligations.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Canada federal general corporate rate: 15%; Alberta general corporate rate: 8%; combined general corporate rate: 23%. An Alberta limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and does not itself pay income tax; taxable income and losses are allocated to its partners. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may access an 11% combined small-business rate on eligible active business income within applicable limits.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Canada's statutory Part XIII withholding tax rate on dividends paid or credited to non-residents is 25%. Treaty rates commonly reduce this to 5% for qualifying corporate shareholders meeting ownership conditions and 15% for other qualifying shareholders, but the exact rate depends on the applicable treaty, beneficial ownership, limitation provisions, and anti-avoidance rules. A limited partnership generally distributes partnership proceeds rather than dividends; withholding must instead be analyzed by payment type and partner status.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

A partnership information return is generally due within five months after the partnership's fiscal year-end. For a December 31 year-end, the usual deadline is May 31 of the following year. Partnership information slips must generally be provided to partners by the same deadline. A partnership fiscal period generally cannot exceed 12 months and is subject to partnership fiscal-year rules.

Deadline: · Frequency:

A Canadian-resident corporation generally files its T2 within six months after its taxation year-end. Any balance of tax is generally payable two months after year-end, or three months after year-end for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations meeting statutory conditions. Alberta AT1 is generally filed with the corporate return through the applicable Alberta process and follows the corporation's taxation-year reporting cycle.

Deadline: · Frequency:

T4A and NR4 information returns and recipient slips are generally due by the last day of February following the calendar year. Amounts withheld from non-residents are generally remitted by the 15th day of the following month, subject to the applicable remitter category and CRA instructions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no universal monetary threshold that eliminates the requirement to maintain contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation for an otherwise subject transaction. Form T106 reporting generally applies where reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed the applicable aggregate threshold, commonly CAD 1,000,000 for the relevant reporting framework. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to an ultimate parent entity or surrogate parent in Canada belonging to an MNE group meeting the EUR 750 million consolidated-revenue threshold. A Local File and Master File may be required under OECD-aligned rules or foreign parent-country requirements even where no Canadian monetary threshold applies.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a standalone regime titled Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Economic substance remains relevant under the Income Tax Act general anti-avoidance rule, treaty principal-purpose and anti-conduit principles, transfer-pricing rules, corporate-residence analysis, beneficial-ownership requirements, and the common-law sham and factual-misrepresentation doctrines. Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act section 239, conviction may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to five years, or both. Civil reassessment, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, and other administrative penalties may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, officers, and individuals who authorize or participate in the conduct may face prosecution and personal criminal liability. Corporate limited liability does not protect a person from personal liability for fraud, knowing participation, torts, statutory director liability, or criminal conduct. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax sanction but may arise from bail, court orders, or enforcement proceedings.
Unremitted Payroll Withholdings and GST/HST

Statutory sanction: CRA may assess the corporation or partnership for the unremitted amount, interest, late-remittance penalties, failure-to-file penalties, and gross-negligence penalties. Directors may be assessed personally under Income Tax Act section 227.1 for source deductions and Excise Tax Act section 323 for GST/HST, subject to statutory limitation and due-diligence rules.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable after statutory collection conditions are met. A director should maintain payment controls, segregation of trust funds, filing calendars, reconciliations, and evidence of due diligence. Imprisonment is possible for deliberate tax offences but is not automatic for an ordinary remittance default.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under Income Tax Act section 247, deny deductions, adjust income, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the applicable adjustment where statutory conditions are met, deny treaty benefits, and apply interest and other penalties. General anti-avoidance and treaty anti-abuse provisions may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may incur personal exposure where they knowingly participate in fraud, misrepresentation, sham arrangements, or criminal tax conduct. Ordinary transfer-pricing disagreement normally creates corporate or partnership tax liability rather than automatic personal liability.
Partnership Misclassification and Improper Limited-Partner Conduct

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include reassessment of income and losses, denial of deductions, interest, penalties, loss of limited-liability protection under applicable Alberta partnership law, civil claims by creditors, and potential prosecution for fraudulent records or tax evasion.

Director personal liability: An LP has no directors unless a corporate general partner is used. The general partner ordinarily bears unlimited partnership liability. Limited partners may face personal exposure for their own torts, guarantees, fraudulent conduct, unauthorized management activity, or statutory violations.
Shell Company, Beneficial Ownership, and Money Laundering

Statutory sanction: Criminal Code money-laundering offences can result in substantial fines and imprisonment, including imprisonment of up to ten years for indictable offences depending on the charge. Proceeds may be restrained, forfeited, or subject to a civil forfeiture process. Corporate and individual tax penalties may apply separately.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly deal with proceeds of crime, facilitate concealment, or submit false beneficial-ownership information may face criminal prosecution, asset restraint, forfeiture, and imprisonment. A travel ban is not automatic but may be imposed as a bail or sentencing condition.
AML and Sanctions-Control Failure

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties can be imposed for applicable reporting-entity failures, with amounts determined by the violation and compliance history. Criminal offences under AML and sanctions legislation may result in fines, imprisonment, asset seizure, or forfeiture. Canadian sanctions violations may attract significant criminal and administrative consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, compliance officers, and controlling persons may face personal regulatory or criminal exposure for knowing participation, authorization, obstruction, or falsification. A CRO is not automatically a FINTRAC reporting entity solely because it provides clinical-trial services, but its activities, payment services, financial intermediation, and client relationships must be classified.
Clinical-Trial Regulatory and Data-Integrity Breach

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include Health Canada enforcement, inspection findings, product or trial suspension, licence or authorization consequences, contractual damages, civil claims, privacy penalties, professional sanctions, and criminal prosecution for fraud, obstruction, or falsification where applicable.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct or knowingly permit falsification, unsafe conduct, privacy violations, or obstruction may face personal civil and criminal liability. Limited partnership status does not shield a person from liability for personal misconduct or statutory offences.
Unlicensed or Unregistered Business Operations

Statutory sanction: Possible sanctions include stop orders, administrative penalties, suspension or cancellation of authorizations, prosecution, contract termination, civil damages, and disallowance of tax deductions where expenditures or activities are unlawful or unsupported.

Director personal liability: General partners and responsible officers may face personal liability for knowingly conducting unlicensed operations, regulatory offences, fraud, negligence, or breach of fiduciary and statutory duties. Imprisonment depends on the specific offence and proof of culpability.
