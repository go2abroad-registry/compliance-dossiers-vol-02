Applicable AML statute: Alberta limited partnerships are governed principally by the Partnership Act, RSA 2000, c P-3, and the Limited Partnerships Act, RSA 2000, c L-7. Alberta's statutory individual-with-significant-control register regime under the Business Corporations Act, RSA 2000, c B-9, applies to Alberta corporations, not generally to limited partnerships. Canada has no general public beneficial-ownership register for Alberta limited partnerships. The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, SC 2000, c 17, and its regulations impose beneficial-ownership identification and verification duties on covered reporting entities in specified transactions; they do not create a universal LP filing register. Relevant sanctions laws include the United Nations Act, RSC 1985, c U-2, the Special Economic Measures Act, SC 1992, c 17, and applicable provisions of the Criminal Code, RSC 1985, c C-46.
Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

An Alberta LP must maintain the registered office and records arrangements required by the Partnership Act and its registration particulars. A commercial registered-office service may be acceptable if it is a genuine address at which required notices and records can be received and statutory obligations are met. A mailbox-only address may be inadequate. Virtual offices do not replace actual operational substance, tax residence analysis, banking due diligence, or any licensing and professional-conduct requirements. The consulting model does not itself establish authority to practise Alberta law; legal advice or representation reserved to authorized practitioners must be delivered through appropriately licensed professionals.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — Business Registration Online portal and GST/HST program accounts; for Alberta provincial corporate tax coordination, the Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration (TRA) is the provincial counterpart, but GST/HST and federal tax registration is centralized at CRA.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity establishing or registering a limited partnership in Alberta must first have the partnership obtain a federal Business Number (BN) through CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO) or by paper form RC1. The partnership must then open a GST/HST program account (RT) if it makes taxable supplies in Canada and is required to register. For a non-resident partner, confirmation of the partnership's BN and partner identification (Form RC445 or equivalent) is required to administer the pass-through allocation. The LP must provide the BN on all tax filings, customs documents, and invoices. After BN issuance, the LP may be required to register provincially with Alberta's Corporate Registry for extra-provincial registration, but the CRA BN/GST/HST account is the statutory national tax identifier.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — A foreign entity establishing or registering a limited partnership in Alberta must first have the partnership obtain a federal Business Number (BN) through CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO) or by paper form RC1. The partnership must then open a GST/HST program account (RT) if it makes taxable supplies in Canada and is required to register. For a non-resident partner, confirmation of the partnership's BN and partner identification (Form RC445 or equivalent) is required to administer the pass-through allocation. The LP must provide the BN on all tax filings, customs documents, and invoices. After BN issuance, the LP may be required to register provincially with Alberta's Corporate Registry for extra-provincial registration, but the CRA BN/GST/HST account is the statutory national tax identifier.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canadian tax law does not have a US-style 'check-the-box' election. A Canadian limited partnership is statutorily treated as a fiscally transparent flow-through entity for income tax purposes, with income allocated to partners under the Income Tax Act. For GST/HST, the partnership is a 'person' separate from its partners and must register in its own capacity. Elections relevant to this structure include the GST/HST partnership fiscal-year election (under ETA section 252), the section 216 election for non-resident partners to file on a net basis, and partnership fiscal-period alignment elections under the Income Tax Act. No hybrid mismatch election is available under Canadian domestic law.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated supplies) in a calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters; compulsory registration once threshold exceeded, but a person may voluntarily register for GST/HST.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under the Excise Tax Act, supplies of goods exported by a GST/HST registrant are zero-rated if the supplier maintains proof of export and the goods are delivered outside Canada within prescribed timeframes. Services supplied to a non-resident person are zero-rated if the service is performed primarily outside Canada, or if the non-resident is not registered under the GST/HST regime and certain conditions are met. For an anti-dumping trade-defense consulting firm, legal and advisory services provided to a non-resident client are generally zero-rated as 'advisory, consulting or professional services' when the non-resident is not a GST/HST registrant and the services are not supplied to a Canadian resident. The firm must obtain documentary evidence of the non-resident status of the client and the offshore nature of the supply.

  • Customs Export Declaration (A48/CAED or electronic export reporting via CRA/Statistics Canada)
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill evidencing physical export
  • Commercial invoice or contract confirming the supply
  • Bank/payment records and inward remittance certificate or wire confirmation evidencing payment from non-resident
  • Proof of non-resident status (e.g., non-Canadian GST/HST registration status, foreign address, and business status)

Canada does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping regime. GST/HST registrants must issue prescribed information on invoices (Section 169/223 of the Excise Tax Act), including BN and GST/HST registration number. Electronic invoicing is permitted but not mandatory; there is no government API clearance requirement. The CRA requires electronic filing of GST/HST returns (GST34) for registrants with total annual taxable supplies above a threshold (generally CAD 1.5 million), using CRA-approved software or GST NETFILE. Tax recovery audits and e-audit data exchange via CRA's online portals are standard.

The CRA can audit zero-rating claims retroactively and require the registrant to self-assess GST/HST if documentary proof is inadequate. False or fraudulent statements, failure to maintain records, and net tax misstatements are subject to penalties under the Excise Tax Act: generally 25% of the net tax amount in issue for gross negligence (with amounts up to 100% of the evasion amount under criminal prosecution), plus interest. Claiming zero-rating on non-qualifying exports or fictitious exports can lead to reassessment, clawback of input tax credits, civil penalties of up to CAD 8,000 per violation for knowingly making false statements, and criminal liability under sections 239 and 327 of the Excise Tax Act. For repeat or egregious cases, the CRA may also impose a penalty of 10% of the tax collected but not remitted, and prosecution may include imprisonment. Transfer pricing and cross-border service documentation must be maintained for at least six years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for ordinary current-account and capital-account transactions. Canada does not generally impose capital controls, mandatory repatriation, dividend-conversion, or profit-remittance restrictions. A Canadian limited partnership may receive and remit foreign currency subject to bank due diligence, sanctions screening, tax compliance, partnership-agreement authority, source-of-funds verification, and applicable withholding-tax rules. Payments to non-residents may require Canadian tax withholding, information reporting, or treaty analysis, depending on their legal character. Canada also maintains investment and national-security review regimes, including the Investment Canada Act, but these are not general foreign-exchange controls.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, mandatory conversion ratio, or general deadline for converting export receipts into Canadian dollars. Export proceeds may remain in foreign-currency accounts or be converted at the partnership's discretion. Customs, goods-export reporting, sanctions, tax, transfer-pricing, and anti-money-laundering obligations remain applicable where relevant. Service exports, including trade-remedy consulting, generally require accurate invoicing, contract documentation, foreign-client identification, and appropriate GST/HST treatment; no foreign-exchange conversion filing is normally required solely because a service fee is received from abroad.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no general exchange-control authority requiring routine approval of foreign-exchange transactions. Anti-money-laundering and sanctions controls are administered principally by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), while prudential oversight of federally regulated banks is conducted by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium to High for supplemental collection, payment, and foreign-currency functionality, but not a complete substitute for a Canadian commercial-bank relationship. An offshore electronic-money institution cannot independently open a Canadian deposit account or provide unrestricted Canadian banking services without the required Canadian authorization or a permitted regulated-partner structure. The partnership must verify the provider's licensing, safeguarding model, geographic permissions, correspondent-bank arrangements, sanctions controls, data-processing terms, insolvency protections, withdrawal rights, and service continuity. Canadian-dollar settlement may depend on a Canadian bank or authorized payment-service-provider arrangement. The provider's onboarding does not eliminate Canadian legal obligations relating to source of funds, tax reporting, sanctions, beneficial ownership, or record retention.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Not applicable at Alberta LP entity level; the LP is fiscally transparent under Income Tax Act s.96 and income/loss flows to partners. Combined federal + Alberta general corporate headline rate for a corporate partner is 23% (15% federal + 8% Alberta provincial).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Canadian Part XIII statutory withholding on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident is 25%. Under the Canada–US DTA, the general rate is 15%, and 5% for a US corporate shareholder owning at least 10% of the voting shares. An Alberta LP is not a corporation; ordinary distributions of partnership profits to non-resident partners are not dividends and are not subject to Part XIII withholding. They are taxable to the non-resident partner under Part I unless a DTA exempts the profit share for lack of a Canadian permanent establishment.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no statutory CAD de minimis threshold for contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation; adequate documentation is required for all cross-border transactions between related persons. The Country-by-Country Reporting threshold for MNEs, including the requirement to maintain master file/local file materials and file CbC report, applies where consolidated group revenue is at least CAD 750 million. CRA may request contemporaneous documentation within three months of a review; failure to provide it can trigger a 10% transfer pricing penalty on the net adjustment under s.247(3).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) framework comparable to UAE ESR. Substance is nonetheless legally required through Income Tax Act s.245 (General Anti-Avoidance Rule), common law sham and substance-over-form doctrines, and the at-risk limitation for limited partners. An Alberta LP that exists only as a shell to redirect anti-dumping consulting revenue to non-resident related parties, without real Canadian employees, physical office, actual business risk, or substantive economic activity, will be recharacterized or have its allocation denied by CRA.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False or Deceptive Statements

Statutory sanction: Gross negligence civil penalty under s.163(2) of the Income Tax Act equal to 50% of understated tax, plus compound daily interest; criminal prosecutions under s.239 can result in fines of 50% to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment for up to two years; CRA may also deny treaty benefits and reassess outside the normal limitation period in cases of misrepresentation.

Director personal liability: The general partner of the LP is jointly and severally liable for the partnership's unpaid tax liabilities; where the general partner is a corporation, its directors may be personally liable for unremitted Part XIII or payroll withholdings under s.227.1 and GST/HST under ETA s.323. Where fraud or willful blindness is established, courts may pierce the corporate veil and impose civil restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment.
Transfer Pricing / Cross-Border Recharacterization

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing adjustment under s.247(2) plus a 10% penalty under s.247(3) for failure to make reasonable efforts and provide contemporaneous documentation; GAAR under s.245 may apply to recharacterize the entire arrangement and impose a 25% avoidance penalty; daily compound interest accrues on all reassessed amounts.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner can be assessed personally for unremitted source deductions and corresponding penalties; if the arrangement amounts to a sham or fraudulent conveyance, Alberta courts may disregard the LP and the corporate general partner's limitation of liability, exposing directors to civil and criminal liability.
Anti-Money Laundering / FINTRAC Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties can reach CAD 1 million for individuals and CAD 2 million for entities per violation; criminal convictions can result in imprisonment for up to five years; FINTRAC can impose compliance orders and publication of penalties.

Director personal liability: Officers, directors, and senior managers who authorize or acquiesce in AML non-compliance can be personally prosecuted; professional licensing bodies for legal and accounting practitioners may impose suspension, disbarment, or revocation; international cooperation can lead to extradition where money laundering or terrorism financing is involved.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance / Aggressive Tax Avoidance

Statutory sanction: CRA can apply s.245 GAAR to deny the tax benefit and impose a penalty of 25% of the benefit, or 50% where misrepresentation is involved; extended reassessment periods apply; CRA can also impose large penalties for failure to file foreign reporting forms and may refer the matter to the Public Prosecution Service of Canada for criminal tax evasion.

Director personal liability: The general partner and its directors can be held jointly and severally liable for the LP's federal and provincial tax debts where the LP is used as a sham to evade tax; Alberta case law permits veil-piercing where the partnership structure is used for fraud, oppression, or a mask for illegal conduct, including international anti-dumping fee manipulation.
