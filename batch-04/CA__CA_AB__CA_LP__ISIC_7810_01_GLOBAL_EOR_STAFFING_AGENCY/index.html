Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum capital is prescribed under the Partnership Act. Each limited partner's contribution must be cash or property (services are not a valid capital contribution). Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; for Alberta, the CRA administers federal and Alberta corporate income tax, GST/HST, and payroll remittances. Registration and filings are performed through the CRA 'My Business Account' portal and the Business Registration Online (BRO) service.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada or making taxable supplies in Canada must obtain a Canada Revenue Agency Business Number (BN) before commencing commercial activities. The standard procedure for an Alberta limited partnership is: (1) register the limited partnership under the Alberta Partnership Act and obtain the Alberta corporate registry number; (2) apply online to CRA for a federal BN, selecting the applicable program accounts (GST/HST 'RT', payroll 'RP', corporation income tax 'RC' if the partnership has elected or is deemed to be a corporation - otherwise no RC account for a pure pass-through limited partnership; import/export 'RM' if applicable); (3) register for the GST/HST account once taxable supplies made in Canada exceed the C$30,000 small-supplier threshold or volunteer for early registration; (4) register for a CRA payroll account if the LP acts as an employer of record for Canadian employees; (5) for Alberta provincial corporate income tax purposes, where the LP is transparent and partners report their shares, each partner (including non-residents) must have or obtain a CRA business number or social insurance number/individual tax number to file partnership allocation returns; a non-resident limited partner is required to obtain an International Tax Number or individual tax number for Canadian T2/S125 filings allocations. The LP itself, for GST/HST and payroll, is a separate 'person' and holds its own BN and program accounts.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no general check-the-box entity classification election comparable to the United States. The tax character of a limited partnership is fixed by legal form and the Income Tax Act; it is a flow-through vehicle unless deemed to be a corporation under the federal or provincial corporate attribution rules. The principal available election mechanisms relevant to an Alberta LP are: (a) partnership income and loss allocation elections among partners, subject to the 'reasonable allocation' requirements and the specified member partnership loss restriction rules; (b) elections under subsection 96(3) or the Taxpayer Migration rules to change a business's fiscal period for partners; (c) the section 216 election for a non-resident to report Canadian-source business income on a net basis rather than on a gross withholding basis; (d) the section 217 election for non-resident individuals to include Canadian-source payments in income on a net basis; and (e) for GST/HST, the election under section 211 of the Excise Tax Act to treat a taxable supply of a service or intangible personal property as being made outside Canada when the registrant supplies a service to a non-resident and the supply would otherwise be considered made in Canada. no provincial or federal hybrid mismatch election exists specifically for limited partnerships beyond the mandatory application of sections 18.2(1) and 18.2(4) of the Income Tax Act.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000 (small-supplier threshold). A person, including a non-resident carrying on business in Canada, must register for GST/HST when the value of taxable supplies made in Canada by the person and all associated persons exceeds C$30,000 in any single calendar quarter or in the aggregate over the immediately preceding four calendar quarters. Voluntary registration is permitted even below the threshold. For an Alberta LP, once registered, the 5% GST applies to taxable supplies of employer-of-record, payroll administration, contractor compliance, and related staffing services unless the supply is zero-rated as an export of services to a non-resident.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under the Excise Tax Act, a supply of services made by a GST/HST registrant to a non-resident client qualifies for zero-rating when the supply is not one of the statutorily excluded categories (e.g., services performed in respect of real property situated in Canada, services rendered in respect of tangible personal property situated in Canada, or a supply of a service made to a non-resident who is present in Canada and is registered under the Excise Tax Act for the purpose of receiving the service). For a global EOR and cross-border contractor payroll business supplied from an Alberta LP, the zero-rating rule generally applies to payroll, HR administration, employer compliance, and contractor onboarding services supplied to offshore employers or foreign staffing resellers, provided the CRA 'non-resident recipient' evidentiary test is met and the benefit of the service is not consumed by a resident employee in Canada. For physical goods exports, zero-rating applies only if the goods are delivered, shipped, or caused to be delivered to a non-resident outside Canada and exported in accordance with the Customs Act and Schedule VI Part I of the Excise Tax Act. The registrant must retain documentation showing the recipient's non-resident status, the commercial contract, the actual performance of the service, and, for goods, evidence of exportation within a reasonable period.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (G13/A) or electronic export declaration submitted through the Canadian Export Reporting System
  • Commercial invoice and detailed service/payroll billing substantiation
  • Bill of Lading / Airway Bill / proof of export issued by a common carrier or courier
  • Signed statement of goods/services supplied to the non-resident, including the retained non-resident status evidence (e.g., certificate of residence or sworn declaration)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank transfer records evidencing non-resident payment and foreign exchange repatriation

The CRA does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, government-approved digital stamping, or continuous third-party transaction clearance. Electronic invoicing is lawful and commercially accepted, but not compulsory. Registrants must file GST/HST returns electronically using CRA-certified software through the GST/HST NETFILE service, and remittances must be made electronically if the registrant's average monthly net tax remittance is C$10,000 or more. Payroll information returns (T4 and T4A) must be filed electronically when more than 50 slips are issued. There is no CRA-permissioned API or real-time machine-verification stamping process for GST/HST invoices; documentary retention rules under section 87 of the Excise Tax Act remain the primary mandate.

The CRA is entitled to reassess a zero-rated export claim at any time if false statements, negligence, careless misstatement, or wilful default are established; the ordinary four-year reassessment limitation period does not apply in those cases. Where the CRA disallows zero-rating, the registrant is liable for the uncollected 5% GST, interest computed from the original reporting date, administrative penalties for false statements under the Excise Tax Act (including gross negligence penalties based on the understated net tax), and, in aggravated cases, criminal prosecution for tax evasion under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act, with fines and imprisonment available. Retroactive clawback assessments are routinely issued where an exporter fails to produce export evidence, where the service is performed in Canada for the benefit of a resident, or where the extended deadline for foreign currency repatriation (180 days or the reasonable commercial window for services) is not met. The registrant must maintain all export substantiation for at least six years after the end of the relevant fiscal year.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible under Article VIII of the IMF Articles of Agreement. Canada imposes no outward or inward exchange control licensing on current or capital account transactions. Profit remittance from a Canadian limited partnership to non-resident partners is subject to Part XIII withholding tax (25% statutory; reduced under applicable tax treaties, e.g., 15% for U.S. limited partners under the Canada-U.S. Tax Convention, provided an NR301 form is filed). There are no exchange controls preventing repatriation, but non-resident partners must file a section 116 or Part XIII return and obtain a clearance certificate where applicable. Fund transfers are reported to Statistics Canada via the International Transactions Reporting System (ITRS) through financial institutions for balance-of-payments purposes; no prior authorization is required for dividend or distribution remittances.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada does not require export proceeds to be surrendered or converted into Canadian dollars. Exporters, including cross-border service providers such as an Employer of Record, may retain foreign currency receipts in any account, with no mandatory conversion timeline or ratio. However, the entity must report all export and service receipts through the Bank of Canada's Report of International Transactions (Form B13) or the equivalent financial institution reporting mechanism, and any foreign currency accounts must be declared in the annual T2 corporate tax filing (for corporations) or partnership information return (T5013), as applicable. No statutory surrender period applies.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for cross-border payment oversight; foreign investment review is administered by Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (ISED) under the Investment Canada Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium. Offshore digital EMI institutions (e.g., electronic money institutions authorised in the EU or UK) are not regulated as banks in Canada and are generally not permitted to accept deposits or provide clearing services without registration as a Money Service Business (MSB) with FINTRAC. A non-resident EMI may provide limited cross-border payment transmission if it is registered as an MSB and maintains a physical compliance presence or an agent in Canada. Legal constraints include: (i) Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) requiring MSB licensing before offering services to Canadian clients; (ii) OSFI prohibited from allowing unlicensed fintechs to use the term 'bank'; (iii) restrictions on the use of Canadian payment rails (Interac, Real-Time Rail) which are accessible only to regulated financial institutions and their licensed agents. Practical viability for the LP is moderate only if the offshore EMI partners with a Canadian chartered bank or obtains FINTRAC registration; otherwise, the treasury function should rely exclusively on Tier-1 Canadian commercial banks.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections from foreign partners or parent entities must be routed via SWIFT MT103 with a specific purpose code, typically 'Foreign Direct Investment' or 'Intercompany Capital Contribution' (Bank of Canada's BOP coding, e.g., 'E360' for direct investment equity). The receiving Canadian bank will record the transaction for Statistics Canada's ITRS and under the Investment Canada Act the LP's foreign controller must file a notification if the enterprise's asset value exceeds the prescribed threshold (CAD 1 billion for WTO investors since 2021, subject to periodic review). For standard Alberta LP contributions, a board resolution (or limited-partnership consent), a legal description of the capital call, and a certificate from the general partner that the funds are not derived from criminal activity are required. The bank will verify against the account title, conduct sanctions screening, and may request a copy of the registered LP declaration and partnership agreement to confirm signatory authority.For cross-border settlement of service invoices, contractor payroll, and withholding taxes, the primary clearing rail is SWIFT MT103 for direct beneficiary transfers. For large-value B2B procurement of subcontracted staffing services, a commercial letter of credit (LC) may be used when the counterparty requires payment security; however, given the low-risk nature of the EOR platform, MT103 with full structured remittance information (e.g., invoice number, contractor ID, tax reference) is preferred. Domestic Alberta payroll disbursements run through the Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS) or the newer Real-Time Rail (RTR) / Interac e-Transfer for smaller payouts, but for cross-border contractor payments, the LP should maintain USD and CAD settlement accounts and use FX hedging instruments to manage cash-flow volatility. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium. Offshore digital EMI institutions (e.g., electronic money institutions authorised in the EU or UK) are not regulated as banks in Canada and are generally not permitted to accept deposits or provide clearing services without registration as a Money Service Business (MSB) with FINTRAC. A non-resident EMI may provide limited cross-border payment transmission if it is registered as an MSB and maintains a physical compliance presence or an agent in Canada. Legal constraints include: (i) Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) requiring MSB licensing before offering services to Canadian clients; (ii) OSFI prohibited from allowing unlicensed fintechs to use the term 'bank'; (iii) restrictions on the use of Canadian payment rails (Interac, Real-Time Rail) which are accessible only to regulated financial institutions and their licensed agents. Practical viability for the LP is moderate only if the offshore EMI partners with a Canadian chartered bank or obtains FINTRAC registration; otherwise, the treasury function should rely exclusively on Tier-1 Canadian commercial banks.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not itself subject to corporate income tax. Taxable income is allocated to the partners. If a Canadian corporation is used as a partner or operating entity, the general combined federal-Alberta corporate income tax rate is 23%: 15% federal and 8% Alberta. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally obtain an 11% combined rate on eligible active business income within the applicable small-business limits: 9% federal and 2% Alberta.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax applies to dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to an applicable tax treaty or other statutory reduction. The Canada-Alberta LP itself normally does not pay dividends; partnership distributions generally represent distributions of partnership property or previously allocated income and must be analyzed separately from corporate dividends.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not use a single universal statutory Local File threshold expressed solely in local currency. Under Income Tax Act section 247, contemporaneous documentation is required for relevant non-arm's-length cross-border transactions and must be prepared by the taxpayer's filing due date and provided to the CRA within three months of a written request. For penalty administration, CRA guidance generally treats a Canadian entity with gross revenues of at least CAD 10 million and an applicable non-arm's-length transaction of at least CAD 1 million as a principal risk threshold; these amounts are not a safe harbor from the arm's-length requirement. A Canadian Master File or Local File may also be required under the OECD three-tier framework where the applicable Canadian filing and documentation conditions are met. CbC reporting generally applies at the EUR 750 million consolidated group-revenue threshold.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone economic-substance regulation equivalent to the UAE ESR regime. Substance remains relevant under the general anti-avoidance rule, transfer-pricing rules, treaty beneficial-ownership and principal-purpose analyses, corporate-residence rules, partnership characterization, and permanent-establishment determinations. The LP should maintain Canadian commercial substance proportionate to its claimed activities, including genuine decision-making, personnel or controlled functions, books and records, banking, contractual authority, risk control, and evidence of services. A registered address, nominee general partner, or pass-through invoicing model without corresponding functions may be challenged.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and deliberate misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act section 239 can impose a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, or imprisonment for up to two years, or both. Civil reassessment, interest, gross-negligence penalties, and denial of deductions may apply separately.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, officers, and controlling persons may face criminal prosecution where personal participation or authorization is proven. Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and certain trust amounts under Income Tax Act section 227.1 and related provisions. Imprisonment is legally available; travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty but may arise from criminal process, bail conditions, enforcement proceedings, or immigration consequences.
Failure to remit payroll source deductions

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unremitted amount, arrears interest, penalties generally ranging from 10% to 20% for failures to deduct or remit, and additional penalties for repeated or knowingly false conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally assessed for unremitted trust amounts if statutory due-diligence and limitation rules are satisfied. General partners may face comparable exposure depending on the partnership and applicable provincial law.
GST/HST fraud or non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of tax, interest, civil penalties, denial of input tax credits, gross-negligence penalties, and criminal prosecution for tax evasion or false claims. GST/HST collected and not remitted may be treated as trust amounts.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted GST/HST under the Excise Tax Act, subject to statutory defenses and limitation periods. Criminal conviction may result in fines and imprisonment.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: Reassessment under Income Tax Act section 247, denial or adjustment of deductions, secondary adjustments where applicable, and a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the applicable adjustment where the statutory conditions are met.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may face civil exposure for knowing participation in fraudulent records or distributions. Criminal liability requires proof of the applicable offence and culpable conduct; ordinary transfer-pricing disagreement is not by itself criminal.
Shell company, sham partnership, or abusive treaty arrangement

Statutory sanction: Application of the general anti-avoidance rule, denial of treaty benefits, reassessment, withholding tax, interest, penalties, and possible partnership recharacterization. The principal-purpose test may deny treaty benefits where obtaining the benefit was one of the principal purposes of the arrangement.

Director personal liability: General partners and decision-makers may incur civil restitution, assessment, and prosecution exposure for fraudulent implementation. Corporate veil piercing is exceptional and fact-specific; mere ownership or tax-efficient structuring is not sufficient.
Unlawful operation of staffing or employment services

Statutory sanction: Provincial administrative penalties, orders to pay wages and statutory entitlements, workers' compensation assessments, licensing sanctions, tax reassessments, and prosecution under applicable provincial legislation. Immigration advice or recruitment activities may require additional authorization.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally liable for certain unpaid wages, regulatory offences, and knowing participation in unlawful employment practices. Imprisonment depends on the specific provincial offence and proof of culpability.
AML, sanctions, and suspicious payment activity

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, registration cancellation, seizure or forfeiture, sanctions penalties, and criminal prosecution under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, the Criminal Code, or sanctions legislation. Payment processors may suspend or terminate accounts.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and authorized signatories may face personal criminal liability for knowing participation, laundering, terrorist financing, sanctions evasion, or obstruction. Fines, imprisonment, asset restraint, and forfeiture may apply; travel restrictions may arise from criminal proceedings but are not automatic.
Recordkeeping and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: Assessment based on available information, denial of deductions or credits, information-return penalties, compliance orders, civil penalties, and prosecution for obstruction or false records.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and general partners may be personally liable where they authorized or knowingly permitted false or incomplete records. A Canadian corporation's individuals-with-significant-control obligations and Alberta partnership records should be maintained continuously.
Improper distribution or insolvency-related transfer

Statutory sanction: Recovery of improper distributions, tracing and restitution, insolvency clawback, interest, civil damages, tax-trust enforcement, and potential criminal charges for fraud or breach of trust.

Director personal liability: General partners have potentially unlimited liability under the partnership structure, notwithstanding limited liability of limited partners. Directors may face statutory and fiduciary claims; personal liability can include restitution, asset recovery, and, for criminal conduct, imprisonment.
