Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no U.S.-style general check-the-box election for partnerships. An Alberta limited partnership is generally treated as a partnership for Canadian income-tax purposes, with income and losses allocated to partners under the partnership agreement and the Income Tax Act. Available statutory mechanisms may include a subsection 97(2) tax-deferred rollover for qualifying transfers of property to a partnership, subsection 85 elections for qualifying transfers to a taxable Canadian corporation, capital-cost allowance elections, GST/HST elections where statutory conditions apply, and treaty-based or statutory elections affecting non-resident partners. Partnership classification, residence, permanent-establishment, and hybrid-mismatch outcomes must be assessed separately in each partner jurisdiction.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding zero-rated supplies, measured under the small-supplier rules. Registration is generally required when the threshold is exceeded in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters. Voluntary registration is permitted for eligible businesses below the threshold and generally creates filing and collection obligations.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods may generally be zero-rated when supplied for export and the supplier obtains and retains evidence that the goods were exported from Canada in accordance with the Excise Tax Act and applicable regulations. Services supplied to non-residents are not automatically zero-rated. Zero-rating depends on the specific place-of-supply and exported-service rules, including the recipient's non-resident status, the nature and use of the service, and exclusions for services related to Canadian real property, tangible personal property situated in Canada, or persons physically present in Canada. Corporate secretarial and registered-agent services performed for a non-resident customer require transaction-specific analysis; Canadian performance, Canadian business operations, or a Canadian establishment of the recipient may prevent zero-rating. Input tax credits may be claimed by a GST registrant for eligible GST incurred in commercial activities, subject to documentary evidence and statutory limitations.

  • Canada Customs export declaration or applicable CBSA export reporting record
  • Commercial invoice identifying the supplier, recipient, description, value, and export destination
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, courier record, or other carrier evidence of export
  • Packing list and proof of delivery or importation in the destination jurisdiction, where applicable
  • Contract, purchase order, customer tax-residency evidence, and correspondence supporting the export or non-resident-service qualification
  • Evidence of payment or commercial settlement, where relevant; Canada generally does not require an inward-remittance certificate as a general GST zero-rating condition

Canada has no general real-time GST invoice-clearance, fiscal-stamping, or mandatory B2B e-invoicing regime for ordinary Alberta businesses. GST registrants must issue compliant invoices or receipts showing prescribed information, maintain reliable electronic books and records, and retain supporting records for the statutory retention period. CRA electronic filing and electronic payment requirements may apply based on registrant status, filing frequency, or prescribed thresholds. Electronic invoices are acceptable if integrity, authenticity, accessibility, and auditability are maintained.

Canada has no general statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for claiming GST zero-rating or input tax credits. CRA may reassess improperly zero-rated supplies as taxable, assess unremitted GST, deny input tax credits, and charge arrears interest. Gross-negligence penalties may apply where the taxpayer knowingly or negligently makes false statements or omissions; civil penalties may apply for false GST returns, failure to file, failure to remit, or failure to maintain adequate records. Knowingly participating in fraudulent invoices, fictitious exports, or false claims may result in criminal prosecution, fines, and imprisonment. Reassessments may include earlier reporting periods subject to the ordinary assessment period, extended periods for misrepresentation attributable to negligence, fraud, or wilful blindness, and applicable GST/HST anti-fraud and general-anti-avoidance rules.

Stage 04

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is an IMF Article VIII member; the Canadian dollar is freely convertible and both current-account and capital-account transactions are generally unrestricted. No permission is required for inbound or outbound remittances except where the transaction is subject to federal sanctions, Canadian Economic Action Regulations, customs export controls, or anti-money-laundering reporting. Profit remittances by an Alberta Limited Partnership are not subject to exchange-control approval, but non-resident partners receiving Canadian-source income through distributions may be deemed to have made a taxable remittance under Part XIII Income Tax Act, creating a withholding obligation at source. Withholding rates are set at 25% absent treaty relief, often reduced to 5%-15% by tax treaties. Under provincial law, Alberta does not impose separate exchange or remittance approval. Private placement of capital is subject to Investment Canada Act reporting and federal beneficial-ownership transparency rules.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender or mandatory conversion ratio. Exporters are free to retain foreign currency, convert it at commercial bank rates, or use it for trade settlement. The only compliance duties are: (i) accurate transaction reporting on Form A and customs declarations for goods crossing the border, (ii) FINTRAC large-transaction reports for cash or wire transfers of C$10,000 or more, and (iii) declaration of foreign transactions on Form T1135 if the LP or non-resident-controlled entity has foreign income and reporting thresholds apply. Alberta has no separate provincial rule.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), in coordination with the Department of Finance Canada and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada maintains no centralized foreign-exchange-control administration; currency convertibility is governed by trade regulations, sanctions, and tax reporting requirements.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility: Limited for an Alberta LP using an EU-style electronic money institution (EMI) as its primary banking rail. Canada does not recognize the EMI authorization; foreign EMIs cannot provide deposit-taking or lend credit for a Canadian-domiciled entity. They may act as corporate payment facilitators or digital-wallet providers through registered Canadian money-services-business (MSB) channels, but they are not licensed deposit institutions and cannot offer accounts denominated in Canadian dollars for 'banking' unless issued a bank order under the Bank Act or a federal credit-union authorization. FINTRAC registration, provincial licences under the Money Services Act, and clearing through Payments Canada are required before reliance. Viability rating: Low as a substitute for a bank; Medium as a supplemental FX and disbursement rail for secretarial-fee collection from offshore clients.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

The LP may receive capital contributions from non-resident partners through direct SWIFT MT103 wire transfers to its Canadian operational account, using the following protocol: (i) Must be wired to the bank's branch account with beneficiary being the Alberta Limited Partnership and a clear reference to 'Capital Contribution'; (ii) beneficiary bank will apply FINTRAC reporting if ≥C$10,000 and will perform sanctions/LISTS checks; (iii) the LP must disclose the purpose in the wire narrative as 'ISIC 8299 registered-agent and secretarial retainer' or 'equity contribution'; (iv) any capital injection must be matched to the LP's capital-call schedule and contribute to the registration of no par value units under Alberta Partnership Regulations; (v) if contribution exceeds C$1,000,000 or involves a non-WTO sector, the Investment Canada Act notification may be required; (vi) cross-currency wires are settled via SWIFT settlement rates determined by the bank's FX desk, with no central-bank approval. Inbound monies from disallowed foreign jurisdictions must be frozen and reported; for Canadian dollars, the transfer is irrevocable once the MT103 is credited through Payments Canada's Lynx system.Outgoing settlements for trade-related payments (e.g., payments for procurement of office infrastructure, foreign legal/AML compliance tools, or distribution of profits to limited partners) are cleared by: (i) SWIFT MT103 for correspondent banking to non-Canadian beneficiaries; (ii) Payments Canada Lynx for large Canadian-dollar transfers; (iii) Western Union/Forex remittance rails for small invoice settlements; (iv) Commercial letters of credit (LCs) issued by a Canadian bank under UCP600, with MT700/MT710 messages for documentary trades; and (v) CAD-denominated automated clearing (ACSS) for direct withdrawals and deposits.
