E-government filing portal

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — the Alberta LP is fiscally transparent under Income Tax Act s. 96: neither the partnership nor the limited partnership as such pays federal income tax; each partner includes its allocated share of webtoon syndication income. Territorial scope: resident partners taxed on worldwide income; non-resident partners taxed only on Canadian-source partnership income (ITA s. 115.1), subject to applicable treaty relief. For GST/HST, the LP is a separate person under the Excise Tax Act and is the supplier of record unless Apple/Google act as merchant of record. Alberta levies no PST, no provincial VAT, and no capital tax; Alberta corporate income tax (general rate 8%, small-business rate 2%) is imposed only at the corporate-partner level and is collected by CRA.

Available election forms

Canada does not offer a U.S.-style check-the-box election. Relevant elections and mechanisms: (1) U.S. partners may file IRS Form 8832 to classify the Canadian LP as a partnership or taxable association for U.S. federal purposes; (2) voluntary registration election for small suppliers under ETA s. 240 to claim input tax credits; (3) evidence-and-election protocol for zero-rating exports of intangible personal property under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 23; (4) joint election under ETA s. 273 for certain shared supplies; (5) hybrid mismatch rules under ITA ss. 18.4 through 18.6 are mandatory anti-avoidance rules and are not elective; (6) Alberta Partnership Act forms for certification of limited partner status to preserve limited liability and pass-through treatment.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the GST/HST small-supplier threshold measured by total worldwide taxable supplies in the current calendar quarter and the preceding four consecutive quarters. Mandatory registration applies upon crossing the threshold; voluntary registration is permitted below it. Non-resident suppliers of digital services to Canadian consumers face the same CAD 30,000 threshold under the 2021 simplified registration framework.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 23 to a supply of intangible personal property (digital webtoon content, chapter-unlocking rights, and licences) made to a non-resident recipient who is not registered under the ETA, provided the property is delivered for consumption wholly outside Canada. Related services supplied to non-residents are zero-rated under s. 7 unless they relate to Canadian real or tangible property. Conditions for the Alberta LP: (1) app-store territory, IP address, and payment-card billing country must be outside Canada; (2) the recipient must be a non-registered non-resident; (3) no GST/HST may have been collected on the export transaction; (4) Apple/Google sales reports must attribute the transaction to a foreign territory and show CAD amounts; (5) where Apple or Google act as merchant of record and collect GST/HST for Canadian consumers, only net-of-tax proceeds are booked and zero-rating is not claimed for those Canadian sales. Input tax credits attributable to zero-rated export sales are fully recoverable. Canada has no statutory 180-day FX repatriation deadline for digital export proceeds; the 180-day benchmark is used by banks, PSPs, and auditors as the standard documentation and settlement norm.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A) — required only if physical promotional merchandise is exported; not applicable to intangible webtoon digital content
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill — required only for physical goods; for digital exports, CRA accepts Apple App Store Connect and Google Play Console territorial sales and tax reports as substitute evidence of export
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) or bank/PSP settlement advices (SWIFT MT103) in CAD — evidencing repatriation of foreign-sale proceeds through the partner bank account of the LP
  • Platform settlement reports with geo-IP and billing-country attributes, Apple/Google Merchant of Record agreements, and the LP's internal zero-rating substantiation files retained for six years under ITA s. 230

Canada imposes no real-time fiscal clearance, pre-approval, digital stamping, or mandatory tax-API e-invoicing regime. Invoice content is governed by ETA s. 223 (supplier name and BN, recipient, date, description, consideration, tax amount). Registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 1.5 million must file GST/HST returns electronically via GST/HST NETFILE or EDI; all registrants must retain records for six years under ITA s. 230. Canada is voluntarily adopting the Pan-Canadian PEPPOL e-invoicing standard, but CRA does not operate an invoice-clearing or pre-posting audit system. Platform sales reports from Apple/Google must be reconciled to the LP's GST/HST returns and invoice records.

CRA audits digital exporters by cross-referencing Apple/Google payout reports with GST/HST returns and geo-attribute evidence; insufficient proof of non-resident receipt triggers reassessment. Where zero-rating is denied, the LP owes 5% GST on the full supply plus arrears interest; additional exposure includes the ETA s. 280/285 false-statement and gross-negligence penalties of up to 50% of net tax, criminal tax-evasion prosecution under ETA s. 327, and mirror income-tax penalties under ITA s. 163.2 (including 50% gross-negligence penalties). The normal reassessment window is four years, but it is unlimited where misrepresentation, fraud, or wilful neglect is shown. If Apple/Google collected GST/HST on Canadian sales while the LP also zero-rated those sales, CRA will claw back the unreported tax with interest and penalties. There is no separate statutory clawback term beyond the reassessment window; un-repatriated export proceeds may be recharacterized as partner benefits or trigger transfer-pricing adjustments.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is a freely convertible currency in both the current and capital accounts. There are no general restrictions on foreign-exchange transactions, dividend distributions, or profit repatriation for Foreign-Owned or Domestic-Controlled Entities. However, for a Canadian Limited Partnership (CA_LP) whose partners are non-residents, routine remittance of distributable profits is subject to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (default 25%, treaty-reduced where applicable) and to the completion of a Remittance Certificate (NR4) and mandatory FINTRAC reporting for outgoing wires exceeding CAD 10,000. Capital-account transfers, including capital escrow injections, repatriation of contributed capital, and redemption of partnership interests, may be subject to Investment Canada Act notification thresholds if a non-Canadian acquires control of the entity's Canadian business, but no currency conversion license is required. Restrictions only apply to prohibited transactions under the Criminal Code, United Nations Act sanctions, and the Corruption of Foreign Public Officials Act, as well as to payments to designated persons under the Canadian Services (Special Economic Measures) Regulations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender, mandated conversion, or holding-period restriction applies to export proceeds in Canada. A webtoon syndication LP may hold U.S. dollars, euros, Korean won, or other settlement currencies in foreign-currency bank accounts at Canadian Schedule I banks without conversion obligation. The only compliance duties are the accurate recognition of foreign-exchange gains or losses for income-tax purposes under the Income Tax Act and the provision of annual T3010/T5013 filings. For corporate treasury clarity, the LP should adopt a written netting and conversion policy that requires all export receipts from Apple and Google platform settlements to be converted to CAD on a monthly cycle via the operating bank's dealer desk, unless otherwise hedged.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) with prudential oversight delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); sanctions and anti-money laundering enforcement coordinated through FINTRAC and Global Affairs Canada. Canada maintains no dedicated foreign-exchange control administration; reserve management and currency stability functions are exercised by the Bank of Canada under the Currency Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility for offshore digital EMI platforms is rated LOW-to-MEDIUM. Offshore electronic money institutions cannot independently provide true CAD-clearing or transparent account locomotion in Canada because only Payments Canada members (Schedule I banks, credit unions, and authorized payment service providers) have direct access to the Lynx and ACSS clearing rails. An offshore EMI can act as a front-end collection and disbursement engine only if it contracts with a regulated Canadian bank as its settlement agent and if the entity itself registers as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Additional legal constraints include the Bank Act prohibition against deposit-taking without authorization, fingerprinting and compliance obligations under the Counter-Terrorist Financing regime, and Alberta's provincial consumer protection disclosure rules. Consequently, the prudent architecture is to open a primary CAD operational account at a Tier 1 bank and complement it with a non-resident USD/EUR collection account at a Luxembourg or U.K.-regulated EMI only for payouts to non-Canadian webtoon creators, with the EMI holding no Canadian clearing role.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility for offshore digital EMI platforms is rated LOW-to-MEDIUM. Offshore electronic money institutions cannot independently provide true CAD-clearing or transparent account locomotion in Canada because only Payments Canada members (Schedule I banks, credit unions, and authorized payment service providers) have direct access to the Lynx and ACSS clearing rails. An offshore EMI can act as a front-end collection and disbursement engine only if it contracts with a regulated Canadian bank as its settlement agent and if the entity itself registers as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Additional legal constraints include the Bank Act prohibition against deposit-taking without authorization, fingerprinting and compliance obligations under the Counter-Terrorist Financing regime, and Alberta's provincial consumer protection disclosure rules. Consequently, the prudent architecture is to open a primary CAD operational account at a Tier 1 bank and complement it with a non-resident USD/EUR collection account at a Luxembourg or U.K.-regulated EMI only for payouts to non-Canadian webtoon creators, with the EMI holding no Canadian clearing role.

Stage 06

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal corporate income tax plus 8% Alberta general corporate income tax, producing a combined general corporate rate of 23% where a corporate partner is taxable in Alberta. An Alberta limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and does not itself pay corporate income tax; taxable income is allocated to its partners.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited to non-residents, subject to an applicable income tax treaty. A limited partnership does not ordinarily pay dividends; distributions are generally partnership distributions or allocations. Tax treatment depends on the partner's status, the character of the amount, and whether the distribution includes income already allocated for Canadian tax purposes.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T5013 information return is generally due within 5 months after the partnership fiscal period-end. A partnership with a December 31 year-end generally files by May 31 of the following year. Corporate partners generally file T2 returns within 6 months after their taxation year-end and pay any balance within 2 months after year-end, subject to the 3-month payment rule for eligible Canadian-controlled private corporations.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Issue and file the applicable information return by the last day of February following the calendar year in which the amount was paid or credited. Remit required withholding within the applicable CRA remittance period.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate value of non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents generally triggers the Canadian contemporaneous documentation threshold for the relevant taxpayer and taxation year. Canada requires contemporaneous documentation under section 247 of the Income Tax Act but does not use a universal OECD-style Local File/Master File filing requirement for all taxpayers. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of a multinational enterprise group with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone United Kingdom-style or Cayman-style Economic Substance Regulations applicable generally to Alberta limited partnerships. Canadian tax outcomes are nevertheless tested under the arm's-length transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule in section 245 of the Income Tax Act, specific anti-avoidance provisions, beneficial-ownership principles, sham and substance-over-form doctrines, partnership allocation rules, and permanent-establishment concepts. A partnership or related company should maintain genuine commercial functions, decision-making, personnel or outsourced capacity, intellectual-property control, risk assumption, books and records, and bank-account activity consistent with its stated business.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act may result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to 5 years, reassessment of tax, civil penalties, gross-negligence penalties, and interest. False statements and omissions may also trigger third-party and preparer penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, signing officers, and persons who participated in the conduct may face prosecution and personal criminal exposure. Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions. Civil tax debt does not ordinarily create an automatic travel ban, but court orders, bail conditions, arrest warrants, or immigration consequences may restrict travel in individual cases.
Failure to Withhold or Remit Non-Resident Tax

Statutory sanction: CRA may assess the payer for the unremitted withholding, applicable penalties, prescribed interest, and information-return penalties. Treaty reduction without valid residence and beneficial-ownership evidence may be denied retroactively.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted source deductions under section 227.1 if statutory conditions are met, including failure of the corporation to remit and lack of due diligence.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust Canadian income under section 247, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory threshold is met, deny deductions, apply withholding tax, and charge interest. Contemporaneous-documentation penalties may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for an ordinary transfer-pricing adjustment, but personal exposure may arise for fraud, knowing participation, source-deduction failures, or criminal tax evasion.
Artificial Partnership Allocation and Limited-Partner Abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA may reallocate partnership income, deny losses, apply the general anti-avoidance rule, assess tax and interest, and impose penalties for false statements or gross negligence. A partnership may also lose intended flow-through treatment for particular arrangements.

Director personal liability: General partners and persons controlling the arrangement may face civil restitution, reassessment, and criminal liability where deceit or evasion is established. Limited liability does not protect a person from personal wrongdoing.
Shell Company and Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: Treaty benefits may be denied under treaty limitation-on-benefits or principal-purpose rules, domestic anti-avoidance provisions, and beneficial-ownership analysis. CRA may reassess withholding, penalties, interest, and related corporate tax.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly arrange sham ownership, false residence evidence, or deceptive treaty claims may face civil penalties and criminal prosecution. A general automatic director travel ban does not arise solely from treaty-shopping allegations.
Corporate Transparency, Beneficial Ownership, and AML

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include account closure, suspicious-transaction reporting, FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, provincial corporate-registry consequences, asset restraint or forfeiture, and criminal prosecution under applicable anti-money-laundering and proceeds-of-crime legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, beneficial owners, and officers may face personal criminal exposure, fines, imprisonment, forfeiture, and regulatory disqualification where they authorize or facilitate prohibited conduct. Travel restrictions may arise from criminal process, warrants, sanctions, or bail conditions, not automatically from an AML review.
Unlicensed Money Services, Payment, or Sanctions Activity

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC registration violations may produce significant administrative monetary penalties; sanctions violations may result in asset freezes, criminal charges, substantial fines, and imprisonment under Canadian sanctions legislation and the Criminal Code.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors and officers may be prosecuted personally where they consented to, authorized, or participated in the conduct. Corporate separateness does not shield intentional criminal conduct.
Intellectual-Property Misappropriation and Copyright Infringement

Statutory sanction: Remedies may include injunctions, statutory damages, compensatory damages, delivery-up or destruction of infringing materials, accounting of profits, legal costs, platform takedowns, and criminal penalties for commercial-scale infringement.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally liable where they authorize, direct, or knowingly participate in infringement. Criminal copyright exposure may include fines and imprisonment; ordinary corporate infringement does not automatically pierce the corporate veil.
Books, Records, and Audit-Trail Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny deductions, impose record-keeping and information penalties, assess tax using available information, extend reassessment periods for misrepresentation or fraud, and issue compliance orders.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may face personal penalties or criminal exposure for obstruction, destruction of records, false evidence, or knowing participation in non-compliance. Civil tax liability remains primarily at the taxpayer or withholding-agent level unless a statutory personal-liability rule applies.
