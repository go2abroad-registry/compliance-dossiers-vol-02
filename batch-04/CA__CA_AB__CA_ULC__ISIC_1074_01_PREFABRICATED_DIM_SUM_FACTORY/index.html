Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – Business Registration Online (BRO) portal; Alberta corporate income tax is administered by the CRA under the Alberta Tax Collection Agreement.

E-government filing portal

For an Alberta Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC), after incorporation under the Business Corporations Act (Alberta), the ULC must obtain a Business Number (BN) from the CRA via the BRO or by completing Form RC1. The BN serves as the primary tax identification number. The ULC must then open program accounts for Corporate Income Tax, GST/HST, and Payroll Deductions if applicable. Registration can be completed online, by telephone, or by mail. No separate Alberta TIN is required; Alberta taxes are collected through the CRA BN system.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No check-the-box election exists under Canadian domestic law. The ULC is taxed as a corporation under the Income Tax Act. Hybrid mismatch rules under sections 18.4 and 18.5 of the Income Tax Act may require adjustments if the ULC is treated as a fiscally transparent entity in a foreign jurisdiction. Under the Canada–US tax treaty (Article IV(7)), the ULC may be treated as a corporation for treaty purposes upon certain elections agreed by the competent authorities. Also, the ULC may elect under subsection 89(2) to be deemed a Canadian resident corporation in specific circumstances.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies made in Canada, except for non-residents without Canadian nexus; voluntary registration is available for persons with zero-rated or exempt supplies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

For goods, a supply is zero-rated if the goods are exported by the supplier or delivered to a common carrier for export (sections 15, 16, and 20 of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act). For services, a supply to a non-resident is zero-rated under section 7 of Schedule VI, except services performed in respect of Canadian real property, tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time the service is performed, or services rendered to a non-resident individual physically present in Canada. A fabricated dim sum factory exporting frozen goods qualifies for zero-rating when the export documentary requirements are met.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Border Services Agency Form B13A)
  • Bill of Lading or air/sea waybill
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank confirmation of foreign exchange settlement
  • Commercial Invoice
  • Packing List

No real-time fiscal clearance, API-based tax e-invoicing, or digital stamping regime exists in Canada. Suppliers must retain electronic records and issue prescribed invoices under the Excise Tax Act, but the CRA does not require prior approval or real-time validation of invoices. The Pan-Canadian e-invoicing standard is voluntary.

The CRA may deny zero-rating if proper documentary proof is not maintained in the exporter's books. A claim that is not substantiated is subject to reassessment and clawback of the tax benefit within the normal four-year reassessment period, which is extended in cases of negligence or misrepresentation. Penalties include a gross negligence penalty equal to 50% of the understated net tax, statutory interest, and criminal prosecution for fraud, with fines of up to 200% of the evaded tax and potential imprisonment.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada's currency, the Canadian dollar (CAD), is freely convertible on both current-account and capital-account transactions. There are no general restrictions on profit remittance, dividend repatriation, or capital outflows; however, interest, dividends, and royalties paid to non-residents are subject to Canadian Part XIII withholding tax at 25% absent an applicable treaty reduction. An Alberta Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) may be treated as a flow-through entity for Canadian tax purposes, but distribution mechanics must still comply with the corporate statute and bank documentation. Inbound investments exceeding Investment Canada Act thresholds require notification or review, but this is not a foreign-exchange control measure.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion period, and no minimum conversion ratio applies in Canada. Exporters must retain proceeds in any freely convertible currency; the only reporting obligations are statistical trade filings under the Customs Act/Statistics Act and cross-border currency or monetary instrument reporting to FINTRAC for movements of CAD 10,000 or more, which is an AML declaration, not a conversion or surrender rule.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada / Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) / Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); Canada maintains no dedicated Foreign Exchange Administration and no exchange-control approval authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low-Medium legal viability for offshore Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) and non-bank digital payment providers. Canadian banking law reserves deposit-taking to institutions licensed as banks or obtaining an order under the Bank Act; foreign EMIs generally cannot hold Canadian retail deposits or offer CAD-denominated clearing accounts without establishment as a Canadian bank or partnership with a licensed Canadian financial institution. The Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) prescribes a registration regime for payment service providers (PSPs), but this does not confer deposit-taking authority. For a frozen-food manufacturing ULC needing trade LCs and SWIFT wires, a purely offshore EMI is not a legal substitute for a Canadian-tier bank account.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound capital injection into the Alberta ULC must be routed through the Canadian banking system using SWIFT MT103 for the cross-border leg. The wire should be titled as 'Capital Contribution' with a clear purpose code, generally 'G2 - Direct Investment or Capital Contributions' or the receiving bank's equivalent country-purpose classification, and must include the ULC's account number and legal entity name. Canadian banks will require the underlying corporate resolution, shareholder register amendment, and a confirmatory cost-surface: the injection may be equity subscription or intercompany debt; for equity, no Canadian withholding applies; for intercompany debt, Part XIII withholding on interest applies to a non-resident lender unless a treaty relief or arm's-length rate is documented. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal corporate income tax is generally 15% after the federal abatement. Alberta's general corporate income tax rate is 8%. The combined general rate is therefore 23% for a corporation that is not eligible for the Canadian-controlled private corporation small-business regime. A qualifying Alberta CCPC may generally access a combined rate of approximately 11% on eligible active business income up to the applicable business limit, subject to federal and Alberta conditions.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The domestic Canadian withholding rate on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident is generally 25%. A qualifying treaty may reduce the rate, commonly to 5% for a corporate shareholder meeting ownership and voting thresholds and 15% for other qualifying treaty residents. The applicable treaty, beneficial ownership, anti-avoidance provisions, and any limitation-on-benefits rule must be verified.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 and AT1 returns are generally due six months after the end of each taxation year. Corporate tax balances are generally due two months after year-end. A qualifying CCPC may generally have three months to pay its balance if statutory conditions are satisfied, including the relevant taxable-income and association conditions. Filing and payment deadlines are separate.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada generally requires contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. The commonly applicable documentation exemption is available where the corporation's gross revenue for the year is less than CAD 10 million, subject to statutory conditions and exceptions. Canada does not impose a universal separate Local File and Master File filing requirement in the same form used by some jurisdictions. A Master File and Country-by-Country Report may be relevant for constituent entities of multinational enterprise groups meeting the OECD CbC threshold of EUR 750 million consolidated group revenue, with Canadian filing and notification requirements. Form T106 reporting thresholds and transfer-pricing documentation thresholds are separate.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no general standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the United Arab Emirates or certain other jurisdictions. Substance remains relevant under the Income Tax Act, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership analysis, transfer-pricing rules, debt characterization, foreign-affiliate rules, and judicial doctrines concerning sham transactions and commercial reality. The ULC should maintain Canadian decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational capacity, manufacturing assets, inventory controls, quality systems, risk assumption, financing records, and commercially documented functions consistent with its remuneration. A paper-only ULC receiving management fees, royalties, financing income, or treaty benefits presents a high challenge risk.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Return

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment, tax, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalty generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, and possible prosecution. Tax evasion can result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years on indictment under the Income Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal liability where they participated in, authorized, acquiesced in, or directed the conduct. Corporate status does not protect deliberate personal misconduct. A conviction may produce restitution orders, probation, asset restraints, and immigration or travel consequences under general law, but there is no automatic tax-evasion travel ban.
Failure to Remit Trust Amounts

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unremitted amount, interest, penalties, and potential criminal prosecution. Directors can be jointly and severally liable for corporate source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts unless the statutory due-diligence defence is established.

Director personal liability: Director liability can survive dissolution or insolvency subject to statutory limitation periods and procedural requirements. Directors must establish reasonable care, diligence, and skill in implementing controls and responding to known remittance failures.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Reassessment under the Income Tax Act, denial or adjustment of deductions, imputed income, interest, and a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, including a material failure threshold. Secondary tax, withholding, customs, and GST/HST consequences may also arise.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally exposed for knowingly participating in false records, fraud, or tax evasion. Ordinary transfer-pricing adjustments do not automatically create personal civil liability or pierce the corporate veil.
Treaty Abuse, Conduit Arrangement, or Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty relief, withholding at 25%, payer liability for short withholding, interest, penalties, reassessment under the general anti-avoidance rule, and possible application of treaty principal-purpose or limitation-on-benefits provisions.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure if they knowingly approve sham or fraudulent arrangements. A ULC's hybrid classification must not be used to claim inconsistent tax treatment without documented treaty analysis.
Unlawful Corporate Distribution and Insolvency Misconduct

Statutory sanction: Repayment or recovery of unlawful distributions, creditor remedies, oppression or derivative claims, insolvency proceedings, director indemnity exposure, and potential civil or criminal consequences for fraud or breach of fiduciary duty.

Director personal liability: Alberta corporate law may impose personal liability for authorized unlawful distributions, unpaid wages in prescribed circumstances, oppressive conduct, fraudulent conveyances, and knowing participation in breaches. Corporate veil piercing is exceptional and fact-dependent, not automatic.
Corporate Registry and Beneficial-Ownership Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative penalties, loss of good standing, inability to obtain certificates, prosecution, court orders, and potential dissolution or cancellation depending on the default.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur statutory penalties and liability for knowingly false filings. Concealment of beneficial ownership can also elevate tax, AML, sanctions, and financial-institution de-risking exposure.
Anti-Money Laundering and Sanctions

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, seizure or restraint of proceeds, criminal money-laundering penalties, forfeiture, and sanctions-related prosecution. Criminal money laundering can attract substantial fines and imprisonment, with the maximum depending on the offence and circumstances.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers can be prosecuted for knowing participation, counselling, or wilful blindness. A bank may freeze or reject transactions and file a suspicious transaction report. There is no automatic director travel ban, but criminal orders, bail conditions, forfeiture, immigration consequences, or sanctions restrictions may limit travel.
Food Manufacturing, Excise, and Product-Compliance Tax Risk

Statutory sanction: Licence suspension, product detention or recall, administrative penalties, customs reassessment, GST/HST and income-tax reassessment, seizure, prosecution, and business interruption.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for statutory offences where they authorized, directed, or failed to take reasonable steps to prevent the conduct. Environmental, food-safety, and workplace offences may create separate personal prosecution risks.
Record Retention and Destruction of Evidence

Statutory sanction: Extended audit, adverse evidentiary inferences, failure-to-keep-records penalties, denial of deductions or credits, obstruction charges, and prosecution where destruction is intentional.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally exposed for obstruction, misleading investigators, or directing destruction of records. Corporate dissolution does not erase record-retention and cooperation obligations.
