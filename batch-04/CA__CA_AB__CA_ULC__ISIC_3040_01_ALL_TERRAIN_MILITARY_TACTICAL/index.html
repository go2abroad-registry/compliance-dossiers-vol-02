Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — accessed through the 'My Business Account' online portal. Alberta corporate income tax is administered by the CRA under the federal-provincial tax collection agreement; federal Goods and Services Tax / Harmonized Sales Tax (GST/HST) is also administered by the CRA. Alberta does not levy a provincial sales tax and does not maintain a separate tax identification regime. The Business Number (BN) serves as the single integrated tax identifier for federal and Alberta corporate tax, GST/HST, and other CRA-administered accounts.

E-government filing portal

For a non-resident-owned Alberta Unlimited Liability Company (ULC): (1) incorporate or continue the ULC under the Business Corporations Act (Alberta) and obtain articles of incorporation; (2) file Form RC1A, Request for a Business Number and Tax Account Registrations, with the CRA, or use the CRA's online Business Registration service; (3) activate the corporate income tax account, the GST/HST account, and any payroll or excise accounts required by actual operations; (4) appoint a Canadian representative for service of process if the ULC has no physical presence in Canada, and provide such agent's information to the CRA during account intake; (5) obtain the BN, GST/HST registration number, and CRA program account numbers (e.g., 'C' for corporate tax, 'RT' for GST/HST); (6) for controlled military goods, obtain any required registrations under the Export and Import Permits Act and Global Affairs Canada exporter database. No separate Alberta corporate tax identification is issued; Alberta corporate tax obligations are reported through the CRA BN.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible (for cross-border check-the-box and hybrid purposes, given the ULC's unlimited shareholder liability structure). Under the Canadian Income Tax Act, however, the ULC remains an entity-level taxpayer by default and is not treated as a pass-through vehicle unless a foreign jurisdiction's classification election is recognized; Canada has no domestic check-the-box regime. — For a non-resident-owned Alberta Unlimited Liability Company (ULC): (1) incorporate or continue the ULC under the Business Corporations Act (Alberta) and obtain articles of incorporation; (2) file Form RC1A, Request for a Business Number and Tax Account Registrations, with the CRA, or use the CRA's online Business Registration service; (3) activate the corporate income tax account, the GST/HST account, and any payroll or excise accounts required by actual operations; (4) appoint a Canadian representative for service of process if the ULC has no physical presence in Canada, and provide such agent's information to the CRA during account intake; (5) obtain the BN, GST/HST registration number, and CRA program account numbers (e.g., 'C' for corporate tax, 'RT' for GST/HST); (6) for controlled military goods, obtain any required registrations under the Export and Import Permits Act and Global Affairs Canada exporter database. No separate Alberta corporate tax identification is issued; Alberta corporate tax obligations are reported through the CRA BN.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible (for cross-border check-the-box and hybrid purposes, given the ULC's unlimited shareholder liability structure). Under the Canadian Income Tax Act, however, the ULC remains an entity-level taxpayer by default and is not treated as a pass-through vehicle unless a foreign jurisdiction's classification election is recognized; Canada has no domestic check-the-box regime.

Available election forms

No domestic Canadian check-the-box election exists under the Income Tax Act. A ULC may nonetheless be elected as a disregarded entity or partnership under U.S. Treasury Regulation §301.7701-3 if it is 'eligible' and meets the check-the-box criteria, which is feasible for an Alberta ULC because unlimited liability prevents 'per se corporation' status in the U.S. classification context. Such election creates hybrid entity implications; Canada's response to hybrid mismatches is contained in Income Tax Act sections 18.4, 18.6, and 20.4, applicable to Canadian-resident shareholders or related entities. The ULC may also be classified differently for treaty purposes, but no statutory election is available to treat the ULC as a partnership or sole proprietorship for Canadian federal tax filings without an actual foreign tax classification event and proper reporting of all applicable information returns.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

$30,000 CAD in gross revenue from taxable supplies — mandatory GST/HST registration for a non-resident carrying on business in Canada when taxable supplies in Canada exceed $30,000 over any four consecutive calendar quarters (or a single calendar quarter if the threshold is exceeded in that quarter). Additional mandatory registration applies to non-residents that solicit offers or orders for taxable supplies in Canada through an employee or agent, irrespective of the $30,000 threshold. Since the ULC's all-terrain tactical vehicle sales to Canadian or foreign governments may involve Canadian supply chains, an Indian/foreign exporter should assess its place-of-supply rules before relying on the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to supplies of tangible goods that are exported by the supplier or on the supplier's behalf, provided the goods are removed from Canada 'as soon as a reasonable period after the supply' and are not further processed, manufactured, or used in Canada. For military tactical all-terrain vehicles under ISIC 3040 / NAICS 336992, the criteria are: (a) the purchaser is a non-resident and the goods are destined to a foreign address or military installation outside Canada; (b) the goods leave Canada under a valid customs export declaration (Form B13A or electronic equivalent); (c) full sale proceeds are received in foreign currency or Canadian dollars from a foreign purchaser; (d) any applicable export permit (including controlled goods authorization under the Export and Import Permits Act) is obtained before export; and (e) the supplier retains contemporaneous transport and payment records. Services supplied to a non-resident recipient are generally zero-rated if they are not performed in connection with real or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of supply, or if the recipient is not resident in Canada and the service is not primarily for consumption in Canada. Warranty, maintenance, and technical documentation services delivered offshore to a foreign military are zero-rated if the recipient is non-resident.

  • Customs Export Declaration — Canada Border Services Agency Form B13A Export Declaration or the electronic Canadian Export Reporting System record, bearing the exporter's BN and a unique transaction reference
  • Bill of Lading / Through Airway Bill — signed carrier document evidencing loading and export from Canada to the foreign destination
  • Commercial Invoice — fully itemized invoice showing the purchaser, export description, tariff classification, quantities, unit values, and the sales contract reference
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / proof of foreign payment — bank SWIFT MT103, wire transfer advice, or letter of credit settlement evidencing receipt of payments from the non-resident purchaser
  • Transportation evidence — dock receipt, truck bill of lading, railroad waybill, or cargo manifest showing the inland and outward movement from Canada
  • Export Permit — where the vehicles are controlled under Canada's Export and Import Permits Act, a valid export permit issued by Global Affairs Canada, together with end-use and end-user certification

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or API-based electronic invoicing for CRA tax clearance. Electronic invoices are acceptable if they are made available to the CRA in a legible, machine-readable format and contain the prescribed GST/HST information: vendor legal name and BN, transaction date, description, GST/HST amount or rate, and any exemption or zero-rating indicator. The Pan-Canadian Trust Framework for B2B e-invoicing is a voluntary interoperability model, not a statutory mandate. CRA has no pre-clearance or per-transaction validation gate for GST/HST invoices.

The CRA may reassess a zero-rated supply as taxable at 5% GST if the exporter fails to produce the required export documentation within six years from the assessment year; in the case of wilful misrepresentation, the limitation period is open-ended. Penalties under the Excise Tax Act include: (a) section 280.1 penalties for late or incorrect remittances, up to 6% of the amount; (b) Part IX false statements or omissions — 25% of the understated GST where the error is made knowingly or under circumstances of gross negligence, and up to 40% of the tax evaded in fraud cases; (c) section 285.1 failure to comply with an order or requirement, up to $25,000 per day for continued non-compliance; and (d) criminal prosecution under section 239 for tax evasion on the GST/HST portion — fines up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment. The CRA also has authority to claw back input tax credits claimed on exported vehicles if zero-rating is overturned, with interest from the date the input tax credit was recognized. Separate enforcement may include denial or revocation of export permits under the Export and Import Permits Act, customs penalties under the Customs Act (up to $25,000 per violation), and referral to the Public Prosecution Service of Canada for export-control offenses involving military tactical hardware.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada holds full current and capital account convertibility under IMF Article VIII; the Canadian dollar floats freely with no state-administered rate, no import/export FX licensing, and no restriction on repatriation of dividends, distributions, interest, or capital. Profit remittance friction arises exclusively from non-treaty withholding tax exposure under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (R.S.C. 1985, c.1) and from the Investment Canada Act net-benefit review for non-resident acquisitions exceeding applicable financial thresholds. The Alberta ULC structure is pass-through eligible for non-resident members only under foreign tax elections; Canadian treasury policy must not assume that tax transparency of a ULC converts Canadian legal cash-flow rights into zero-risk remittance mechanics. For defense-related cash flows, in-scope export permits under the Export and Import Permits Act must accompany all currencies moving for military-offset conditions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender, conversion, or repatriation timeline applies to Canadian export receipts; exporters may retain foreign currency in domestic or offshore accounts without a mandatory conversion ratio. Mandatory compliance is information-based: report cross-border electronic funds transfers equal to or exceeding C$10,000 to FINTRAC, and file controlled-goods and military-commodity documentation with Global Affairs Canada and the Controlled Goods Directorate. Defense-export proceeds must be traceable to invoiced trade shipments and must not be commingled with political-risk mitigation funds without a written treasury policy.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary authority; foreign exchange administration is shared between the Department of Finance Canada (policy), the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) (bank supervision), FINTRAC (transaction monitoring), and Global Affairs Canada (export controls). Canada maintains no dedicated central foreign-exchange-control agency.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Negligible as a primary rail. Canada does not maintain an electronic-money-institution (EMI) licensing category; a non-resident fintech must register as a money services business (MSB) under the PCMLTFA and is prohibited from taking deposits without OSFI authorization. Offshore EMIs cannot access Lynx or the Canadian real-time settlement systems directly, do not issue defense-grade trade finance instruments, and their correspondent networks repeatedly reject military-sector payments. Use only as a supplementary payroll or contingency account and never for sovereign LC or capital escrow flows.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory Form T106 filing if aggregate annual non-arm's length cross-border transactions exceed C$1,000,000. Contemporaneous Local File and Master File documentation is expected when the level of cross-border transactions exceeds C$5,000,000 or when the gross revenue criterion is met (CRA penalty threshold under subsection 247(2)).

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR). Economic substance is evaluated through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR), the OECD Multilateral Instrument (MLI) Principal Purpose Test (PPT), and the statutory substance-over-form doctrine. A ULC engaged in military vehicle manufacturing must maintain tangible premises, qualified personnel, operational autonomy, and actual board decision-making in Alberta; otherwise, treaty benefits may be denied and transfer pricing assessments may be imposed.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Willful False Statements)

Statutory sanction: 50% gross negligence civil penalty; criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act with a fine of up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to 5 years; court-ordered restitution.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly instruct or participate in evasion are personally liable for the penalties, may have personal assets seized, may be subject to travel bans (the Passport Office may refuse/revoke passports), and face extradition proceedings where applicable.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA denies treaty-reduced withholding rates and re-assesses Part XIII tax at 25%; GAAR may be applied to annul tax benefits; a 50% misrepresentation penalty and late-payment interest are typically assessed. Defense procurement contracts may be revoked under industrial security regulations.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII withholding tax, penalties, and interest. The CRA may enforce against personal bank accounts and immovable property; severe cases trigger criminal prosecution for false attestation.
AML / Sanctions Non-Compliance (PCMLTFA)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to C$1,000,000 per violation for corporations; criminal money-laundering offence punishable by imprisonment up to 10 years and a fine at the court's discretion. The Director of Public Prosecutions may bring indictment.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly fail to comply or who participate in laundering proceeds are exposed to criminal liability, including imprisonment, personal fines, disqualification from acting as a director under the Canada Business Corporations Act, and seizure of personal assets.
