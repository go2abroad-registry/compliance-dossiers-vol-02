Tax ID Application Infrastructure

      

        
Competent tax authority
Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; digital business portal: My Business Account and GST/HST NETFILE (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html)

        
E-government filing portal
A foreign-owned unlimited liability company (ULC) incorporated under the Alberta Business Corporations Act must first complete provincial corporate registration to obtain its Alberta Corporation Certificate. It then obtains a federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA through the Business Registration Online service or Form RC370. The ULC must open a separate BN account for GST/HST (RT) and Corporate Income Tax (RC). For non-resident owners, the same BN/account structure applies; foreign entities may also apply by mail with Form RC371. Registration must be completed before making taxable supplies exceeding the small supplier threshold or within the filing deadlines of the CRA.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

      

        
Default tax classification
Pass_Through_Eligible

        
Available election forms
Under Canadian domestic law, an Alberta ULC is a separate legal corporation taxed at the entity level under the Income Tax Act. However, for U.S. federal tax purposes, the Canadian ULC constitutes an 'eligible entity' under U.S. Treasury Regulation §301.7701-3 and may elect on IRS Form 8832 to be treated as a disregarded entity (single member) or as a partnership (multiple members), thereby enabling pass-through taxation. Canada's hybrid mismatch rules (including excessive interest and financing restrictions) follow BEPS Action 2 and generally disallow deductions for hybrid mismatches; no elective exemption or domestic check-the-box election exists under the Canadian Income Tax Act.

Standard VAT / GST rate
5%

        Export 0% zero-rating conditions
        
Zero-rating of exported goods is available under Division III of the Excise Tax Act (s.21) when the GST registrant actually exports tangible personal property by shipping it to a destination outside Canada, and the supplier retains evidence in the ordinary course of business proving the export. Services may be zero-rated under s.9 and s.23 when supplied to a non-resident person and performed wholly outside Canada, or in respect of real estate located outside Canada. The exporter must establish export through documentation, and the supply must not be stripped of commercial substance or artificially routed to avoid GST.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

      

        
Current-account convertibility
The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for ordinary current-account and capital-account transactions. Canada generally permits non-resident ownership, inbound equity, shareholder loans, dividends, interest, royalties, service fees, and liquidation proceeds, subject to corporate authorization, solvency requirements, withholding tax, transfer-pricing rules, treaty entitlement, beneficial-ownership verification, sanctions screening, and bank anti-money-laundering review. An Alberta unlimited liability corporation remains legally liable for its obligations; its tax classification and cross-border treatment must be separately confirmed under Canadian and investor-jurisdiction rules. Canadian-resident corporations generally face 25% withholding tax on dividends, interest, royalties, and certain other payments to non-residents, subject to statutory exemptions, domestic rules, and applicable tax treaties. Dividends must be lawfully declared and paid only when permitted by the Alberta Business Corporations Act and the corporation's financial position.

        
Export proceeds surrender rules
Canada has no general statutory requirement to surrender export proceeds to a Canadian bank, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no universal statutory deadline for converting foreign-currency export receipts. Exporters may retain foreign currency in Canada or offshore, subject to contractual, tax, accounting, sanctions, customs, and anti-money-laundering obligations. Goods exporters must maintain accurate commercial invoices, customs records, shipping evidence, and books and records. Banks may impose contractual settlement, documentation, or reporting requirements, and foreign jurisdictions may impose separate collection or repatriation controls.

      

      
Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no general foreign exchange administration requiring approval for ordinary commercial currency conversion, capital movements, or profit remittances. Financial institutions remain subject to the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, sanctions legislation, tax reporting rules, and applicable Bank of Canada, OSFI, FINTRAC, and payment-system requirements.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

      
      

        Non-resident shareholder, director, or ultimate beneficial owner with no Canadian operating history
        
Root cause: Non-resident shareholder, director, or ultimate beneficial owner with no Canadian operating history

        
Defense evidence: Certified articles and corporate registry profile; Alberta registered-office evidence; director and shareholder registers; ownership chart to natural persons; passports and residential-address evidence; tax-residency self-certifications; board resolution; business plan; explanation of Canadian commercial purpose; and evidence of expected Canadian operations.

      

      
      

        Alberta unlimited liability corporation structure perceived as unusual or tax-driven
        
Root cause: Alberta unlimited liability corporation structure perceived as unusual or tax-driven

        
Defense evidence: Alberta incorporation certificate; current corporate registry search; governing articles and bylaws; legal memorandum explaining unlimited liability status and authority of signatories; parent-company organizational chart; solvency and capitalization evidence; and written explanation of the intended commercial and tax treatment without overstating pass-through status.

      

      
      

        Incoming shareholder equity or shareholder-loan funds from an offshore account
        
Root cause: Incoming shareholder equity or shareholder-loan funds from an offshore account

        
Defense evidence: Executed subscription or loan agreement; board approval; capitalization table; originating-bank statements; source-of-funds and source-of-wealth evidence; audited or management financial statements of the funder; beneficial-owner identification; wire instructions; and transaction-purpose narrative matching the accounting treatment.

      

      
      

        Manufacturing and international trade activity with unclear counterparties or goods classification
        
Root cause: Manufacturing and international trade activity with unclear counterparties or goods classification

        
Defense evidence: Detailed product catalogue; bills of materials; supplier and customer contracts; purchase orders; commercial invoices; shipping and customs documents; HS classifications; country-of-origin records; sanctions screening; end-use and end-user statements; expected countries, currencies, volumes, and monthly transaction counts.

      

      
      

        High foreign-currency turnover, rapid pass-through payments, or payments unrelated to the stated business
        
Root cause: High foreign-currency turnover, rapid pass-through payments, or payments unrelated to the stated business

        
Defense evidence: Treasury policy; rolling cash-flow forecast; bank-account map; approved counterparty list; segregation-of-duties matrix; invoice-to-payment reconciliation; payment-approval workflow; explanation of working-capital cycles; and documented limits for merchant, supplier, payroll, tax, and intercompany transactions.

      

      
      

        Use of offshore fintech accounts, virtual IBANs, payment aggregators, or third-party settlement accounts
        
Root cause: Use of offshore fintech accounts, virtual IBANs, payment aggregators, or third-party settlement accounts

        
Defense evidence: Provider licensing and regulatory-status evidence; service agreement; account ownership confirmation; safeguarding terms; settlement-flow diagram; reconciliation controls; beneficial-owner evidence; customer and supplier contracts; and documented prohibition on undisclosed third-party funds.

      

      
      

        Incomplete tax-residency, CRS, FATCA, or beneficial-ownership information
        
Root cause: Incomplete tax-residency, CRS, FATCA, or beneficial-ownership information

        
Defense evidence: CRA business number and tax-account evidence where available; Canadian and foreign tax-residency certifications; CRS self-certifications; FATCA forms where applicable; corporate tax classification analysis; controlling-person forms; and signed confirmation of changes-reporting responsibilities.

      

      
      

        Sanctions, export-control, politically exposed person, adverse-media, or high-risk-country match
        
Root cause: Sanctions, export-control, politically exposed person, adverse-media, or high-risk-country match

        
Defense evidence: Screening results; identity-resolution analysis; proof of no-match; contracts and end-use certificates; ownership and control analysis; sanctions counsel review where necessary; enhanced monitoring plan; and documented escalation approval before processing.

      

      
    

    

      
Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

      

        Route funds from an account held in the documented name of the shareholder, parent, or approved lender to the Alberta corporation's Canadian bank account. Use a written subscription agreement or shareholder-loan agreement, board approval, consistent beneficiary information, and a payment narrative identifying equity contribution, paid-in capital, or shareholder loan. Canada does not impose a universal statutory FX purpose-code system for ordinary commercial wires; however, the sending and receiving banks may require a payment purpose, legal-entity identifier, tax-residency data, source-of-funds evidence, and beneficial-owner information. Avoid third-party remitters unless disclosed and approved. Reconcile the wire to the general ledger, capitalization table, and bank confirmation. Maintain records supporting any later dividend, interest, royalty, management-fee, or principal repayment. For higher-risk or larger transactions, use a commercial documentary letter of credit, standby letter of credit, documentary collection, or bank-guaranteed structure, subject to the applicable ICC rules and the issuing, confirming, and advising banks' credit requirements. Match each payment to purchase orders, invoices, customs and shipping documents, supplier due diligence, HS codes, sanctions screening, and approved payment instructions. Use CAD, USD, EUR, or the contractual settlement currency through a designated operating or foreign-currency account. Stage 06
Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

      
Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

    

    

      
Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)
Federal general corporate income tax rate: 15%; Alberta general corporate income tax rate: 8%; ordinary combined general rate: 23%. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access a 9% federal rate and a 2% Alberta small-business rate on eligible active business income within applicable limits, producing an aggregate rate of approximately 11%. A non-resident-controlled ULC generally does not qualify for the Canadian-controlled private corporation regime.

      
Dividend withholding tax (WHT)
Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident is generally 25%. Under the Canada-United States tax treaty, the rate is generally 5% where the beneficial owner is a company owning at least 10% of the voting power of the Canadian payer, and 15% in other qualifying cases. Other treaties commonly reduce the rate to 5%, 10%, or 15%, depending on ownership and treaty conditions.

    

    

      
Mandatory Return Breakdown

      
      

        
        
Deadline:  · Frequency: 

        
The T2 return is due six months after the end of each taxation year. Any balance owing is generally due two months after year-end. A Canadian-controlled private corporation meeting the prescribed conditions may generally pay its balance up to three months after year-end, subject to instalment and eligibility rules. The filing deadline is not extended by the later payment date.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

      
Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is automatically unnecessary. Under Income Tax Act section 247, contemporaneous documentation is required for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions to support the arm's-length price. Form T106 generally applies where aggregate reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1 million, subject to the statutory rules and exclusions. Canada does not impose a universal domestic Local File/Master File threshold equivalent to all OECD terminology; a Local File and Master File may nevertheless be appropriate or required under specific reporting frameworks, including country-by-country reporting for an international group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year.

      
Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a separate general Economic Substance Regulation regime equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Canadian tax outcomes are tested through the Income Tax Act, the general anti-avoidance rule in section 245, the sham doctrine, specific anti-avoidance rules, transfer-pricing rules, beneficial-ownership principles, treaty anti-abuse provisions, and common-law business-purpose and factual-substance analysis. A ULC should maintain Canadian decision-making, personnel, premises, manufacturing capability, inventory controls, risk assumption, capitalization, commercial contracts, and bank-account evidence consistent with its claimed functions. A foreign tax classification as a pass-through entity does not by itself establish Canadian pass-through treatment or treaty entitlement.

      

    

    

      
Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

      
      

        Tax evasion and false tax reporting
        
Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act section 239 offences may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to two years, or both. Gross-negligence penalties are generally 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed credit. Tax, interest, reassessment, and civil penalties remain payable independently.

        
Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face prosecution where they personally participated, authorized, acquiesced in, or connived in the offence. A corporation does not shield deliberate criminal conduct. Directors may also face civil recovery, asset seizure, restitution orders, and prohibition consequences. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty but may arise from arrest, bail, immigration, or enforcement proceedings.

      

      
      

        Failure to remit payroll source deductions and Part XIII withholding
        
Statutory sanction: The corporation may be assessed for the unremitted amount, interest, and statutory penalties. Repeated or deliberate failures can produce enhanced penalties and prosecution. Income Tax Act section 227.1 may impose personal director liability for unremitted payroll source deductions, subject to statutory due-diligence and limitation rules. Part XIII amounts may also be assessed against the payer.

        
Director personal liability: Directors can be jointly and severally liable for eligible unremitted source deductions when statutory conditions are met. Liability can survive resignation if the statutory assessment period and other conditions are satisfied. Civil collection may include garnishment, liens, seizure, and judgment enforcement; imprisonment generally requires a separate criminal conviction.

      

      
      

        GST/HST fraud and improper input tax credits
        
Statutory sanction: Assessments may include tax, interest, civil penalties, gross-negligence penalties, and denial of credits. Criminal offences under the Excise Tax Act may result in substantial fines and imprisonment. Directors may be exposed to derivative liability for certain unremitted amounts where statutory conditions apply.

        
Director personal liability: Personal liability may arise for directors who authorized or participated in fraudulent GST/HST conduct, and for statutory remittance liabilities where the prescribed director-liability rules apply. Corporate dissolution does not automatically eliminate assessed liabilities.

      

      
      

        Transfer-pricing manipulation
        
Statutory sanction: CRA may make a section 247 transfer-pricing adjustment, impose a 10% transfer-pricing penalty on the adjustment where statutory conditions are met, deny unsupported deductions, assess withholding tax, and charge interest. Contemporaneous documentation may reduce penalty exposure but does not guarantee acceptance.

        
Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable merely because a transfer-pricing adjustment is made. Exposure increases where directors knowingly approved sham transactions, false records, evasion, fraudulent withholding positions, or distributions made while the corporation was unable to meet statutory obligations.

      

      
      

        Treaty abuse and beneficial-ownership failure
        
Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty relief, reassess withholding at 25%, impose interest and penalties, and apply the treaty principal-purpose test, limitation-on-benefits provisions, domestic general anti-avoidance rule, or beneficial-ownership principles. The payer may remain liable for underwithheld tax.

        
Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal exposure where they knowingly participated in false treaty declarations, deliberate underwithholding, or fraudulent documentation. A mere treaty disagreement ordinarily creates corporate tax liability rather than automatic criminal liability.

      

      
      

        Corporate shell, sham, or inadequate economic substance
        
Statutory sanction: CRA may recharacterize transactions, deny deductions, deny treaty benefits, apply section 245, adjust transfer prices, assess withholding tax, and challenge the factual existence of the claimed business. Corporate registration does not establish tax residence, business purpose, or treaty entitlement.

        
Director personal liability: An Alberta ULC carries unlimited shareholder liability under its governing corporate structure, subject to the applicable articles and statute; this is distinct from judicial piercing of the corporate veil. Directors may face civil restitution, creditor claims, oppression remedies, statutory director liability, and criminal consequences for fraud or tax evasion. Travel bans are not an automatic consequence but may arise in criminal or enforcement proceedings.

      

      
      

        Anti-money-laundering and beneficial-ownership non-compliance
        
Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act may produce FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, transaction restrictions, compliance orders, public naming, and criminal prosecution. Criminal laundering and proceeds offences can carry substantial fines and imprisonment, potentially up to 10 years or more depending on the offence.

        
Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally prosecuted where they direct, authorize, consent to, or participate in laundering, terrorist-financing, fraud, or record falsification. Assets may be restrained, seized, or forfeited. Criminal proceedings may generate bail conditions, passport surrender, travel restrictions, restitution, and imprisonment.

      

      
      

        Corporate insolvency and unlawful distributions
        
Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include corporate recovery actions, director liability under applicable corporate and insolvency legislation, transactions-at-undervalue remedies, preference recovery, tax collection enforcement, and bankruptcy or receivership proceedings.

        
Director personal liability: Directors may face personal civil liability for statutory breaches, wrongful or oppressive conduct, unpaid source deductions, fraudulent conveyances, and knowing participation in asset stripping. The ULC's unlimited-liability structure can expose shareholders to corporate obligations according to its governing articles and applicable Alberta law; it does not automatically convert every corporate debt into a director debt.

      

      
      

        Failure to maintain books, records, and transfer-pricing evidence
        
Statutory sanction: CRA may impose record-keeping penalties, disallow deductions or credits, draw adverse inferences, reassess using estimates, and impose penalties for failure to provide information. The normal reassessment period may be extended or become indefinite in cases involving misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, willful blindness, or fraud.

        
Director personal liability: Directors may face statutory record-production orders, prosecution for obstruction or destruction of records, and civil consequences where inadequate records caused tax loss or creditor prejudice. Imprisonment is generally associated with criminal obstruction, fraud, or tax-evasion facts rather than ordinary record-keeping failures.
