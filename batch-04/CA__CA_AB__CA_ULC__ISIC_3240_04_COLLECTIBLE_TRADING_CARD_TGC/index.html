Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no United States-style check-the-box election for Canadian corporations and no general statutory election that converts an Alberta unlimited liability corporation into a Canadian income-tax pass-through entity. An Alberta ULC is generally a corporation for Canadian income-tax purposes and is subject to Canadian corporate taxation on its taxable income. Its legal form may permit classification as fiscally transparent for United States federal income-tax purposes under U.S. entity-classification rules, but that is a foreign classification and does not change its Canadian tax status. The Canada-U.S. treaty contains specific hybrid-entity and fiscally transparent-entity rules that must be analyzed for treaty benefits, withholding, and income attribution. Canadian elections may apply to particular transactions or regimes, including capital cost allowance, depreciation, loss-utilization, amalgamation, wind-up, functional-currency, and transfer-pricing matters, but none is a general pass-through election for an Alberta ULC.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding supplies of financial services and generally excluding goodwill and supplies of capital property, measured under the small-supplier rules over the applicable single-quarter and preceding four-quarter periods. A non-resident or other person may also be required to register when carrying on commercial activity in Canada and making taxable supplies in Canada under the GST/HST rules. Voluntary registration is generally available before the threshold is exceeded, subject to statutory conditions.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods supplied in Canada and exported from Canada may generally be zero-rated at 0% GST where the statutory export conditions are satisfied, including export by the supplier or by the recipient in circumstances prescribed by the Excise Tax Act and its regulations. For collectible trading cards, the supplier should establish that the goods were exported from Canada, were not consumed, used, or supplied in Canada before export, and that required delivery, export, and documentary conditions were met. Services may be zero-rated only where the specific exported-service provisions apply; the mere fact that a customer is non-resident is insufficient. Services directly in respect of goods situated in Canada, or services performed in Canada, may remain taxable unless a specific zero-rating rule applies. Input tax credits may generally be claimed for GST incurred on commercial inputs used to make zero-rated supplies, subject to documentary and timing requirements.

  • CBSA export declaration through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS), where export reporting is required by the Customs Act and applicable regulations
  • Commercial invoice or equivalent accounting document identifying the exporter, purchaser, goods description, quantity, value, currency, country of origin where relevant, and delivery terms
  • Packing list and transport documentation, such as a bill of lading, air waybill, courier record, or carrier manifest, where applicable
  • Proof of export and delivery outside Canada, including carrier confirmation, customs exit evidence, tracking records, or recipient delivery confirmation
  • Purchase order, sales contract, customer correspondence, and payment records supporting the export transaction
  • Import-country customs documentation where available and relevant
  • Evidence supporting the GST treatment, including the applicable zero-rating provision and reconciliation to the GST return
  • Inward remittance certificate: not generally required under Canadian GST or foreign-exchange law; bank records or payment evidence may nevertheless be retained as commercial and audit support

Canada has no general nationwide real-time VAT invoice-clearance, digital-stamping, or continuous-transaction-control mandate for ordinary GST supplies. GST registrants must issue invoices or other documentary evidence containing prescribed information when required, retain books and records, and file GST returns electronically where applicable. Electronic invoices are generally acceptable if they preserve required information and auditability. Certain CRA-administered programs may require electronic filing or prescribed electronic data, but there is no general API-based pre-clearance requirement for ordinary collectible trading-card sales. Customs export reporting is conducted through CBSA CERS where applicable and is separate from GST invoicing.

Canada has no general statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds and no general requirement to obtain an inward remittance certificate. The CRA may reassess GST, deny input tax credits, and convert improperly zero-rated supplies to taxable supplies, including applicable interest, where export conditions or documentary support are absent. A registrant may be liable for the GST that should have been collected, plus prescribed interest and applicable penalties. Penalties may apply for false statements, gross negligence, knowingly participating in false documentation, failure to file, failure to remit, or failure to maintain adequate books and records; serious tax evasion may result in prosecution, fines, and imprisonment. Customs non-compliance may lead to administrative monetary penalties, seizure, forfeiture, reassessment of duties and taxes, and prosecution under the Customs Act. Voluntary disclosure, objection, and appeal mechanisms may be available subject to statutory conditions. Retention periods generally require records supporting GST and income-tax positions to be maintained for at least six years from the end of the relevant year, subject to longer periods or specific CRA requirements.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully open convertibility regime for both current and capital account transactions under Article VIII of the IMF Articles of Agreement. There are no exchange controls restricting current payments, trade settlement, dividends, interest, royalties, management fees, or repatriation of invested capital. The Canadian dollar is freely floating and fully convertible, and there is no requirement for central bank approval to convert CAD into foreign currency or foreign currency into CAD. Profit remittance hurdles are limited to fiscal and corporate constraints rather than exchange-control restrictions: non-resident shareholders of an Alberta ULC face federal and Alberta withholding taxes under Part XIII of the federal Income Tax Act (typically 25% on dividends, reducible by tax treaty), arm's-length and transfer-pricing requirements under subsection 247(2), and Alberta corporate solvency tests. No Exchange Control Act, no currency licensing regime, and no balance-of-payments authorization exists in Canada.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender, conversion, or repatriation timeline for export proceeds. There is no mandatory conversion ratio, no requirement to convert foreign currency receipts into CAD, and no obligation to hold export earnings with a specific domestic institution. An Alberta ULC engaged in export sales may maintain foreign-currency accounts at any OSFI-regulated Canadian bank and may freely retain, convert, or disburse export receivables. The only operative reporting duties are transactional: FINTRAC requires reporting of every electronic funds transfer of CAD 10,000 or more entering or leaving Canada, and every large cash transaction of CAD 10,000 or more. Export documentation must satisfy Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) and Statistics Canada trade reporting thresholds, but such filings are statistical and not exchange-control measures.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with prudential supervision by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and anti-money-laundering/anti-terrorist-financing oversight by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM, subject to material legal constraints. An offshore digital e-money institution (EMI) is not recognized as a bank under the Canadian Bank Act and cannot hold itself out as a bank or access Canadian clearing and settlement systems without an OSFI-regulated sponsor. If an offshore EMI provides services to persons in Canada or carries on business in Canada, it will likely be treated as a money services business (MSB) and required to register with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. A ULC using payment platforms such as Stripe or Authorize.Net will normally be boarded on a payment-facilitator model with funds held at a sponsor bank in Canada, not with the offshore EMI. Direct cross-border liquidity via an offshore EMI is viable only where the EMI holds a European EMI licence, has no Canadian touchpoints, settles into an OSFI-regulated correspondent, and operates under an FATF-equivalent registration. For an Alberta ULC with no Canadian physical operations, reliance on foreign EMIs as principal treasury accounts generates adverse KYC outcomes, potential EFT reporting gaps, and Alberta unclaimed-property exposure. The preferable blueprint is a Canadian bank account with platform sub-ledger settlement rails.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital contributions from a non-resident shareholder to the Alberta ULC should be routed through SWIFT MT103 messages using an OSFI-regulated Schedule I Canadian bank as the receiving institution. Payment field 72 narrative must clearly encode 'CA-ULC EQUITY / ALBERTA ULC CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION'. The purpose code should identify the transfer as a direct foreign equity investment into an Alberta unlimited liability company, avoiding garbled retail remittance codes. The prescribed verification bundle comprises: (a) an executed ULC share-subscription or capital-contribution agreement; (b) a ULC board resolution authorizing the specific bank account and wire; (c) a source-of-funds statement from the remitting shareholder accredited by the remitting bank; (d) a letter of instruction from the ULC's registered agent confirming corporate existence; and (e) if the transfer equals or exceeds CAD 10,000, FINTRAC EFT reporting is automatic and no advance agency approval is required. The receiving bank may also request a Schedule B or 'corporate wire detail form' declaring the ultimate beneficiary and economic activity code. It is advisable to use a documented standing instruction with the bank's international payments desk so that repeated capital injections are not flagged as test wires.Outbound disbursements for holographic cold foil printing supplies, production equipment, licensing royalties, and domestic fulfillment services should use the following clearing rails in descending order: (i) SWIFT MT103 straight-through-processing wires for invoices between CAD 2,000 and CAD 500,000, denominated in CAD, USD, EUR, or GBP; (ii) documentary commercial letters of credit (MT700/MT707) governed by UCP600 and ISBP745 for significant equipment imports, with a confirmed LC added when the supplier requires unconditional payment; (iii) domestic large-value settlements via the Large-Value Transfer System (LVTS), the legacy wire rail for CAD payments, which are being progressively complemented by the Real-Time Rail (RTR) for small-value instantaneous disbursements; (iv) commercial virtual-card payments for low-ticket cloud services and digital subscriptions; (v) and for Canadian domestic supplier payments under CAD 20,000, Interac e-Transfer connected through the bank's business API. Multi-currency operational accounts at RBC or TD permit USD clearing via correspondent accounts and direct CAD conversion at mid-market plus disclosed spread. The preferable blueprint is a Canadian bank account with platform sub-ledger settlement rails.
