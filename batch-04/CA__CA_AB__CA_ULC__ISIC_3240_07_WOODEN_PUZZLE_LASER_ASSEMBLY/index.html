Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including the Business Registration Online portal and My Business Account portal. Alberta corporate income tax is administered by the CRA under federal-provincial tax administration arrangements. Alberta incorporation and corporate registry filings are handled separately by Service Alberta and Red Tape Reduction, Corporate Registry.

E-government filing portal

An Alberta unlimited liability corporation must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN), generally a nine-digit identifier, and register the applicable program accounts, including the GST/HST account (RT) and, where applicable, payroll (RP), corporate income tax (RC), import-export (RM), and information-return accounts. The corporation is first incorporated or registered through the Alberta Corporate Registry or federally under the Canada Business Corporations Act. The corporation then registers with the CRA through Business Registration Online where eligible, or by submitting the prescribed registration information through the CRA non-resident registration process, telephone service, or authorized representative. Required information normally includes the legal name, Alberta registered office, directors, incorporation number and date, business activities, fiscal year-end, ownership and controlling-person information, contact details, expected taxable supplies, and non-resident business information. A non-resident owner does not prevent registration, but the corporation must establish that it is carrying on, or intends to carry on, a business requiring the relevant CRA account. A GST/HST registration is separate from the legal entity's BN and must be maintained, returns filed, and tax remitted electronically or through CRA-approved channels.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

An Alberta ULC is a Canadian corporation and is generally subject to Canadian corporate income tax at the entity level; Canada does not provide a general domestic check-the-box election that converts a corporation into a tax-transparent partnership. A ULC may be treated as disregarded or fiscally transparent for United States federal tax purposes by filing the applicable U.S. entity-classification election, ordinarily Form 8832, but that election does not change its Canadian corporate status or Canadian filing obligations. Canadian elections may be available for specific transactions, including section 85 rollover elections, section 86 share reorganizations, subsection 97(2) partnership transfers, and corporate affiliation or amalgamation elections where statutory conditions are met. Canadian anti-hybrid mismatch rules can restrict deductions or impose inclusion consequences for cross-border hybrid arrangements. GST/HST elections may also be available for qualifying closely related corporations, joint ventures, financial institutions, and approved simplified accounting methods.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding certain supplies, measured under the small-supplier rules. The threshold generally applies over a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters. Registration may be required earlier where the single-quarter threshold is exceeded and may be voluntary before mandatory registration. A non-resident may have registration obligations when carrying on business in Canada and making taxable supplies, subject to applicable small-supplier and non-resident rules; registration is not determined solely by the customer's residence.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods supplied in Canada may generally be zero-rated where they are exported from Canada and the statutory export conditions are satisfied, including shipment or delivery outside Canada and retention of satisfactory evidence that the goods left Canada. Goods supplied to a non-resident are not automatically zero-rated if delivered, used, or consumed in Canada. Services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated only where they fall within a specified exported-service provision and the statutory conditions are met; an offshore customer, foreign payment, or foreign bank account alone is insufficient. Relevant exclusions can apply to services connected with Canadian real property, tangible personal property, litigation, personal services, and services performed in Canada. A registrant may claim input tax credits for eligible GST incurred in making zero-rated supplies, subject to ordinary documentation, attribution, and timing rules. Export evidence should be retained with the transaction file, including the purchaser, destination, shipping terms, transport records, customs records, and proof of delivery or export.

  • Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) export declaration or other applicable CBSA export-reporting record, where required
  • Commercial invoice identifying the exporter, purchaser, goods, value, currency, destination, and shipping terms
  • Packing list and product description sufficient to reconcile the shipment
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, courier record, or other carrier transport document
  • Carrier delivery confirmation, foreign customs entry, tracking record, or other objective proof that the goods left Canada
  • Export contract, purchase order, customer correspondence, and evidence identifying the non-resident purchaser where relevant
  • Certificate of origin or preferential-origin documentation where required by the destination country or trade agreement
  • For exported services, service agreement, customer residency evidence, work-performance records, invoices, payment records, and documentation establishing that the statutory zero-rating conditions and exclusions are satisfied
  • Inward remittance evidence may support commercial substantiation but is not a general Canadian statutory prerequisite for GST zero-rating

Canada has no general real-time GST invoice-clearance, fiscal-stamping, or continuous transaction-control mandate for ordinary Alberta businesses. GST registrants must issue invoices or receipts containing prescribed information when required, calculate GST correctly, retain books and electronic records, and generally preserve supporting records for six years. Electronic invoices and electronic accounting records are generally acceptable if they remain reliable, accessible, complete, and reproducible. Commercial exports requiring a customs declaration must generally be reported electronically through the Canada Border Services Agency's Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS), subject to exemptions and reporting thresholds; CERS is a customs reporting system and does not constitute real-time GST invoice clearance. Import GST, customs declarations, and applicable import-account requirements are separate from domestic sales invoicing.

Canada has no general statutory requirement that export proceeds be repatriated within 180 days or within another fixed period as a condition of GST zero-rating. The CRA may deny or reverse zero-rating where export evidence is inadequate, the goods did not leave Canada, the service did not qualify, or the transaction was mischaracterized. The resulting GST may be assessed, together with arrears interest and applicable penalties. Failure to register, collect, remit, file, maintain records, or provide prescribed information can attract GST/HST penalties, including late-filing, late-remittance, failure-to-obtain-registration, failure-to-file, gross-negligence, and false-statement penalties. Fraudulent or knowingly false claims may create criminal exposure under the Excise Tax Act and the Criminal Code. The CRA may reassess beyond the ordinary reassessment period in cases involving misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, willful blindness, fraud, or failure to file; related input tax credits and refund claims may also be denied or clawed back. Export documentation should generally be retained for at least six years from the end of the relevant year, subject to longer retention where an objection, appeal, audit, investigation, or statutory direction remains outstanding.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible under IMF Article VIII, with no exchange controls on either current-account (trade in goods and services) or capital-account (FDI, portfolio, loans) flows. Profit remittance from an Alberta ULC is unrestricted as to FX conversion; the only fiscal hurdle is Part XIII withholding tax on deemed dividends (25% statutory, reduced to 15% general and 5% for a US corporate beneficial owner holding at least 10% of voting shares under Canada-US Treaty Article X). Capital repatriation and intercompany loan repayments require only board authorization and a bank remittance instruction; no central-bank approval is required.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender timeline and no mandatory conversion ratio exist for Canadian export receipts; 0% of proceeds must be surrendered. Exporters may hold USD or CAD balances in any domestic or offshore account. Procedural obligations are limited to: (i) CRA reporting of cross-border balances exceeding CAD 100,000 on Form T1135; (ii) FINTRAC reporting of electronic transfers of CAD 10,000 or more; and (iii) inclusion of export revenue in the ULC taxable income for the year earned without deferral on unrepatriated amounts unless a valid section 17 or FAPI exception applies.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central monetary and clearing authority; foreign exchange policy and regulatory oversight are exercised by the Department of Finance Canada and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI). AML/ATF enforcement is vested in FINTRAC. Canada does not operate a dedicated foreign-exchange administration or a foreign-exchange licensing regime.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low-Medium. Canada does not recognize EU-style Electronic Money Institution (EMI) licences; any offshore EMI or digital bank must register as a Canadian Money Service Business (MSB) with FINTRAC and comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), including a private-sector risk assessment, before clearing CAD settlement accounts. Consequently, offshore-EMI accounts (UK FCA or EU-licensed) are not viable as primary treasury accounts. The optimal structure is a domestic Schedule I operating account for all wire and trade settlement, with FINTRAC-registered PSPs (Stripe Canada for card settlement; Amazon Payments for marketplace payouts) servicing e-commerce collections.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal plus 8% Alberta. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access an 11% combined small-business rate: 9% federal plus 2% Alberta, on eligible active business income within applicable limits.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian corporation to a non-resident, subject to an applicable treaty or statutory reduction. Under the Canada-United States treaty, the rate is generally 5% where the beneficial owner is a company owning at least 10% of the voting power, and 15% in other treaty cases. Other treaties commonly provide rates between 5% and 15%, subject to treaty-specific ownership and anti-abuse conditions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 and AT1 returns are generally due six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. For a December 31 year-end, the filing deadline is June 30 of the following year. Corporate tax balances are generally due two months after year-end; a qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally use a three-month balance-payment deadline if statutory conditions are met. The filing deadline and payment deadline are separate obligations.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Generally due by the corporation's T2 filing deadline, six months after the fiscal year-end.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Part XIII tax is generally remitted by the 15th day of the month following the month in which the amount was paid or credited. Annual information returns are generally due by the last day of February following the calendar year.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a single transaction-value threshold for preparing contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation. A corporation must make reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length prices and maintain contemporaneous documentation for relevant non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. Form T106 generally applies where the aggregate value of reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 1,000,000, subject to statutory exclusions and filing rules. A formal local-file-style contemporaneous record is particularly expected where annual gross revenues are at least CAD 10,000,000, although documentation obligations are not eliminated below that revenue level. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational enterprise groups with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750,000,000, or the equivalent threshold under applicable Canadian rules. Canada does not impose a universal standalone Master File filing requirement for every corporation.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no general standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the United Arab Emirates, Cayman Islands, or certain other jurisdictions. Economic substance remains relevant through the general anti-avoidance rule, sham and series-of-transactions principles, beneficial-ownership analysis, treaty principal-purpose tests, transfer-pricing rules, debt-characterization rules, interest-limitation rules, foreign-affiliate rules, and corporate-residence and permanent-establishment analysis. The ULC should maintain real Canadian governance, decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational capability, production records, inventory control, contracting authority, banking control, and risk-bearing evidence proportionate to its laser-assembly business. A foreign parent cannot convert an artificial Canadian profit or expense allocation into an arm's-length result solely through contractual labels.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Corporate Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, criminal conviction can result in fines generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, in addition to the evaded tax and interest, and imprisonment of up to five years. Civil gross-negligence penalties, reassessments, denied deductions, and prosecution costs may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize, assent to, or participate in false reporting may face personal criminal prosecution, civil recovery, director-liability assessments where statutory conditions are met, restitution, and asset seizure. Corporate limited liability does not protect intentional tax fraud.
Unremitted Source Deductions, GST/HST, and Trust Amounts

Statutory sanction: The corporation may be assessed for the unpaid amount, interest, and penalties. Directors may be jointly and severally liable for specified unremitted source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts unless statutory due-diligence defenses apply.

Director personal liability: CRA may issue director-liability assessments, register judgments, seize assets, garnish accounts, and pursue directors personally. Directors may also face prosecution for deliberate failures, although imprisonment is generally associated with the underlying criminal conduct rather than ordinary administrative arrears.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation and Non-Arm's-Length Diversion

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under the transfer-pricing provisions, deny or recharacterize deductions, impute arm's-length pricing, apply a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where statutory conditions are met, and impose interest and additional penalties for incomplete documentation.

Director personal liability: Directors can face civil exposure for knowingly approving fraudulent or oppressive transactions, shareholder claims, creditor claims, and criminal liability where the arrangement involves fraud, false records, or tax evasion. Routine transfer-pricing adjustments do not automatically create personal liability.
Treaty Abuse and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: The reduced treaty rate may be denied, the payer may be assessed for the shortfall in Part XIII withholding, and interest and penalties may apply. GAAR or treaty anti-abuse rules may be invoked where the arrangement defeats the object, spirit, or purpose of the relevant provisions.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors and officers may face assessments for failure to exercise reasonable withholding diligence and may face civil or criminal consequences if treaty documents or payment records were intentionally falsified.
Shell Company, Sham ULC, and Corporate-Residence Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: CRA may determine residence, permanent-establishment, source, beneficial ownership, or taxable income contrary to the stated structure; deny deductions and treaty benefits; apply GAAR; assess penalties and interest; and refer fraudulent conduct for prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors may face breach-of-duty claims, creditor remedies, oppression claims, personal assessments for trust amounts, asset tracing, and criminal liability if the shell structure is used to defraud creditors, evade tax, launder proceeds, or falsify records. Corporate veil piercing is fact-specific and is not automatic merely because the company has limited or unlimited liability.
Prohibited Corporate Distribution and Insolvency Misconduct

Statutory sanction: The distribution may be voidable or recoverable under corporate, insolvency, fraudulent-conveyance, and creditor-protection rules. Directors may be liable for improper distributions, oppression, breach of fiduciary duty, and losses caused to the corporation or creditors.

Director personal liability: Potential consequences include restitution, repayment of distributions, asset seizure, civil judgments, bankruptcy-related orders, director disqualification in appropriate proceedings, and imprisonment where the conduct constitutes fraud or another indictable offense.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Proceeds-of-Crime Exposure

Statutory sanction: Applicable reporting entities may face FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, publication of violations, and criminal prosecution. Money laundering and proceeds-of-crime offenses can result in substantial fines, forfeiture, and imprisonment, with penalties depending on the offense and mode of prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who direct, authorize, or knowingly facilitate the conduct may be prosecuted personally, have property restrained or forfeited, and face imprisonment. An entity's low-risk AML classification does not excuse suspicious-transaction controls, beneficial-owner verification, sanctions screening, or source-of-funds review.
Unlawful Export, Sanctions, and Product-Control Conduct

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include seizure, forfeiture, administrative or criminal penalties under Canadian sanctions, customs, and export-control legislation, prohibition orders, and imprisonment for serious intentional violations.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize or knowingly permit prohibited transactions may be prosecuted personally and may face asset freezes, forfeiture, travel restrictions where ordered, and imprisonment.
Failure to Maintain Books, Records, and Beneficial-Ownership Evidence

Statutory sanction: CRA may impose record-keeping penalties, extend reassessment periods where misrepresentation or neglect is involved, disallow unsupported deductions, apply adverse evidentiary inferences, and seek court orders compelling production. Obstruction or false evidence may lead to prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally prosecuted for obstruction or false statements, exposed to civil claims for negligent record governance, and unable to rely on the corporation's separate personality where records were deliberately manipulated or destroyed.
