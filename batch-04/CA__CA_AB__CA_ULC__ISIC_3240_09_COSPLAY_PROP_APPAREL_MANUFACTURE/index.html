Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – federal and Alberta corporate income tax administration under the Canada-Alberta tax collection agreement; GST/HST administration; online portal: CRA 'My Business Account' and Business Registration Online (BRO).

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must obtain a Business Number (BN) from the CRA, generally by filing Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number' online or via a tax agent. For an Alberta ULC, the entity must first be incorporated under the Alberta Business Corporations Act through the Alberta Corporate Registry. The CRA issues a 9-digit BN with program accounts (e.g., RT for GST/HST, RP for payroll, RC for corporate income tax). Non-resident applicants may appoint an authorized representative, file GST/HST registration, and must provide Canadian mailing or registered office details. Proof of foreign address and a certificate of corporate existence are typically required.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

An Alberta ULC is a separate legal person and is taxable as a corporation under the Canadian Income Tax Act. However, because a ULC is an 'eligible entity' under U.S. Internal Revenue Code §7701, its shareholders may make a check-the-box election under Treasury Regulation §301.7701-3 to treat the ULC as a disregarded entity or partnership for U.S. federal tax purposes. Canada has no statutory election to convert the ULC into a pass-through for domestic Canadian tax. Canadian hybrid entity provisions (including subsection 94.2 and related foreign affiliate rules) and the Canada-U.S. Tax Convention should be reviewed to determine whether withholding on deemed distributions, Part IV tax, or non-Resident withholding obligations arise.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies made in Canada during any single calendar quarter or the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters ('small supplier' threshold). Once this economic threshold is exceeded, GST/HST registration is mandatory. Non-resident suppliers with a Canadian presence (e.g., carrying on business in Canada) may also be required to register even if below the threshold, and non-resident sellers using Canadian fulfillment warehouses or e-commerce platforms may be subject to separate deemed supplier rules.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under Schedule VI, Part V of the Excise Tax Act. For tangible cosplay prop apparel manufactured in Alberta, the sale is zero-rated when the supplier exports the goods as part of the supply, the goods are delivered to a destination outside Canada, and the supplier retains documentary proof of exportation. Services related to the export (e.g., design, tailoring or logistics services supplied to a non-resident who is not registered) may also be zero-rated under Part V, sections 7 and 8 if performed outside Canada or in respect of goods to be exported. All supporting export records must be kept in Canada at the supplier's place of business and be available to the CRA on demand.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A) or CBSA cargo control / export reporting record
  • Commercial invoice with vendor's Business Number and GST registry number
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical export to a foreign consignee
  • Proof of payment / inward remittance record (bank remittance advice, SWIFT/MT103, or letter of credit confirmation)

Canada does not impose a real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or prior CRA validation requirement for GST/HST invoices. Registrants may issue invoices in paper, PDF, EDI, XML, or PEPPOL e-invoice formats provided the invoice contains all prescribed information under section 169 of the Excise Tax Act. GST/HST returns are filed electronically through GST/HST NETFILE (mandatory for registrants with annual taxable supplies over CAD 1.5 million or those filing via an EFile agent). The CRA can request audit access to e-invoice transaction data through its GST/HST audit procedures.

The CRA may deny zero-rating and reassess the supply at 5% GST plus interest and penalties if the exporter cannot produce contemporaneous proof of physical export. Fraudulent export zero-rating claims are subject to civil penalties under section 285 of the Excise Tax Act (e.g., 25% of the tax sought to be evaded), and can trigger summary or indictable criminal prosecution under section 239 of the Income Tax Act. Reassessment periods may be extended from 4 to 7 years in cases of negligence or misrepresentation; repeated failures to maintain proper books and records can result in cancellation of GST/HST registration and administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 2,500.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is a freely convertible currency with no exchange control restrictions under the Currency Act, the Bank of Canada Act, or the Income Tax Act (ITA). Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility. Resident and non-resident corporations, including an Alberta ULC, may remit dividends, management fees, royalties, interest, and liquidation proceeds abroad without prior central-bank approval, provided the remittance is not subterfuge for a taxable dividend and complies with ITA withholding obligations (non-resident withholding tax under Part XIII: dividends 25% unless treaty-reduced, interest 25% unless exempt, royalties 25%), the Canada-US Tax Treaty (if applicable), thin-capitalization rules under ITA s.18(4) and s.17.1, and transfer-pricing documentation under s.247(2). No surrender or conversion requirement is imposed on inbound or outbound capital transactions. However, the CA_ULC's pass-through election (if made) does not alter banking counterparty KYC expectations; CRA Form T2033 and a Canadian corporate tax residency certificate are expected. Practical remittance hurdles are documentary only: CRA Certificate of Residency (Form NR301 or treaty residence certificate), audited or reviewed financial statements, a completed wire instruction with correct SWIFT BIC and beneficiary tax identifier, and FATCA/CRS self-certification (Form W-8BEN-E/CRS form) for offshore accounts.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender of export proceeds and no mandatory conversion ratio to CAD. Export receipts may be retained in foreign currency, provided they are reported for income tax purposes in CAD at the Bank of Canada noon exchange rate (CRA administrative practice) and the ULC's financial statements are prepared in CAD. The Customs Act (s.95-99), the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) reporting thresholds, and Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) export documentation requirements apply: a completed B13A Export Declaration is required where export value exceeds CAD 2,000 (for non-US destinations) or where CBSA requests; no forex surrender certificate is needed. For proceeds settled via cross-border wire, records must be retained for 5 years per PCMLTFA. There is no statutory timeline for repatriation; however, transfer-pricing rules require arm's-length moving of cash for trade receivables (an implicit 60-90 day standard under OECD TP guidelines is often applied), and any deemed-dividend risk from unreasonably deferred receivables must be managed.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC); prudential regulator: Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML regulator: FINTRAC; exchange control administration: Minister of Finance under the Currency Act; interpretive oversight on tax treaty matters: Canada Revenue Agency (CRA)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-to-High for ancillary treasury, but NOT as the primary banking relationship. Canadian law treats a non-resident fintech EMI / digital cross-border bank as a 'money services business' (foreign MSB) if it operates in Canada; registration with FINTRAC is mandatory under the PCMLTFA before any activity in Canada. A foreign EMI incorporated in the EEA/UK/Hong Kong (e.g., Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer, Revolut Business) is not a Schedule I Canadian bank and cannot hold 'bank' characterization under the Bank Act; it can only operate as an MSB or authorized foreign bank (AFB) branch licensed by OSFI. An AFB license is impractical for this SME profile. Practical constraints: (i) FINTRAC MSB registration and a Canadian money-laundering compliance officer appointment; (ii) MSBs in Canada are prohibited from providing cash withdrawal services beyond regulatory thresholds and must report EFTs over CAD 10,000; (iii) foreign EMIs are typically not covered by Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC) insurance, requiring counterparty risk limits; (iv) ULC structure with unlimited liability is viewed favorably for transparency but requires full UBO disclosure under client due diligence. Recommended use: collections rail for Shopify Payments, Stripe cross-border payouts, and / or zero/low-FX spread flow for CAD-USD-EUR settlements, while keeping segregated 'capital escrow' cash in a tier-1 Canadian bank.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a direct statutory Local File/Master File monetary threshold; however, mandatory Form T106 (Reportable Transactions) file if the total fair-market value of non-arm's-length cross-border transactions exceeds CAD 1,000,000. CRA expects contemporaneous TP documentation when intercompany transactions exceed this threshold, and a Master File may be expected in multi-entity groups; absence of documentation exposes the taxpayer to a 10% transfer-pricing penalty.

Economic substance scope: Alberta/Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR); nevertheless, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s.245) and established judicial doctrines require that the ULC carry on a real business with economic substance, commercial purpose and genuine Canadian management. The ULC must employ staff, maintain premises and control manufacturing decisions in Canada; if the entity is merely a conduit created to access treaty benefits or shift profits, CRA may apply GAAR and recharacterize the transactions without regard to the ULC's legal form.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion – Suppression of Revenue

Statutory sanction: Prosecution under ITA s.239 with fines ranging from 50% to up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to 5 years, plus interest and civil assessments for unreported amounts and GST/HST.

Director personal liability: Directors and shareholders can be held personally liable because an unlimited liability corporation (ULC) does not provide limited liability; CRA may pierce the corporate veil and seek civil restitution from personal assets, impose travel restrictions during criminal proceedings, and, upon conviction, achieve incarceration of the directing mind.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Part XIII / GST-HST

Statutory sanction: Withholding tax becomes immediately due and payable when the amount is paid or credited, payable with daily compound interest; failure to withhold can trigger penalties up to 10% of the amount and administrative fines; GST/HST failures add separate civil penalties and can lead to asset garnishment.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII and GST/HST amounts under ITA s.227.1 and ETA s.323; the unlimited-liability structure means founders and shareholders risk personal bank accounts and real property, and any court-ordered travel ban or imprisonment attaches to individuals rather than the legal entity.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: A transfer-pricing penalty under ITA s.247(8) of 10% of the understated taxable income applies where reasonable efforts are absent; gross-negligence penalties may reach 50% of the tax payable, CRA may adjust transfer prices and disallow deductions, and foreign accretion penalties can increase the tax liability.

Director personal liability: A director who signed false or incomplete T106 schedules can be subject to the 50% gross-negligence penalty and civil liability for the resulting tax; ULC shareholders remain unprotected by limited liability and may be required to satisfy the full tax, interest, penalties and legal fees from personal assets, including restraint orders and travel bans pending appeal.
Shell Company / GAAR Abuse / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may invoke GAAR under ITA s.245 to deny the tax benefit, impose a 25% GAAR penalty under s.245(5), and reassess an amount consistent with the economic substance; treaty benefits may be denied under LOB/PPT provisions and Part XIII renderings of dividends become fully taxable at the 25% statutory rate.

Director personal liability: Where the ULC has no substance, the courts are more willing to disregard the corporate veil; directors can be held civilly liable for the taxes of the sham entity, and because ULCs inherently lack limited liability, all shareholders are jointly exposed. Criminal prosecution may lead to prohibition orders and travel bans, and any imprisonment sentence is served by the natural persons who authorized the sham arrangements.
