Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — federal tax administration portal: My Business Account (www.canada.ca/my-business-account). Alberta corporate income tax is collected by the CRA and remitted to Alberta through a tax collection agreement; there is no separate Alberta GST/PST administration.

E-government filing portal

A newly incorporated Alberta Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) must apply for a federal Business Number (BN) via CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO) portal (www.canada.ca/business-registration), or by mailing Form RC1 to the nearest CRA tax services office. Required documentation includes the Alberta Certificate of Incorporation, Articles of Incorporation, registered office address, board of directors details, and fiscal year-end. The CRA issues a 15-digit BN with program accounts (e.g., RT for GST/HST, RP for payroll, RM for import/export). The BN is the statutory TIN for federal and Alberta corporate tax filings; no separate provincial TIN is issued for income tax purposes. A GST/HST account is mandatory once taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000, or earlier if voluntary registration is desirable.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canadian federal and Alberta income tax law do not provide a check-the-box classification election; a ULC is a taxable corporation subject to entity-level taxation under the Income Tax Act. However, for U.S. federal tax purposes, an Alberta/BC ULC is an eligible entity because shareholders have unlimited liability; the default U.S. classification is a partnership (or disregarded entity for a single owner) unless the ULC files IRS Form 8832 to elect corporate taxation. In addition, Canadian hybrid mismatch rules (Income Tax Act sections 18.4–18.6) may recharacterize cross-border hybrid payments, but these are mandatory rules, not elections. Rollover elections under Income Tax Act section 85 may be filed for qualifying property transfers to or from the ULC.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — total worldwide taxable supplies in the immediately preceding four calendar quarters (small supplier threshold); a non-resident carrying on business in Canada is required to register for GST/HST unless this threshold is not exceeded.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of tangible personal property (in this case, interactive AI pet robots / bionic companion robotic animals) are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act when the goods are exported in accordance with the terms of the supply, delivered to a common carrier for export, or supplied to a non-resident for removal from Canada, and the supplier maintains documentary evidence of export. Services that are incidental to the hardware supply (e.g., firmware updates, warranty support) may also be zero-rated when supplied to a non-resident and not performed in connection with Canadian real estate or tangible personal property. Canada imposes no statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline; the 0-day value reflected below indicates the absence of exchange-control legislation.

  • Canada Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A / Canadian Automated Export Declaration) or Canada Export e-Manifest record
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List
  • Carrier Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing movement to a foreign destination
  • Customs broker or carrier certification of export (if applicable); inward remittance documentation may be requested by CRA as part of an audit trail but is not a statutory customs form

Canada has no federal real-time e-invoicing, fiscal clearance, or digital stamping mandate as of 2025. Electronic invoices are acceptable for tax purposes if they are legible, complete, and retained in a format accessible to the CRA. The Pan-Canadian e-invoice framework based on PEPPOL standards is voluntary, not mandatory. No tax-authority pre-clearance or approval of invoices is required.

If the CRA determines that zero-rating was claimed without substantiating export documentation or that goods were diverted back into the Canadian market, the supply is reassessed at 5% GST on the full sale price, plus interest. Civil penalties under the Excise Tax Act may include 20% of net tax for gross negligence (section 280) and, for wilful evasion (section 327), penalties up to twice the evaded tax or criminal prosecution. The CRA reassessment window is generally 4 years for GST/HST and 3 years for income tax, but fraud or false declarations invalidate the limitation period. Exporters must retain relevant documentation for at least 6 years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for both current and capital account transactions. There are no cross-border restrictions on direct or portfolio investment flows, nor on profit remittance. Non-resident shareholders of an Alberta ULC may repatriate dividends and capital without prior exchange control approval. Standard reporting obligations apply: FINTRAC reports for cross-border transfers of CAD 10,000 or more and 24-hour reports for cross-border currency movements of CAD 10,000 or more. Canadian withholding tax on dividends (generally 25% under Part XIII, reduced to 5%–15% under tax treaties) applies at remittance. The ULC may operate a foreign-currency bank account, and financial statements may use an approved functional currency other than CAD with the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA).

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender or mandatory conversion requirement applies to export proceeds. Exporters may hold foreign currency proceeds indefinitely in a foreign-currency bank account licensed in Canada. Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) requires statistical export declarations for commodities, but no FX conversion is imposed. For financial accounting and CRA compliance, export receipts must be translated into Canadian dollars under the Income Tax Act using the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate or an acceptable average rate, with qualifying functional-currency elections available under section 261.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC) as the central bank and monetary authority; the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as the prudential regulator of banks; the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as the antimony laundering and terrorist financing intelligence unit. Canada has no standalone foreign exchange control administration; exchange rate policy is a floating regime managed through BoC operations and the Currency Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility: Medium with severe legal constraints. Domestic fintech platforms in Canada must operate as authorized financial service providers under provincial money-services business laws or as OSFI-regulated banks. Non-resident EMIs (e.g., Wise, Airwallex) cannot serve as the sole primary settlement rail because the ULC must maintain a Canadian-dollar operating account for CRA remittances, payroll, and statutory payments. Such fintechs may be used as supplementary cross-border payment channels, but their corporate onboarding requires Canadian Money Service Business (MSB) registration or a foreign-money-services license, and they must comply with FINTRAC. Any cross-border receivables routed through a non-resident EMI are immediately deemed taxable remittances and are subject to harmonized sales tax (GST/HST) registration obligations where applicable. Foreign-currency disbursements via EMIs may attract additional anti-money-laundering reviews at the receiving bank.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

For an Alberta corporation subject to the general rates, the combined federal and Alberta corporate income tax rate is 23%: federal 15% plus Alberta 8%. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) may generally receive the small-business rates on eligible active business income within the applicable business limit: federal 9% plus Alberta 2%, or 11% combined. The federal and Alberta small-business limits are generally CAD 500,000 each, subject to federal and provincial eligibility rules, association, business-limit allocation, and phase-outs.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The general Canadian Part XIII withholding tax rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is 25%. An applicable income tax treaty may reduce the rate, commonly to 15% for portfolio dividends and, under some treaties, to 5% for a qualifying corporate beneficial owner meeting the treaty's ownership and other conditions. The applicable treaty must be checked for the recipient, beneficial ownership, limitation-on-benefits or principal-purpose provisions, and any other eligibility requirements.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

File the federal T2 and Alberta AT1 within six months after the corporation's taxation year-end. Federal tax is generally payable within two months after year-end; a qualifying CCPC meeting the statutory conditions may have three months to pay its federal balance. Alberta balance-due timing is generally two months after year-end. Instalment requirements may apply. Filing extensions do not extend payment deadlines.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not generally prescribe a BEPS-style Master File/Local File threshold. Under Income Tax Act subsection 247(4), contemporaneous documentation is required for reportable cross-border transactions with non-arm's-length non-residents; the threshold for the prescribed information-return reporting exemption is generally CAD 1 million of aggregate cross-border transaction value with non-arm's-length non-residents for the year. Form T106 may be required when its reporting conditions are met. Country-by-country reporting may apply to an ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group meeting the applicable consolidated-revenue threshold, generally EUR 750 million or the statutory Canadian equivalent.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no general standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to certain offshore regimes. Canadian transfer-pricing, sham, specific anti-avoidance, general anti-avoidance, beneficial-ownership, and treaty anti-abuse rules may nevertheless challenge arrangements lacking commercial rationale or whose legal form does not reflect the actual conduct. Maintain evidence of functions performed, assets used, risks controlled, decision-making, personnel, and commercial purpose.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion, false statements, or willful non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction may result in a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years. Civil tax reassessments, interest, gross-negligence penalties, and other applicable penalties may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors and other individuals may face personal criminal prosecution where their own conduct satisfies the statutory offence. There is no automatic piercing of the corporate veil solely because tax is unpaid. Director liability depends on the applicable statutory provisions and facts.
Unremitted source deductions and withholding tax

Statutory sanction: The corporation may owe the unremitted amount, interest, and statutory penalties. Under the Income Tax Act, directors may be jointly and severally liable for specified unremitted source deductions and related amounts, subject to statutory due-diligence and limitation rules.

Director personal liability: Personal assessment may be made against directors under the statutory director-liability regime. Liability is not equivalent to automatic veil-piercing; statutory defences and procedural limits apply.
Transfer-pricing non-compliance

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust income under Income Tax Act section 247, assess tax and interest, and impose transfer-pricing penalties where the statutory threshold and conditions are met. Separate information-return penalties may apply.

Director personal liability: Corporate assessments generally remain corporate liabilities. Directors may face personal exposure for their own offences or under a specific statutory liability provision; ordinary transfer-pricing adjustments do not automatically create personal liability.
Fraudulent or sham corporate structure

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include denial of deductions or treaty benefits, reassessment, interest, penalties, anti-avoidance adjustments, and criminal prosecution where the conduct meets the relevant offence requirements.

Director personal liability: Individuals may incur liability for their own participation in fraud or statutory offences. Civil restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment are not automatic consequences of a corporate tax dispute; any such measure requires an applicable legal basis and process.
Corporate dissolution or asset distribution with unpaid tax liabilities

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include statutory director assessments where applicable, recovery proceedings, civil remedies, interest, and penalties; criminal sanctions require proof of the relevant offence.

Director personal liability: Director exposure depends on the governing federal or Alberta statute, the director's role and conduct, and available statutory defences. Personal liability, restitution, or restrictions on travel require a specific legal basis and due process.
Money laundering, sanctions, or deceptive payment activity

Statutory sanction: Penalties vary by offence and statute and may include substantial fines, forfeiture, and imprisonment. Payment processors and financial institutions may freeze, reject, or report transactions where legally required.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally liable for their own participation, authorization, or qualifying failure to comply with statutory duties. Liability is fact-specific and is not imposed solely by corporate office.
