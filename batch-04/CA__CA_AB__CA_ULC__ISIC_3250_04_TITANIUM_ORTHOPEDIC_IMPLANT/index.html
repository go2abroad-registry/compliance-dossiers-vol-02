Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC). Operational portals: CRA 'My Business Account' (MBA), Business Registration Online (BRO), and GST/HST NETFILE. Provincial counter-part in Alberta: Alberta Treasury Board and Finance (corporate income tax collection is administered federally under the Alberta Corporate Tax Act via the CRA single-administration agreement).

E-government filing portal

Foreign-resident ULC must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) and GST/HST account before commencing commercial activity in Canada. Process: (1) submit Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number' or register online through CRA Business Registration Online (BRO); (2) provide certificate of incorporation/amalgamation (Alberta Registrar of Companies), registered office address, director/officer particulars, and a Canadian resident agent or attorney for service (extra-provincial registration under the Business Corporations Act (Alberta) may be required); (3) open a separate GST/HST account (RT) and, if engaging in international trade, an Import/Export account (RM) with the CBSA; (4) where the non-resident has no Canadian presence, CRA may require security (Form RC415) as a condition of registration. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation_for_Canadian_Tax_Purposes_Pass_Through_Eligible_only_for_US_Check-the-Box: An Alberta ULC is a 'corporation' and a separate person under the Income Tax Act (Canada) — it is taxed at the corporate level (non-resident shareholders are not attributed income/losses). For U.S. federal tax purposes only, the ULC is eligible for a Form 8832 check-the-box election (disregarded entity or partnership), mirroring pass-through treatment. Territorial scope: Canada taxes worldwide income of resident Canadian corporations; the ULC maintains Canadian residence if incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or provincial equivalent (Alberta Business Corporations Act). Tax-exempt zone: none applicable within Alberta.

Available election forms

(a) Canada–U.S. Tax Treaty Article IV(7) election: U.S. resident shareholders may elect to deem a Canadian ULC a non-resident of Canada for treaty purposes (denying treaty benefits) — formal notice to CRA required; (b) U.S. Form 8832 'Check-the-Box' classification election for U.S. federal tax (disregarded entity/partnership/corporation); (c) No domestic 'check-the-box' under Canadian law — Canada's hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.4–18.6, 256.5) disregard certain hybrid arrangements, including with respect to a ULC's unlimited-liability capital; (d) GST/HST election under s. 156 of the Excise Tax Act for qualifying closely related Canadian-resident corporations to treat supplies as made for nil consideration; (e) Part I.3 large corporation election and various deemed-dividend elections are not applicable to ULC pass-through status in Canada.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: GST/HST registration is mandatory for any person, including a non-resident ULC, making taxable supplies in Canada whose worldwide taxable revenues exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over 12 consecutive months. Exception — a non-resident that 'solicits orders' in Canada or is engaged in a commercial activity in Canada is required to register regardless of the CAD 30,000 threshold if it makes taxable supplies in Canada. Voluntary registration is available below the threshold. The titanium orthopedic implant manufacturer will be a 'registrant' for GST/HST purposes.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act applies to supplies of goods that are exported by the supplier (e.g., porous titanium orthopedic implants sold to foreign hospitals/distributors) — 0% GST is charged provided the goods are delivered by the supplier to an address outside Canada or the supplier causes the goods to be exported 'as soon as is reasonably possible' after the supply. Offshore services supporting the implants (e.g., design, remote technical support) are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VII if supplied to a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST and the services are not rendered to persons in Canada, not in connection with Canadian real property, and not in connection with goods located in Canada at the time of supply. Domestic sale of surgical implants directly to an individual consumer may qualify for zero-rating under Part II of Schedule VI (medical devices, including surgical implants listed under the Medical Devices Regulations) if the supplier holds a Health Canada medical device licence. The ULC must retain evidence of export and be a GST/HST registrant to claim input tax credits (ITCs) on manufacturing inputs (titanium powder, electron-beam equipment, ISO 13485 certification services) and to apply the zero rate.

  • CBSA Canadian Export Declaration (via CAED / Single Window Initiative) including approved exporter number and internal transaction number
  • Bill of Lading / Airway Bill (original or certified copy) evidencing cross-border carriage to foreign consignee
  • Canada Customs Commercial Invoice / CBSA Form CI1 or electronic equivalent showing purchaser, destination, terms of sale (Incoterms), and HS classification 9021.31 (surgical implants / artificial joints)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank SWIFT confirmation evidencing foreign currency settlement from the non-resident purchaser

No real-time fiscal clearance, no digital stamping, and no mandatory API-based clearance regime currently operates in Canada. The CRA has adopted the voluntary Pan-Canadian Trust Framework / PEPPOL e-invoice exchange, and the federal government is consulting on a potential e-invoicing mandate for B2G transactions. Mandatory electronic filing applies: all GST/HST registrants with annual taxable revenues exceeding CAD 1 million must file electronically (GST/HST NETFILE or EDI). Digital record-keeping: registrants must maintain electronic books and records in an accessible, durable format; custom authorities require electronic export declarations via the CBSA Single Window Initiative / Canadian Export Reporting System (CAED).

CRA and CBSA may jointly audit export zero-rating claims; the ULC bears the burden of proof for 'export in fact'. Retrospective clawback: where zero-rating is disallowed, the output tax becomes payable and is assessed with arrears interest (compounded daily), plus a 'false statement' penalty of 25% of the net tax recoverable under s. 285.1 of the Excise Tax Act. Tax evasion penalties: 50% to 200% of the tax evaded plus up to five years imprisonment (ss. 327–329 ETA); third-party penalties apply to accountants/scheme promoters. Documentary deficiency alone triggers reassessment and clawback of ITCs linked to the export supply. For medical devices, failure to hold a valid Health Canada Medical Device Licence also nullifies zero-rating and attracts civil fines under the Food and Drugs Act, in addition to GST/HST assessment. Transfer-pricing review under ITA s. 247 for sales to non-residents may adjust the export price and trigger a Part XIII withholding exposure (25%, reduced to 0% under the Canada–U.S. Treaty where the shareholder is a U.S. resident) if the CRA recharacterizes payments as dividends or royalties.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible for both current and capital account transactions. There are no foreign exchange controls under the Currency Act or the Bank of Canada Act restricting inflow or outflow of funds. Profit remittances to non-resident shareholders are permitted without exchange control approval; however, payments of dividends, interest, royalties, and management fees may be subject to Canadian non-resident withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (typically 25% on dividends absent treaty relief), and remittance payments must be reported where they constitute 'reportable transactions' under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) for large cash or electronic fund transfers (e.g., C$10,000 or more under FINTRAC cross-border wire reporting thresholds where applicable).

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory requirement for mandatory surrender, conversion, or repatriation of export proceeds. Export receipts may be retained in any currency, held in a Canadian or offshore account, settled through intercompany netting, or used directly for foreign currency disbursements, subject only to normal tax transfer-pricing documentation and Canadian financial reporting standards. For a ULC engaged in export of orthopedic implants to the United States, USD inflows may be held in USD-denominated domestic bank accounts without any conversion ratio or timeline obligation.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) in conjunction with the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for cross-border currency and monetary instrument reporting; no separate centralized foreign exchange administration exists because Canada operates under a freely floating currency regime.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low-to-Medium direct banking eligibility: Canadian-chartered banks and authorized foreign bank branches remain the only entities permitted to take deposits under the Bank Act; offshore 'Electronic Money Institutions' (EMIs) are not recognized as banks in Canada and cannot provide prime-brokerage or true deposit account services. For a CA_AB ULC, an offshore EMI may be used only as a supplementary payment-processing rail for low-value operational transactions, provided the EMI has no Canadian regulated activity and the ULC maintains a primary operating account with a Canadian Schedule I bank. Enhanced KYC, FINTRAC registration status for the payment services provider, and the absence of Canadian securities dealing triggers are mandatory preconditions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File and Master File required if the aggregate value of transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 1 million for services, CAD 5 million for other transactions (including goods, intangibles, and financing). Additionally, a Country-by-Country Report (CbCR) is required for multinational groups with consolidated revenue ≥ CAD 750 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a specific Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime like the UAE. However, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under Section 245 of the Income Tax Act disallows tax benefits from transactions lacking genuine economic substance. Additionally, the CRA may apply the 'substance over form' doctrine and transfer pricing rules to recharacterize transactions. For ULCs, the unlimited liability structure may be scrutinized for tax avoidance if used solely to shift profits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act: fines up to 200% of the tax evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years, and civil penalties of 50% of the tax evaded plus interest.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unpaid corporate taxes and penalties if they failed to exercise due diligence (Section 227.1). Civil restitution, travel bans, and potential imprisonment for willful evasion.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Adjustment of taxable income, penalties of 10% of the net adjustment (if documentation incomplete), plus interest. In severe cases, gross negligence penalties of 50% of the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for penalties if they knowingly participated in or acquiesced to the non-compliance. Personal liability for unpaid amounts may be imposed.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR may deny tax benefits, recharacterize transactions, and impose penalties of 25% of the tax benefit. CRA may also assess penalties for false statements.

Director personal liability: Directors may be held personally liable for taxes and penalties if they signed false returns or directed the sham structure. Travel bans and asset freezes may be imposed.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) Violations

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1 million for corporations; criminal penalties up to CAD 2 million and imprisonment for individuals. Suspension or revocation of licenses.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers can be personally liable for AML violations if they knowingly failed to implement compliance programs. Personal fines and imprisonment up to 5 years.
