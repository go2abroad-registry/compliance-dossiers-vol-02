Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – Business Registration Online (BRO) portal and GST/HST Registry; for Alberta corporate registry, Alberta Corporate Registry via Service Alberta and Alberta King's Printer

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity establishing an Alberta ULC must incorporate provincially under the Alberta Business Corporations Act or applicable ULC legislation, obtain an Alberta incorporation certificate and corporate registry number, then register with the CRA through Business Registration Online (BRO) to obtain a federal Business Number (BN), GST/HST account number, payroll account (if applicable), and import/export (RM) account. Non-resident directors and shareholders are permitted; a Canadian resident representative or agent may be required for service of process and CRA correspondence. The ULC may elect to be treated as a disregarded entity or partnership for U.S. check-the-box purposes, but such election does not alter its Canadian tax classification; the entity remains a corporation for Canadian Income Tax Act purposes unless a valid section 216 election applies for certain passive income. Tax registration must be completed before the first taxable supply or import transaction; a foreign entity can register directly for GST/HST using Form RC1 or via BRO using a non-resident registration pathway.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation; Alberta ULC is a corporation under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and is subject to federal and Alberta corporate income tax on worldwide taxable income if resident, or on Canadian-source income if non-resident; however, the ULC structure allows a check-the-box election in the U.S. to be treated as a disregarded entity, and for Canadian tax purposes it is not eligible for full pass-through treatment except for specific elective rules under subsection 39(13) or section 216 for non-residents; the default classification is a taxable Canadian corporation if incorporated in Canada, thus Entity_Level_Taxation is the default, with Pass_Through_Eligible only in the foreign tax jurisdiction context. — A foreign entity establishing an Alberta ULC must incorporate provincially under the Alberta Business Corporations Act or applicable ULC legislation, obtain an Alberta incorporation certificate and corporate registry number, then register with the CRA through Business Registration Online (BRO) to obtain a federal Business Number (BN), GST/HST account number, payroll account (if applicable), and import/export (RM) account. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; Alberta ULC is a corporation under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and is subject to federal and Alberta corporate income tax on worldwide taxable income if resident, or on Canadian-source income if non-resident; however, the ULC structure allows a check-the-box election in the U.S. to be treated as a disregarded entity, and for Canadian tax purposes it is not eligible for full pass-through treatment except for specific elective rules under subsection 39(13) or section 216 for non-residents; the default classification is a taxable Canadian corporation if incorporated in Canada, thus Entity_Level_Taxation is the default, with Pass_Through_Eligible only in the foreign tax jurisdiction context.

Available election forms

No Canadian check-the-box election exists; the CRA follows the legal form under the Income Tax Act. A ULC incorporated in Alberta may elect under section 216 of the Income Tax Act to file on a net basis for certain rental and timber income. For U.S. federal tax purposes, the ULC may file Form 8832 to elect classification as a disregarded entity or partnership, but this does not affect CRA classification. Hybrid mismatch rules under the Income Tax Act (sections 18.2 and 18.3) may apply to deny deductions for payments giving rise to hybrid mismatches; a ULC can elect to be treated as a corporation under Article IV(7) of the Canada-U.S. Tax Convention for treaty purposes. Alberta ULCs may also elect under subsection 39(13) to treat certain gains as business income, and under the Excise Tax Act, a closely related corporation election (GST/HST joint election) may be available to treat intercorporate supplies as zero-rated or exempt, subject to GST/HST rules.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated exports) over a calendar quarter and over the last four consecutive calendar quarters; a non-resident that does not carry on business in Canada may be registrable if it makes taxable supplies in Canada or requests registration. Once the threshold is exceeded, registration is mandatory within 30 days. Voluntarily registration is available for businesses making zero-rated exports to claim input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rated exports under Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act, including supplies of goods exported by the supplier, supplies of services performed on exported goods, and services that are ancillary to exported goods, as well as certain supplies of intellectual property and intangible personal property to non-residents for use outside Canada. For medical imaging equipment, an exported good qualifies for zero-rating if the supplier exports the goods in the ordinary course of business, retains evidence of export within the prescribed time, and possession of the goods is transferred to the purchaser outside Canada. The supply of a single-purpose medical device may also be zero-rated under Part II of Schedule VI if it qualifies as a prescription or medical device, but exports generally follow the export rules rather than the medical device listing.

Canada does not currently mandate a national e-invoicing clearance or real-time fiscal reporting system. However, the CRA requires that all registrants issue and retain electronic or paper invoices containing prescribed information under section 169 and section 3 of the Excise Tax Act and the GST/HST Regulations. In Quebec, Revenu Québec mandates electronic filing for certain GST/HST and QST returns, but Alberta has no provincial mandate. The federal government has announced a phased approach for e-invoicing using the Pan-Canadian PEPPOL framework, but it remains voluntary. Exporters must transmit electronic export declarations through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CES) or via a service provider to the CBSA, with a unique transaction number and digital validation; freight and customs clearance records must be retained. No digital stamping or real-time fiscal clearance is required at the federal level.

The CRA can reassess GST/HST within a four-year normal period, extendable to seven years if negligence or misrepresentation is established. Any input tax credits claimed for export-related inputs may be denied and net tax reassessed, with interest and penalties under the Excise Tax Act. Knowingly making false statements on an export declaration can result in penalties under the Customs Act (up to CAD 25,000 per count) and criminal prosecution for fraud under the Criminal Code. The CBSA may impose administrative monetary penalties under the Customs Act (AMS) and the CRA may impose a penalty equal to 25% of the amount of any knowingly false input tax credit claim. Exporters must substantiate zero-rating within two years of the transaction; failure to retain prescribed export documents can result in reversal of zero-rating and recapture of ITCs plus back interest. Additionally, failure to file export documentation electronically in the prescribed manner can lead to a loss of zero-rating status and the imposition of GST on the supply.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current-account and capital-account items. There are no exchange-control laws, no mandatory currency repatriation periods, no surrender/conversion ratios, and no restrictions on dividend distributions, profit repatriation, intra-group loans, or equity liquidation proceeds. Cross-border payments are nevertheless subject to: (i) compliance with federal economic sanctions administered by Global Affairs Canada (Special Economic Measures Act and United Nations Act); (ii) FINTRAC large-value transaction reporting (C$10,000 and above); and (iii) OSFI guidance on designated-country exposure and KYC. Non-resident shareholders of an Alberta/BC Unlimited Liability Corporation may remit capital and receive distributions without FX approval, but Canadian withholding tax (Part XIII or Part I under tax treaty) may apply to dividends/interest/royalties.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline, mandatory conversion ratio, or surrender to the Bank of Canada for foreign-currency export proceeds. A medical-imaging exporter may retain, invoice, and settle in CAD or freely choose foreign-currency deposit accounts with a Canadian Schedule I bank. For corporate tax and customs purposes, export receipts must be recorded in CAD using a consistent Bank of Canada exchange rate, or an acceptable daily rate, as prescribed under the Income Tax Act and Excise Tax Act (GST/HST).

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); monetary policy and financial system stability authority. Under the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act, Canada maintains a fully liberalised foreign exchange regime with no separate foreign-exchange administration agency. The Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) administers anti-money-laundering/anti-terrorist-financing surveillance, and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) regulates chartered banks.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: LOW-MEDIUM for treasury-grade liquidity clearing. Offshore digital E-Money Institutions (EMIs) licensed by the United Kingdom, the EU, or Singapore are not chartered banks in Canada and are not permitted to accept Canadian-sourced deposits. They may only operate as money-service businesses if registered with FINTRAC and provincially licenced (e.g., in Alberta under the Money Services Act, Ministerial Approval), and even then they may not provide CAD-denominated deposit-account or clearing services equal to Schedule I banks. For commodity trade under commercial letters of credit and SWIFT-priority TT wires, the treasurer should use a Canadian Schedule I bank for all settlement rails. An EMI can be used as a secondary payment oracle for vendor payments, subject to full FINTRAC registration proof and robust ID&V protocols; never as the primary clearing counterparty.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity/investment capital into the CA-AB ULC must settle via SWIFT MT103 (customer credit transfer) from the remitting bank to the ULC's Canadian account, using reference field 70 for exact purpose: 'EQUITY/ULC CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION' or 'INTERCOMPANY LOAN ULC-001'. Canadian receiving banks require no mandated administrative FX approval, but a large-amount notification (FINTRAC STR) is automatic for CAD-equivalent C$10,000 or more. The ULC must maintain a certified capital-contribution ledger, board-minutes approval, and bank-source trace. Purpose code for foreign direct investment under Statistics Canada/balance-of-payments schedules: use IMF CDIS/E601 category 'equity capital', or if classified as loan, use W001 'intercompany financial claim'. The bank may require the capital injection to be booked as either a contribution to retained earnings or redeemable debenture; all documentation must be tagged to the ULC's 'infinite life' balance sheet.For the medical-imaging export business, settlement should be executed on two ideally cleared rails: (1) SWIFT MT103/MT202 for ten-day, non-documentary supplier payments and after-sale service fees; (2) SWIFT MT700/Market Letter of Credit (Commercial LC with UCP600) for high-value phased-array console shipments, providing bank-backed payment on presentation of compliant documents (proforma invoice, bill of lading, inspection certificate, commercial acceptance). Disbursements should also use Payables Clearing via Payments Canada (ACSS/LVTS) for CAD-denominated supplier invoices. All outgoing wires must bear full legal-entity details, remitter reference to underlying PO/contract, and use of proper ISO currency transaction code (e.g., P051 for trade in medical instruments). The bank must screen against OFSI/OSFI sanctions and FINTRAC travel rule requirements. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined general corporate income tax rate for Alberta corporations, comprising 15% federal tax and 8% Alberta tax. An eligible Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access an 11% combined rate, comprising 9% federal and 2% Alberta tax, on qualifying active business income within the applicable small-business limit.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax generally applies to taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident. Treaty relief commonly reduces the rate to 5% for a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting ownership and limitation conditions and 15% for other qualifying portfolio dividends; the applicable treaty must be verified.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return and applicable Alberta schedules are due within six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Corporate tax balances are generally due two months after year-end; a qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally obtain a three-month balance-payment deadline for eligible amounts, subject to statutory conditions. Instalment payments are generally required monthly or quarterly depending on eligibility.

Deadline: · Frequency:

The return and any payment are generally due one, three, or four months after the end of the applicable reporting period, depending on the filing period and registrant status. Exported goods may qualify for zero-rating only where statutory conditions and documentary evidence are satisfied.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada generally requires contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation for non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents; there is no universal CAD monetary threshold that removes the documentation obligation. T106 reporting is generally triggered when aggregate reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1 million. Country-by-country reporting applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group meeting the EUR 750 million consolidated-revenue threshold, converted and applied under the applicable rules. Canada does not impose a separate comprehensive statutory Master File filing requirement equivalent to some jurisdictions, but CRA may require detailed supporting documentation.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone broad Economic Substance Regulation regime. Substance is nevertheless relevant through the transfer-pricing arm's-length principle, treaty beneficial-ownership analysis, general anti-avoidance rule, principal-purpose-test provisions in applicable treaties, thin-capitalization rules, foreign-affiliate rules, debt-versus-equity characterization, and common-law sham and agency principles. A ULC should maintain Canadian decision-making, personnel, premises, production capability, inventory controls, quality systems, contractual authority, banking records, and risk management proportionate to its medical-device manufacturing and export functions. A ULC that is disregarded or treated as fiscally transparent abroad remains subject to Canadian corporate filing and tax obligations unless a specific Canadian rule provides otherwise.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and fraudulent filing

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, conviction may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment of up to two years, or both. False-statement and omission penalties may generally equal 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed credit. Reassessed tax, interest, gross-negligence penalties, and provincial penalties may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal prosecution where they authorized, assented to, or participated in the conduct. Corporate status does not shield a person from personal criminal liability. Directors may also face derivative civil claims, statutory director-liability assessments for unremitted source deductions or certain GST/HST amounts, and restitution orders. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty but may arise from bail, judicial orders, or immigration consequences.
Unremitted payroll, dividend, royalty, or interest withholding

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unremitted amount, interest, penalties, denial of treaty-reduced rates, and possible director liability. Repeated or deliberate non-remittance may lead to prosecution under the Income Tax Act and related statutes.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for certain unremitted source deductions and GST/HST amounts if statutory due-diligence defenses are unavailable. Liability is not automatic for ordinary corporate income tax debts.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under the transfer-pricing adjustment provisions, impose a 10% transfer-pricing penalty on adjusted amounts where statutory conditions are met, deny deductions, impute income, assess withholding, and charge interest. Failure to produce contemporaneous documentation may eliminate penalty protection.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable for a corporate transfer-pricing reassessment merely because it is material. Personal exposure may arise for intentional participation, fraudulent records, knowing authorization, or related statutory remittance obligations.
Treaty abuse and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: Treaty benefits may be denied under domestic anti-avoidance principles, treaty limitation-on-benefits provisions, or principal-purpose tests. CRA may assess the full 25% Part XIII withholding tax, interest, penalties, and applicable reassessment amounts.

Director personal liability: Personal liability is not automatic. Directors may be exposed where they knowingly implemented fraudulent treaty claims, directed false certificates, or participated in evasion or obstruction.
Shell company and sham substance

Statutory sanction: Potential denial of deductions, treaty benefits, tax credits, or residency positions; transfer-pricing reassessment; GAAR application; withholding assessments; customs and GST/HST reassessments; and corporate-law remedies for misrepresentation.

Director personal liability: Courts do not pierce the corporate veil solely because a company is thinly capitalized or foreign-owned. Exceptional veil-piercing or direct personal liability may arise for fraud, sham arrangements, agency, knowing statutory breaches, or misuse of the corporation. Imprisonment requires an applicable criminal offense and conviction.
Anti-money-laundering and proceeds-of-crime exposure

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC compliance failures may result in administrative monetary penalties, compliance directions, publication of violations, and in serious cases criminal investigation. Proceeds-of-crime offenses may result in forfeiture, substantial fines, and imprisonment under the Criminal Code and Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct, authorize, counsel, or participate in laundering or terrorist-financing conduct may be prosecuted personally. Corporate indemnification does not protect against criminal sanctions, forfeiture, or imprisonment.
Medical-device regulatory and export-control breach

Statutory sanction: Potential product seizure, recalls, stop-sale orders, licence suspension or cancellation, administrative enforcement, customs penalties, prosecution, fines, and imprisonment under applicable Canadian health-product, customs, export-control, and consumer-protection legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal prosecution where they authorized or participated in prohibited conduct. Civil product-liability claims, regulatory orders, and director or officer negligence claims may arise independently of corporate tax compliance.
Corporate-record and obstruction failure

Statutory sanction: Additional penalties, reassessment based on available evidence, disallowance of deductions, compliance orders, contempt consequences, and criminal prosecution may apply.

Director personal liability: Individuals who obstruct, destroy records, or knowingly provide false information may be prosecuted personally. Directors should preserve tax, customs, quality, transfer-pricing, and beneficial-ownership records for the statutory retention period and longer where litigation or audit remains open.
