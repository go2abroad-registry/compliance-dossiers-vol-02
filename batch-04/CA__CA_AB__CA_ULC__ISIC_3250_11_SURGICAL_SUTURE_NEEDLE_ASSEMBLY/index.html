UBO Ultimate Beneficial Owner Screening Rules Official Registry Only

>=25% ownership / control

Applicable AML statute: Alberta corporations are principally governed by the Business Corporations Act, RSA 2000, c B-9, and the Business Corporations Regulation, Alta Reg 118/2000. Alberta private corporations must maintain a register of individuals with significant control under Business Corporations Act sections 21.1–21.31; the regime generally captures individuals with direct or indirect control or ownership of 25% or more of voting rights or shares, or who otherwise have control in fact. The register is not a public registry, but specified law-enforcement, tax, and regulatory authorities may obtain access. The federal Canada Business Corporations Act, RSC 1985, c C-44, sections 2.1 and 21.1–21.303, separately imposes beneficial-ownership transparency obligations on federally incorporated corporations and is not the governing incorporation statute for an Alberta corporation. Relevant AML statutes include the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, SC 2000, c 17, and its regulations; the Criminal Code, RSC 1985, c C-46, including terrorist-property provisions; and the United Nations Act, RSC 1985, c U-2, with implementing regulations. FINTRAC obligations attach to reporting entities and regulated activities, not automatically to every manufacturer.
Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

An Alberta corporation must maintain a registered office in Alberta and a records office as required by the Business Corporations Act. A commercial registered-office provider may be used if it satisfies statutory and registry requirements and can reliably receive and forward service. For surgical-consumables manufacturing, a registered-office address alone does not establish operational substance; manufacturing, quality systems, inventory, personnel, and applicable Health Canada compliance must be maintained at appropriate operating premises.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); federal portal: CRA My Business Account and Business Registration Online. Alberta corporate income tax is assessed and collected by the CRA on behalf of Alberta under the federal–provincial Tax Collection Agreement, so no separate Alberta corporate income tax filing is required. The Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) administers the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) for customs and export declarations.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate the Alberta ULC under the Business Corporations Act (Alberta) via the Alberta Corporate Registry and obtain the Corporate Access Number. Step 2: Register with the CRA using Form RC1 or Business Registration Online to obtain the 9-digit Business Number (BN) with program accounts: RP (corporation income tax), RT (GST/HST), RC (payroll), and RM (import/export). Step 3: Register the ULC with the CBSA as an exporter, including the CERS/eManifest account for electronic export reporting. Step 4: If the ULC is wholly owned by non-residents and elects partnership treatment for GST/HST, file Form GST273 jointly with all shareholders under ETA s. 273. Step 5: Obtain and maintain the Health Canada Medical Device Establishment Licence (MDEL) under the Food and Drugs Act as the statutory prerequisite for manufacturing and selling surgical suture needles; the MDEL and GST/HST registration numbers must appear on commercial invoices and export documentation.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (a ULC incorporated in Alberta is deemed resident in Canada under ITA s. 250(4)(a) and taxed on worldwide income at the combined federal 15% and Alberta 8% corporate rates; pass-through eligibility is available only through elective mechanisms described below, not by default under Canadian domestic law)

Available election forms

GST/HST partnership election under ETA s. 273: a ULC wholly owned by one or more non-resident shareholders may jointly elect with all shareholders to be treated as a partnership for GST/HST purposes (CRA Form GST273), transferring registration and reporting obligations to the deemed partnership.; U.S. check-the-box election under Treas. Reg. § 301.7701-3 (Form 8832): commonly elected so the Canadian ULC is disregarded for U.S. federal income tax; the interaction with Canada's hybrid mismatch rules in Part I of the Income Tax Act must be analyzed to prevent deduction/non-inclusion mismatches.; No Canadian income-tax pass-through election exists: a ULC remains a taxable Canadian corporation for Part I tax purposes and cannot elect to be taxed as a partnership or trust domestically.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — a person must become a GST/HST registrant once taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter or over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (ETA small supplier threshold, s. 240); a Canadian-incorported ULC is a resident person subject to full nexus, and export sales of surgical suture needles and braided sutures count as taxable supplies for threshold purposes unless zero-rated; voluntary registration is available below the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to a supply of tangible personal property (including surgical suture needles and absorbable braided sutures) exported by the supplier by way of sale (ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 1), delivered to a common carrier or consignee for export (s. 2), or shipped directly by the supplier to a non-resident purchaser outside Canada (s. 4). Services rendered to a non-resident and not received in Canada may also be zero-rated under Part V, s. 7. Export zero-rating is the standard route for shipped medical consumables; domestic zero-rating of medical devices under Schedule VI, Part II may also apply where the product qualifies as a listed medical device under the Medical Devices Regulations. Canada imposes no statutory FX repatriation deadline; the 180-day period reflects commercial letter-of-credit and SWIFT settlement terms accepted by the CRA as reasonable substantiation of true export proceeds.

  • CBSA Export Declaration — eManifest / Canadian Export Reporting System filing with the applicable HS classification (HS 9018.32 for suture needles; HS 3006.10 for sterile absorbable surgical sutures) and the CBSA export transaction number
  • Commercial Invoice bearing the ULC GST/HST registration number, Health Canada MDEL number, and the notation 'Zero-rated export supply under ETA Schedule VI, Part V'
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical departure of the goods from Canada to the non-resident purchaser
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / SWIFT MT103 bank credit note demonstrating receipt of export proceeds from the foreign buyer within 180 days of shipment

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government API validation mandate exists in Canada. GST/HST registrants with annual taxable supplies above CAD 1.5 million must file returns and remit electronically (GST/HST NETFILE, EDI, or My Business Account); electronic invoices (XML, EDI, or PDF) are accepted and must be retained in legible format for audit. A Pan-Canadian PEPPOL-based e-invoicing framework is being developed but remains voluntary and not yet operational.

The CRA may reassess claimed zero-rating within four years of the reporting period, or indefinitely in cases of misrepresentation or wilful default; where export documentation is incomplete or inconsistent, the supply is recharacterized as taxable, triggering 5% GST on the full invoice value plus arrears interest and penalties. A false statement or omission in a GST/HST return made knowingly or with gross negligence attracts the ETA s. 280(1) penalty equal to the greater of CAD 5,000 and 50% of the net tax attributable to the misstatement; abusive refund or rebate claims are subject to civil penalties under ETA s. 281. Deliberate export-tax evasion is prosecutable under ETA s. 327 or ITA s. 239, with fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment, in addition to full clawback of all zero-rated amounts.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

CAD is freely convertible under a fully floating exchange-rate regime. Canada imposes no exchange controls on current or capital account transactions; dividends, interest, royalties, management fees, and liquidation proceeds may be repatriated without Bank of Canada approval. The hurdles facing an Alberta ULC are fiscal rather than regulatory: Part XIII withholding tax of 25% on non-resident payments (reduced to 5% on qualifying corporate dividends and 0% on arm's-length interest under the Canada-US Tax Treaty where the ULC qualifies for treaty benefits under Article IV(7)), thin-capitalization limits under Income Tax Act s. 18(4) (maximum 1.5:1 debt-to-equity), NR4 information returns and withholding remittance to the Canada Revenue Agency, and the need for a formal ULC pass-through/tax classification memorandum where US shareholders intend hybrid treatment.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender, conversion quota, or repatriation timeline applies to export proceeds in Canada. The ULC may retain foreign-currency receipts offshore or onshore, convert them at the floating rate, or hold them in USD clearing accounts without any central bank permission. Compliance burdens are informational: Form T1135 (Foreign Income Verification Statement) applies when the aggregate cost of foreign property exceeds CAD 100,000; Form T1134 applies to 10%-or-greater foreign affiliates; FATCA/CRS reporting covers offshore financial accounts; and records of every export transaction must be retained for at least five years under the Export and Import Permits Act (surgical suture needle assemblies, HS 9018.32, being non-controlled goods).

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with prudential supervision of deposit-taking institutions conducted by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), and high-value payments clearing oversight by Payments Canada. The governing instruments are the Currency Act, the Bank of Canada Act, and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium feasibility. Offshore digital EMIs (Wise, Airwallex, Revolut Business) are not regulated as banks under the Bank Act. A Canadian ULC may use them for sub-CAD 50,000 disbursements only where the EMI holds a FINTRAC money service business registration for its Canadian payment activity or clears through an OSFI-regulated Schedule I bank. Key legal constraints: (i) an unregistered non-resident EMI cannot lawfully operate a CAD-denominated payment service for Canadian clients; (ii) EMIs cannot issue commercial letters of credit under UCP 600, so the Swift_LC rail requires a Schedule I bank; (iii) synthetic EMI FX pricing is unlikely to qualify for hedge accounting under IFRS 9; and (iv) client funds held with an EMI are not CDIC-insured. Recommended posture: Schedule I bank as the primary clearing rail with the EMI used only as a supplementary TT_Wire corridor for small recurring payments.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Non-resident shareholders inject capital via SWIFT MT103 wire to the ULC's CAD/USD settlement account at a Schedule I bank, referencing the share subscription agreement, the directors' resolution, and the CRA business number. After the bank's source-of-funds review (5-15 business days), the remittance is booked as paid-up capital or contributed surplus; the Alberta ULC has no minimum capital requirement. If the injection is structured instead as a shareholder loan, the same routing applies but the loan must respect Income Tax Act s. 18(4) thin-capitalization (maximum 1.5:1 debt-to-equity), bear arm's-length interest, and suffer Part XIII withholding on each interest payment. No Bank of Canada, FINTRAC, or ISED approval is required under the Investment Canada Act where the ULC's enterprise value is below the 2024 WTO-investor threshold of CAD 2.472 billion; a post-closing notification applies only at or above that threshold. USD injections are converted by the bank's FX desk at the trade-date spot rate and credited to the CAD operating account.Outbound settlements clear over three rails: (i) SWIFT MT103 for open-account suppliers, freight, and insurance, with ISO 20022 remittance citing the purchase-order number, HS code 9018.32, and CBSA importer reference; (ii) irrevocable sight commercial letters of credit issued by the Schedule I bank under UCP 600 for raw-material imports, payable against clean-on-board bills of lading, commercial invoices, packing lists, certificates of origin, and quality certificates; and (iii) standby L/Cs or demand guarantees for bid and performance bonds in provincial health procurement. USD-denominated disbursements draw on the ULC's USD clearing account; CAD/USD conversion is executed via spot contract two business days before the value date or covered by the bank's treasury net-limit service. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium feasibility. Offshore digital EMIs (Wise, Airwallex, Revolut Business) are not regulated as banks under the Bank Act. A Canadian ULC may use them for sub-CAD 50,000 disbursements only where the EMI holds a FINTRAC money service business registration for its Canadian payment activity or clears through an OSFI-regulated Schedule I bank. Key legal constraints: (i) an unregistered non-resident EMI cannot lawfully operate a CAD-denominated payment service for Canadian clients; (ii) EMIs cannot issue commercial letters of credit under UCP 600, so the Swift_LC rail requires a Schedule I bank; (iii) synthetic EMI FX pricing is unlikely to qualify for hedge accounting under IFRS 9; and (iv) client funds held with an EMI are not CDIC-insured. Recommended posture: Schedule I bank as the primary clearing rail with the EMI used only as a supplementary TT_Wire corridor for small recurring payments.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate value of non-arm's length cross-border transactions (triggers mandatory T106, Schedule 25-ITIR, and contemporaneous Local File documentation under s. 247(4) ITA); Master File documentation is required where gross revenue exceeds C$50,000,000 or total assets exceed C$100,000,000 combined with related-party cross-border transactions above C$1,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) statute; substance is enforced through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s. 245 ITA), the s. 247 commercial-rationality requirement, the Limitation on Benefits Article XXIX A under the Canada-US Treaty, and CRA's anti-dividend-stripping benefit test. The Alberta ULC must maintain real manufacturing operations (plant, sterilization equipment, employees, inventory risk) in Alberta to be respected as a Canadian manufacturer and to preserve treaty relief on outbound dividends.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion - False T2 Return

Statutory sanction: Under s. 239(1) ITA: fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded, plus imprisonment for up to 5 years; civil gross negligence penalty of 50% of understated tax (s. 163(2)); unlimited reassessment period (s. 152(4)).

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize or acquiesce in the false return are jointly and severally liable for corporate penalties, may face criminal prosecution, and Canadian courts can issue travel restraints and asset preservation orders where evasion involves concealment or flight risk.
Withholding Tax Defalcation - Part XIII

Statutory sanction: 10% penalty on unremitted amounts under s. 227(8) ITA, plus daily compounded interest; CRA may issue a 'requirement to pay' freezing bank accounts (s. 226(2)); shareholders of a ULC face unlimited personal liability for corporate tax debts.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for the full unremitted Part XIII amounts plus penalties under s. 227.1 ITA without proof of intent; CRA may obtain an ex parte court order restricting the director's departure from Canada and seize personal assets.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Penalty of 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment, minimum C$5,000, under s. 247(3) ITA; the adjusted amount is added to taxable income and the denied deduction may be recharacterized as a deemed dividend attracting 25% Part XIII WHT; T106/Schedule 25 late-filing penalties up to C$2,500 each.

Director personal liability: Directors are not strictly liable absent direction, but a director who knowingly signs false TP documentation faces gross negligence penalties under s. 163.2 ITA and can be personally assessed for culpable conduct; CRA may attribute adjustment amounts to principal shareholders.
Shell Company / Treaty Shopping - ULC Conduit

Statutory sanction: GAAR reassessment under s. 245(2) ITA denies the tax benefit and applies a 25% penalty (s. 245(2.1)); denial of Limitation on Benefits relief reinstates 25% Part XIII WHT on all historical distributions; CRA may disregard the ULC's existence and assess the US parent directly.

Director personal liability: The ULC's unlimited liability structure means shareholders (including the US parent) are personally liable for all CRA assessments and corporate debts; directors face joint liability for unpaid taxes upon failure to exercise due diligence, and travel bans may be imposed pending final assessment.
AML / Payment Rails Infraction (PCMLTFA)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act up to C$1,000,000 for corporations and C$500,000 for individuals; money laundering offences under s. 462.31 Criminal Code carry imprisonment up to 5 years and forfeiture of proceeds.

Director personal liability: Directors who exhibit wilful blindness to suspicious wire transfers can be personally named in FINTRAC penalty notices, face criminal liability for directing laundering transactions, and may receive prohibition orders barring future corporate directorship.
