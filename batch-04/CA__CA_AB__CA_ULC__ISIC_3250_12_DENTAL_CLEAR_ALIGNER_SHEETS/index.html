Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) - Federal tax administration for Goods and Services Tax (GST), corporate income tax, and payroll; portal: CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) and My Business Account. Provincial corporate registry: Alberta Corporate Registry under Service Alberta, pursuant to the Alberta Business Corporations Act.

E-government filing portal

Step 1 - Incorporate the ULC under the Alberta Business Corporations Act through the Alberta Corporate Registry (obtain Certificate of Incorporation, Alberta corporation number, registered office in Alberta, and authorized director records). Step 2 - Apply to CRA for a nine-digit Business Number (BN) with program accounts (RP: GST/HST, RC: Corporate Income Tax, RT: Payroll, RZ: Import/Export) by filing online through Business Registration Online or, for non-resident applicants, by submitting Form RC1 with supporting incorporation documents, Alberta registered office address, director list, and completed forms identifying the US parent and Canadian resident agent where applicable. Step 3 - Register for the GST/HST account before ceasing small-supplier status; registration is mandatory immediately upon worldwide taxable turnover exceeding CAD 30,000 in the previous four consecutive calendar quarters. Step 4 - If products are manufactured or imported into Canada, register the RZ (CBSA) program account and, where applicable, obtain a Health Canada Medical Device Establishment Licence (MDEL) for dental aligner sheets; CRA issues the BN within 5-10 business days for online filings, with confirmation letter following by mail.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible / Territorial_Scope: The Alberta ULC is a for-profit corporation whose shareholders bear unlimited liability for debts, but it is eligible under CRA administrative policy (published 2007) to elect fiscal transparency whereby the ULC is treated as a partnership for Canadian income tax purposes. For US federal tax purposes the ULC may be treated as a disregarded entity via a check-the-box election by its American parent. Under Article IV(7) of the Canada-US Tax Convention (as amended by the 2007 Protocol), the ULC may elect to be treated as a US resident for treaty purposes. The ULC remains a distinct registrant for GST/HST purposes, and Alberta has no provincial sales tax; all export sales of dental aligner sheets are subject to the federal territorial sourcing rules.

Available election forms

(i) Canadian ULC transparency election - CRA administrative position under Income Tax Technical News No. 41/43 allows an Alberta ULC, with unanimous shareholder consent and confirmation that no Canadian-resident shareholders hold the shares, to file an election with CRA effective from a specified date, causing the ULC to be taxed as a partnership rather than a corporation; no T2 return is required if the election is valid. (ii) Treaty residency election - under Article IV(7) of the Canada-US tax treaty, the ULC may elect to be treated as a resident of the United States for treaty purposes (including on Form 8832 impact and treaty-based return disclosures) provided it is fiscally transparent under US law; this election avoids double taxation and modifies withholding rates but must be disclosed to CRA. (iii) US check-the-box election - the US parent may file Form 8832 (Entity Classification Election) with the IRS to treat the Canadian ULC as a disregarded entity for US federal income tax purposes, aligning with the Canadian transparency election. (iv) GST/HST voluntary registration - even below the CAD 30,000 threshold, the ULC may elect to register for GST/HST to claim input tax credits (ITCs) on establishment, manufacturing, and export costs.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 - small supplier threshold measured on worldwide taxable supplies (including commercial activities in Canada) in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters and in the current calendar quarter; revenues from financial services, sale of capital property, goodwill, and zero-rated exports are excluded from this calculation. A person ceases to be a small supplier and must register when the CAD 30,000 cumulative threshold is crossed.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero rating applies under section 1 of Part V, Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act to supplies of tangible personal property (dental clear aligner sheets) sold to a non-resident purchaser where the supplier or the purchaser, or the purchaser's agent, exports the goods from Canada as soon after delivery as is reasonable and, for goods delivered to the recipient in Canada, no later than 60 days after delivery to the purchaser or their agent. The ULC must not perform further processing, finishing, assembling, or value-added services in Canada after the sale; the goods must leave Canada in the same condition in which they were sold. The zero-rated treatment applies even if the ULC is not registered for GST/HST, but to claim input tax credits and a net refund the ULC must be a GST/HST registrant. Related-party export sales (US parent as purchaser) require arm's-length pricing under transfer-pricing rules and contemporaneous documentation supporting both the export price and the commercial substance of the transaction.

  • Customs Export Declaration: CBSA Form B13A / A85 or approved electronic eManifest filing in the Canadian Export Reporting System (CES) for shipments valued above CAD 2,000
  • Commercial Invoice issued by the exporting ULC identifying the foreign buyer, HS classification (e.g., 3920.90 or 9018.49 medical/dental device classification), Incoterms, unit price, and currency
  • Bill of Lading (ocean) or Air Waybill (air) naming the ULC as shipper and the foreign party as consignee, evidencing physical removal from Canada
  • Cargo Control Document / carrier dispatch sheet certified by the freight forwarder or customs broker showing the goods departed the Canadian border
  • Purchase order and sales contract with the non-resident purchaser evidencing transfer of title and risk at the border or at origin
  • Foreign customs import documentation (entry summary or duty receipt) from the destination country confirming arrival outside Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate, bank SWIFT/Wire transfer confirmation, or payment ledger demonstrating foreign-exchange settlement of the export invoice
  • Warehouse dispatch records, container tracking logs, or trucker GPS exception reports supporting the 60-day export window requirement

No statutory real-time fiscal clearance, API-based electronic invoicing, or digital stamping mandate exists under Canadian federal law (unlike Latin American jurisdictions). CRA requires registrants to maintain complete books and records in a readable and auditable format; invoices may be issued in paper or electronic form (PDF, EDI, XML, or standard business document formats) without prior CRA approval. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically via GST/HST NETFILE, EDI, or CRA My Business Account once annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 1,500,000, with prescribed filing frequencies (monthly, quarterly, or annual). For exports of goods departing Canada, the Canadian Export Reporting System (CES) under the CBSA mandates an electronic export declaration (Form B13A / A85) or an approved eManifest filing for shipments valued over CAD 2,000, and the carrier is legally required to present the export cargo control document at the border; there is no digital-stamping or block-chain clearance regime.

CRA auditors test export zero-rating claims by verifying that the goods physically left Canada within 60 days of delivery and that the supplier retained the full documentation bundle for six years. Inadequate or contradictory evidence results in reassessment converting the zero-rated supply to taxable at 5%, requiring the ULC to remit GST, disallowing corresponding input tax credits, and imposing interest at the prescribed daily-compounding rate from the original filing date. Knowing or grossly negligent false statements on GST/HST returns attract a penalty under Section 285 of the Excise Tax Act of the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the net tax understated, with an extended four-year reassessment period where misrepresentation is attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default and an unlimited period for fraud; criminal evasion under Section 327/328 ETA exposes the ULC and directors to fines of 50%-200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment. CRA also applies the 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation benchmark in transfer-pricing and export-receivable audits: unrealistically delayed, permanently unremitted, or non-collected intercompany receivables will be re-characterized as a form of shareholder benefit or loan, creating deemed interest, withholding tax, capital gains issues, and penalties under Section 247 of the Income Tax Act.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility on both current and capital account. The CAD is a freely floating currency with no central bank exchange rate fixing. Profit and capital remittance by a non-resident-owned Alberta ULC (CA_ULC) is unrestricted under the Currency Act; the only hurdles are administrative: Part XIII withholding tax compliance (CRA), FINTRAC reporting on outbound EFTs over CAD 10,000, and OSFI-mandated bank attestations for source of funds. No foreign exchange allocation, prior approval, or quota regime applies.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No export proceeds surrender requirement exists in Canada. Manufacturers of dental clear aligner sheets (ISIC 3250 / NAICS 339114) may retain USD receipts offshore or in USD-denominated domestic accounts. Statutory reporting applies, not conversion: T1135 filing if the cost base of specified foreign property exceeds CAD 100,000; FINTRAC Large Value Transaction Reports for EFTs at or above CAD 10,000; and customs export documentation (B13A) filed through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) for goods exported from Alberta.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), in coordination with the Department of Finance Canada and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI). Canada has no separate Foreign Exchange Administration authority; exchange and capital controls are absent.

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility: Medium for treasury operations, High for the retailer payout rail. Canadian law provides no separate EMI charter. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Combined 23% federal and Alberta general corporate rate: 15% Part I federal plus 8% Alberta general corporate tax for an Alberta-resident corporation; not reduced by the federal small business deduction unless the entity qualifies as a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) and meets ownership criteria.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on taxable Canadian dividends paid to non-resident individuals, partnerships, and corporations; applies to ULC dividend distributions. The Canada-US treaty reduces the statutory rate to 5% where the beneficial owner is a US corporation owning at least 10% of the voting capital of the Canadian payer, and to 15% in other cases.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CA$1,000,000 aggregate fair-market-value of reportable non-arm's-length cross-border transactions triggers mandatory Form T106 (Information Return of Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents) filing; CRA administrative policy treats the same threshold as the intended benchmark for contemporaneous Local File and Master File documentation. Country-by-Country reporting applies at the consolidated group revenue threshold of EUR 750,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Alberta and Canadian federal domestic law do not impose a separate 'Economic Substance Regulation' regime as used in zero-tax offshore centres. However, the Canada Revenue Agency applies the statutory General Anti-Avoidance Rule, common law substance-over-form, and treaty limitation-on-benefits provisions, so a ULC must demonstrate real management, employees, risk assumption, and operational substance to secure treaty benefits, loss utilization, and transfer pricing positions.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Wilful Failure to File

Statutory sanction: Indictment under subsection 239(1) of the Income Tax Act carries a fine of not less than 100% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years. Civil gross negligence penalties of 50% of understated tax apply, with interest and a six-year reassessment period.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize or participate in evasion can be personally assessed under the wilful blindness or knowledge standard. CRA may issue director liability assessments for unremitted withholdings, and courts can pierce the corporate veil in fraud cases for restitution, asset freezes, travel restrictions, and civil bankruptcy remedies.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance and T106 Omission

Statutory sanction: Section 247(3) penalty of 10% of the net upward transfer-pricing adjustment when the adjustment exceeds CA$50,000; failure to file T106 attracts administrative penalties of CA$100 per month for each document, up to 24 months, plus compounded daily interest and foreign-source income recharacterization.

Director personal liability: Transfer pricing penalties are assessed against the corporation, not directors directly, but a director is personally exposed where false T106 filings or fraudulent transfer pricing documents support tax evasion. Professional disciplinary action, liability to the corporate estate, and criminal exposure under section 239 can follow for deliberate documentation fraud.
Withholding Tax Remittance Failure and Hidden Non-Resident Distributions

Statutory sanction: The payer is liable for the gross amount that should have been withheld, plus penalty under subsection 227(9) of 10% on failure to remit and 20% on repeated failure, compounded daily interest, and the CRA can pursue the amounts directly from the non-resident recipient.

Director personal liability: Directors of the non-resident payor are jointly and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII withholdings under subsection 227.1(1), subject only to the limited due-diligence defense. CRA issues director liability assessments without a court order, allowing garnishment, seizure, and interjurisdictional collection, including restrictions on leaving Canada where a travel ban is obtained.
General Anti-Avoidance Rule Abuse and Shell Structure Exploitation

Statutory sanction: The GAAR permits full denial of the tax benefit, reassessment of tax and interest, and the 2023 and later measures impose a 25% GAAR penalty on the tax benefit in abusive non-disclosed transactions. Reassessment beyond the normal three-year period is possible for misrepresentation attributable to neglect or fraud.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign materially false returns or knowingly implement a sham structure may be lenders of personal liability if the corporate veil is pierced under provincial fraudulent conveyance law. Professional tax advisers also face fault, negligence, and CRA consequential penalties, while criminal exposure arises where the arrangement constitutes tax evasion.
Anti-Money Laundering Compliance Failure

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act exposes corporate entities to administrative monetary penalties up to CA$1,000,000 per violation for serious non-compliance; continued or wilful violations can also result in criminal prosecution and imprisonment of officers.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who knowingly direct, or are reckless about, AML breaches are personally liable for FINTRAC penalties in addition to corporate sanctions. Criminal involvement in laundering proceeds of crime under the Criminal Code carries imprisonment for up to ten years, asset forfeiture orders, and mandatory financial disgorgement.
