Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital under the ABCA; a subscription of at least one no-par-value common share for nominal consideration (e.g., CAD 1.00) is sufficient. Funding & unlock path: Mandatory Fire Safety and Inspection Certificate under the Alberta Fire Code for storage occupancy, including combustible organic dust (chili pepper and garlic powder) detection and suppression; building occupancy permit under the Alberta Building Code (2023); Alberta Occupational Health and Safety (OHS) workplace clearance covering a written hazard assessment, WHMIS training, respiratory protection for spice dust exposure, and loading-dock traffic safety; municipal fire department pre-plan approval for high-piled palletized storage and emergency evacuation signage.

Paid-in capital rule: No statutory minimum paid-up capital under the ABCA; a subscription of at least one no-par-value common share for nominal consideration (e.g., CAD 1.00) is sufficient. No escrow account or capital deposit is required by Alberta law, but the stated capital account rules (s. 24.1 ABCA) and the solvency test for distributions (s. 36 ABCA) must be observed.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current-account and capital-account transactions. There is no prior approval requirement, no exchange-control quota, and no surrender requirement for export earnings, foreign borrowing, equity injections, or repatriation of profit. Dividend remittances by the Alberta unlimited liability corporation (ULC) to non-resident shareholders are unrestricted at the banking level, but must satisfy Alberta corporate-law solvency requirements, comply with Part XIII non-resident withholding tax under the Income Tax Act, and respect transfer-pricing standards where intercompany payments are used. The Canada-United States tax treaty, if applicable, may reduce withholding rates and preserve the ULC's eligibility as a U.S. tax-disregarded entity, but this does not alter Canadian banking convertibility.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender rule, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no required repatriation timeline for export receipts. Export proceeds may be retained in CAD or foreign currency in a Canadian or offshore account. Nevertheless, the exporter must ensure timely reporting: FINTRAC reports for cross-border electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more, and CRA Form T1135 disclosure by Canadian-resident corporations maintaining specified foreign property with an aggregate cost exceeding CAD 100,000, subject to applicable exceptions.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada; no dedicated central-bank exchange-control authority exists because Canada maintains a fully liberalized foreign-exchange system. The Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises deposit-taking institutions, and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) enforces cross-border money-laundering reporting.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low feasibility for an offshore digital EMI or electronic-money institution as the primary or sole banking channel. Canada does not recognize a separate 'EMI' licence; a provider offering payment or account-like services must be a bank, CMSA-registered financial institution, or a money services business (MSB) registered with FINTRAC. An offshore EMI without Canadian registration cannot open a true deposit account in Canada, clear CAD through Payments Canada rails, or provide trade finance. A Canada-registered MSB or authorized payment-service provider may support disbursement rails, payroll, and currency conversion, but it is not a satisfactory substitute for a tier-one Canadian bank for capital escrow, export collections, or commercial letters of credit. The recommended structure is a tier-one bank for settlement and escrow, with a FINTRAC-registered Canadian payment institution only for low-value repetitive disbursements if a bank does not provide a competitive service.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity capital may be injected by non-resident shareholders through an SWIFT MT103 wire transfer in USD, CAD, EUR, or another freely convertible currency directly to the ULC's tier-one Canadian bank operational account. Canada does not maintain a prescribed government purpose-code table; the remittance field should state 'capital contribution for subscription of unlimited liability company shares' to enable the bank to book the inflow correctly and to avoid misclassification as trade proceeds. The bank's compliance review will require the source of funds, the share subscription agreement, and the board resolution authorizing issuance. If the wire is routed through a U.S. correspondent bank, the transaction must not involve a sanctioned person or entity and should carry accurate originator and beneficiary identifiers to satisfy OFAC and FINTRAC expectations. After receipt, the bank will post the funds to the share-capital or contributed-surplus account in accordance with Alberta business corporate law.Export trade disbursements should be cleared using a tier-one bank in the following combined structure: SWIFT MT103 telegraphic transfer for low-value, standard-credit-risk suppliers; SWIFT MT700/MT707 commercial letters of credit for deferred-payment sales or where supplier credit risk is elevated; and SWIFT MT202 for interbank cover payments if instructed by the beneficiary. For supplier settlements in CAD, high-value payments may be cleared through Payments Canada's Large Value Transfer System or the retail Automated Clearing Settlement System; USD disbursements are cleared through the bank's U.S. correspondent network via Fedwire or CHIPS. Export collections may be processed as documentary collections (CAD/DA and CAD/DP) for existing counterparties. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined federal and Alberta general corporate income tax rate, comprising 15% federal tax and 8% Alberta tax. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access an approximately 11% combined rate on eligible active business income up to the applicable CAD 500,000 small-business limit, subject to federal and Alberta eligibility rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. Common treaty outcomes include 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting the ownership threshold and 15% in other qualifying cases; the exact rate depends on the applicable treaty and facts.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 and AT1 are generally due within six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally have three months after year-end to pay its balance where the statutory conditions are met. Instalment obligations may arise monthly or quarterly depending on the corporation's status and prior-year tax liability.

Deadline: · Frequency:

The filing and payment deadline depends on the assigned reporting period. Annual filers generally file within three months after the fiscal year-end where instalments are required, or within the prescribed period applicable to the registrant's filing category.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which section 247 contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is automatically unnecessary. A T106 information return is generally required when aggregate reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000. A Canadian Local File is required as part of contemporaneous documentation where section 247 applies; Canada does not generally impose a separate domestic Master File threshold. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year. CRA may request documentation, generally with a 3-month production period.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a standalone United Arab Emirates-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Canadian anti-avoidance analysis nevertheless examines legal form, commercial purpose, factual substance, functions performed, assets used, risks assumed, beneficial ownership, central management and control, and the general anti-avoidance rule. Sham transactions, conduit arrangements, treaty shopping, circular cash flows, artificial debt, and arrangements lacking commercial substance may be challenged under the Income Tax Act, treaty anti-abuse provisions, or the general anti-avoidance rule. ULC status and any foreign check-the-box election do not eliminate Canadian transfer-pricing, withholding, corporate-residence, or beneficial-ownership analysis.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and fraudulent reporting

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution may result in fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment of up to five years, reassessment of tax, gross-negligence penalties, interest, and seizure or collection proceedings.

Director personal liability: Directors may face prosecution, fines, imprisonment, civil recovery, director-liability assessments for source deductions and GST/HST, and restrictions arising from criminal enforcement. Piercing the corporate veil is exceptional, but fraud and misuse of the corporation materially increase personal exposure.
Failure to remit withholding tax

Statutory sanction: The corporation may be assessed for the unremitted amount, penalties, and compound interest. Penalties may apply for failure to deduct, late remittance, late NR4 filing, or knowingly assisting a false treaty claim.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for unremitted source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts where statutory due-diligence defenses are unavailable. Criminal liability may arise for wilful non-compliance.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under section 247, impose a 10% transfer-pricing adjustment penalty on qualifying adjustments, deny deductions, impose withholding consequences, and apply interest. T106 non-compliance may generate separate penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors are generally not personally liable merely because a transfer-pricing adjustment is made, but personal exposure may arise from fraud, wilful participation, source-deduction failures, obstruction, or knowing submission of false records.
Thin capitalization and excessive related-party debt

Statutory sanction: Excess interest may be denied, recharacterized for tax purposes, included in income of a related party under applicable rules, and subject to withholding, transfer-pricing, interest, and penalty consequences.

Director personal liability: Personal liability is not automatic, but directors may face exposure for deliberate tax fraud, false solvency representations, improper distributions, or unpaid source deductions.
Shell company and beneficial-ownership concealment

Statutory sanction: Treaty benefits may be denied under beneficial-ownership, limitation-on-benefits, principal-purpose, or anti-avoidance rules. CRA may reassess withholding and income tax, impose penalties and interest, and refer fraudulent conduct for prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face civil restitution, criminal prosecution, fines, imprisonment, and asset-seizure consequences where they knowingly participate in concealment. Travel restrictions may arise only through applicable court or criminal-process orders, not automatically from corporate tax debt.
Customs and import misclassification

Statutory sanction: The Canada Border Services Agency may reassess duties and taxes, impose administrative monetary penalties, seize goods, suspend or revoke privileges, and pursue prosecution for serious violations.

Director personal liability: Directors may face prosecution or personal liability where they authorized false customs declarations, obstructed enforcement, or participated in fraud. Corporate insolvency does not necessarily eliminate customs claims.
AML, proceeds of crime, and sanctions

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include transaction blocking, asset restraint or forfeiture, administrative penalties, criminal fines, and imprisonment under applicable Canadian anti-money-laundering, proceeds-of-crime, and sanctions legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal prosecution, imprisonment, fines, asset forfeiture, director disqualification in appropriate proceedings, and court-ordered travel or release conditions. Civil restitution may be ordered where victims or the Crown establish recoverable loss.
False books, obstruction, and document destruction

Statutory sanction: The corporation may face failure-to-keep-records penalties, compliance orders, reassessment based on available evidence, gross-negligence penalties, and criminal charges for obstruction or false statements.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors and officers may face fines, imprisonment, contempt or enforcement orders, civil recovery, and personal assessments where statutory director-liability provisions apply.
Unlawful profit distributions and insolvency conduct

Statutory sanction: Distributions may be challenged or recovered, and directors may be ordered to repay improper amounts. Tax, payroll, GST/HST, creditor, and insolvency claims may proceed concurrently.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal repayment obligations, civil damages, source-deduction liability, oppression remedies, and insolvency-related claims. Criminal consequences require additional evidence of fraud or other offence conduct.
Failure to maintain corporate and beneficial-owner records

Statutory sanction: The corporation may face corporate-law penalties, tax penalties, adverse audit assumptions, denial of deductions, and inability to defend residence, treaty, ownership, or arm's-length positions.

Director personal liability: Directors may face fines or enforcement proceedings for statutory record failures and may lose due-diligence defenses. Personal criminal liability generally requires knowing or reckless participation in the violation.
