Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) - GST/HST Program, Business Registration Online portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/register-business-online.html); Alberta corporate registry via Corporate Registry System (CORES) for provincial incorporation, but tax account registration is exclusively with CRA

E-government filing portal

Foreign entities are not required to obtain a Canadian business identification number unless they carry on business in Canada or derive revenue from Canadian taxable supplies or permanent establishment nexus. Once nexus exists, apply for a CRA Business Number (BN) and GST/HST account via Business Registration Online, by mail (RC1 form), or by telephone. Non-resident applicants must provide evidence of Canadian representative or agent, describe the nature and location of Canadian activities, and disclose the foreign jurisdiction of incorporation and physical operating address. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation for Canadian federal and Alberta purposes: a ULC is treated as a corporation under the Income Tax Act (Canada), taxable on worldwide income if resident, or on applicable Canadian-source income if non-resident. Pass-through eligibility exists only for foreign tax classification purposes (e.g., U.S. check-the-box election), not for Canadian federal tax purposes. Alberta does not impose separate general corporate income tax distinct from federal administration; Alberta has a flat corporate tax rate applied on allocated taxable income. The ULC retains unlimited shareholder liability, but for Canadian tax purposes it is a separate legal taxpayer.

Available election forms

No federal or Alberta statutory 'check-the-box' election exists; the CRA does not allow an unlimited liability corporation to be fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax. However, a foreign-resident ULC may file IRS Form 8832 to elect disregarded entity or partnership status for U.S. federal income tax in respect of U.S.-source activities. For Canadian hybrid mismatch rules, taxpayers must consider the OECD Multilateral Instrument and section 18.2 of the Income Tax Act, but no elective hybrid mechanism is generally available. Alberta corporate allocation for a ULC with a permanent establishment in multiple provinces follows Technical Interpretation and Regulations 401 Rules, requiring a T2 corporate tax return and Schedule 5 allocation, with no separate provincial election required; Alberta accepts the federal taxable income base subject to provincial allocation and any province-specific tax credits.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies and most exports), measured over any consecutive four calendar quarters; if the threshold is exceeded, a GST/HST account is mandatory. A non-resident carrying on business in Canada through a permanent establishment must, if nominated, obtain a GST/HST registration number; small suppliers may voluntarily register to claim input tax credits and to zero-rate exports.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under sections 6 and 9 of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act for supplies of goods exported by the vendor or a commercial carrier to a destination outside Canada, and for certain qualifying services supplied to non-resident persons outside Canada. For wholesale distribution of acetate eyeglass frames, the supply is zero-rated if title to the goods passes to a non-resident purchaser for export, the goods are adequately identified as exported, and documentary proof of export is retained. Services performed on exported goods or services to non-residents in respect of goods exported outside Canada may also be zero-rated. The supplier must be registered and must not charge GST/HST on the export invoice; instead the supply is reported as zero-rated in line 101 of the GST/HST return. A GST/HST registrant may claim input tax credits for inputs used in the manufacture or distribution of exported frames; exports are not relieved by a separate 'export VAT refund' but rather through the zero-rating mechanism and the input tax credit system.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (B13A) or e-manifest (where required for controlled or regulated goods; if not required, a commercial invoice is the primary export document for low-risk goods)
  • Commercial invoice containing seller and purchaser identity, full description of acetate eyeglass frames, quantity, unit value, terms of sale, and destination
  • Bill of Lading (ocean, air, or truck) issued by the carrier evidencing transportation to a non-resident destination and dated sufficiently close to the invoice date
  • Packing list identifying the shipped frames and verifying physical movement across borders
  • Proof of payment in foreign currency (e.g., inbound SWIFT TT transfer, platform payment receipt) demonstrating the foreign purchaser remitted funds from outside Canada
  • Purchase order or sales contract with the non-resident distributor confirming the export nature of the transaction and the parties' nexus

Canada has no mandatory real-time electronic invoicing or continuous transaction control (CTC) clearance regime as of the assessment date; CRA accepts paper and PDF invoices. There is no required digital stamping or pre-clearance API gateway. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically (e.g., GST50 or GST34 schedules) if registered, and registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 150,000 are required to file and remit electronically (GST/HST NETFILE or via EFT). CRA does not require a digital fiscal invoice for export transactions, but exporters must retain auditable evidence of export including commercial invoices, bills of lading, and third-party transportation documents.

CRA may impose reassessments to collect unremitted GST/HST if zero-rating is claimed without substantiation; the supply becomes taxable at 5% and the taxpayer is liable for the uncollected output tax plus interest. Under the Excise Tax Act, every registrant that fails to obtain or retain adequate export evidence may have its zero-rating denied retroactively. Penalties may include a gross negligence penalty under subsection 285(1) of the Excise Tax Act of the greater of CAD 250 or 25% of the tax amount attributable to the false statement or omission, plus civil penalties for incomplete or misleading returns. Criminal liability may arise under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act (tax evasion) for any person who willfully evades or attempts to evade GST/HST, punishable by up to five years imprisonment and fines ranging from CAD 4,000 to CAD 100,000 (or double the amount evaded), in addition to clawback of refunds or input tax credit claims and interest compounded daily. Export claims for related-party transactions are subject to enhanced transfer-pricing scrutiny by CRA under section 247 of the Income Tax Act, which can apply adjusted arm's-length pricing and impose comparable penalties of 10% of the net adjustment.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains unrestricted CAD convertibility on both current and capital account. There are no exchange controls, no central bank approval requirement for profit remittance, and no limitation on dividends, interest, royalties, management fees, or shareholder loan repayances. The only practical hurdles are the solvency and declared-dividend requirements of the Alberta Business Corporations Act and Canadian non-resident withholding taxes under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act. FINTRAC will automatically receive bank filings for cross-border transfers exceeding CAD 10,000.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. A Canadian ULC engaged in wholesale distribution of ophthalmic eyewear frames may retain export proceeds in any convertible currency and hold them offshore or repatriate freely. Export documentation, commercial invoices, and customs declarations must be retained for Canada Revenue Agency income measurement and for the six-year record-keeping requirement.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (central bank); Department of Finance Canada and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions handle foreign exchange and banking-regime oversight. Canada has no separate Foreign Exchange Administration.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined federal (15%) and Alberta provincial (8%) general corporate rate; lower rates apply to Canadian-controlled private corporations on active business income up to the federal small business limit, with Alberta's provincial rate at 2% for eligible small business income

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% on dividends paid to a non-resident person under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act. Treaty-reduced rates apply, e.g., 10% portfolio / 5% substantial under the Canada-US treaty, 15%/5% under Canada-UK, 15% under Canada-Germany generally; 25% applies if the non-resident does not complete the required declaration and provide treaty residence proof.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not set a fixed threshold but the CRA expects contemporaneous documentation when intercompany transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1 million annually; a Master File and Local File are generally required for MNE groups with consolidated revenue above CAD 10 million; the Country-by-Country Report threshold is CAD 750 million consolidated group revenue. Transfer pricing penalties may apply to adjustments exceeding the lesser of 10% of gross revenue and CAD 5 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada enforces economic substance through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under section 245, transfer pricing rules, and the principal purpose test under the Multilateral Instrument. An Alberta ULC must maintain real employees, premises, decision-making authority, inventory risk, and distribution activities in Alberta; a shell or letter-box company will have income reallocated and lose treaty benefits. There is no standalone ESR regime like the UAE, but the OECD BEPS principles and CRA administrative practice impose an equivalent substance requirement, including for intercompany royalty payments and re-invoicing arrangements.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

GAAR / Treaty Abuse / Shell Company Structure

Statutory sanction: The CRA can deny tax benefits, reassess income under section 245 and transfer pricing rules, and apply the 50% gross negligence penalty under subsection 163(2); Transfer pricing penalty under subsection 247(3) can be up to 10% of the adjustment amount above the threshold; interest accrues from the original due date.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in the scheme may face personal liability for withheld taxes and civil assessments under subsection 160(1); in an unlimited liability company, directors and shareholders are jointly and severally liable for corporate debts including tax liabilities; criminal fraud charges may result in fines up to CAD 5 million and imprisonment up to fourteen years; travel bans and asset freezing can be ordered during criminal proceedings.
Tax Evasion / Wilful Non-Reporting

Statutory sanction: Section 239(1) of the Income Tax Act: fine between 50% and 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to five years; the 50% civil penalty under subsection 163(2) also applies; the Voluntary Disclosures Program does not provide immunity for fraud; CRA criminal investigations may lead to asset forfeiture and public naming.

Director personal liability: Directors who participate in the evasion can be criminally prosecuted as parties to the offence; each director can be convicted individually, with fines up to CAD 5 million and imprisonment; bail conditions often impose travel bans and passport surrender; a director's liability assessment may be issued for unremitted GST/HST and source deductions, requiring personal civil restitution.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Subsection 247(3) penalty of 10% of the adjustment amount where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of 10% of gross revenue and CAD 5 million; the CRA can adjust income and deductions; double taxation may result unless a mutual agreement procedure is invoked; gross negligence penalty of 50% may apply for wilful mispricing.

Director personal liability: Directors who certified the T2 return knowing the intercompany pricing to be false may face criminal liability under subsection 239(1); the unlimited liability nature of the ULC allows the CRA and creditors to pursue director personal assets for the adjusted corporate tax debt; travel bans may be imposed in criminal proceedings.
AML / FINTRAC Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties under the PCMLTFA of up to CAD 500,000 for reporting entities; criminal money laundering under section 462.31 of the Criminal Code carries up to ten years' imprisonment; failure to file Form T1135 on designated foreign assets above CAD 100,000 triggers a penalty of CAD 2,500 plus CAD 100 per month up to CAD 12,000, and gross negligence penalties of 5% of the asset value up to CAD 24,000.

Director personal liability: Directors who directed or acquiesced in AML non-compliance can be personally penalized by FINTRAC under section 75.2; criminal convictions result in imprisonment, asset forfeiture, and travel restrictions; the ULC's unlimited liability structure permits seizure of director personal assets for corporate fines and restitutions.
Late Filing / Failure to Remit Part XIII Withholding

Statutory sanction: Late filing penalty of 5% of unpaid tax plus 1% per month up to 12 months; second offence penalty of 10% under subsection 162(2); failure to withhold Part XIII makes the corporation liable for the full amount under subsection 215(1); gross negligence penalty under subsection 227(10) of 50% of the unremitted amount; information return penalties of CAD 25 per day up to CAD 2,500, escalating to CAD 1,000 per day for large corporations.

Director personal liability: Under subsection 227.1(1), directors are personally liable for unremitted Part XIII withholding and source deductions; the CRA can issue director assessments without court order; due diligence is the only defence; after criminal indictment, travel bans and passport surrender may be imposed, and unlimited liability provides civil remedies against director assets.
