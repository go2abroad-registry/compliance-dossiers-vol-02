E-government filing portal

Foreign-owned entity must obtain a federal GST/HST account by registering with CRA (Form RC1 or online via My Business Account), providing Canadian business address, anticipated Canadian revenues, and appointed authorized representative; if carrying on business in Alberta, file extra-provincial registration with the Alberta Corporate Registry; no separate Alberta income tax return for ULC beyond the general federal/provincial coordination.

No mandatory national e-invoicing or real-time fiscal clearance in Canada; CRA accepts electronic invoices and records under GST/HST section 286 compliance. Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full free-float convertibility without exchange control restrictions. Canada imposes no capital account controls, no licensing of inward direct investment for wholesale trading (Investment Canada Act notification-only below jurisdictional review thresholds, and a greenfield Alberta ULC is not a reviewable acquisition), and no restrictions on CAD repatriation of dividends, interest, intercompany loan repayments, or liquidation proceeds. A Canadian-resident ULC is a treaty-eligible resident under Article IV of the Canada-US Tax Treaty; Part XIII non-resident withholding on dividends is generally reduced under Article X (5% for a US corporate beneficial owner holding 10% or more voting rights, 15% portfolio), and arm's-length interest is exempt under Article XI. No sovereign approval is required to hold USD or EUR operating balances, and CAD weakness is market-determined only.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Under the Currency Act and the PCMLTFA framework, Canadian law prohibits any mandatory surrender, conversion, or channeling of export proceeds; there is no statutory timeline and no conversion ratio applicable to exporters. The Alberta ULC exporting secondhand hydraulic earthmovers (HS 8429.52 / NAICS 423810) may retain 100% of USD/EUR proceeds in any convertible offshore or domestic foreign-currency account without Bank of Canada approval. The only compliance obligations are: (i) bank-filed FINTRAC Electronic Funds Transfer Report for each international EFT over CAD 10,000 remitted to the Minister within 5 business days; (ii) retention of the B13A export declaration, commercial invoice, and proof-of-payment reconciliation for the 6-year CRA record-retention window under ITA 230(1); and (iii) normal accrual of the realized or unrealized FX gain/loss for corporate income tax purposes.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with prudential oversight of payment and clearing systems executed by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and AML/ATF reporting to the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Low for settlement-critical wire and LC flows; Medium only as ancillary collection/FX conversion rails. Canada does not recognize the EU/UK EMI license class; fintechs (Wise Canada, Payoneer, Knightsbridge) operate as FINTRAC-registered Money Service Businesses (MSBs) under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), without Schedule I Bank Act deposit-taking authority, CDIC insurance, SWIFT MT700 issuing capability, or direct CHIPS/Fedwire clearing. They may be used for sub-CAD 100,000 expense payments and small USD collection sweeps, but cannot serve as the primary treasury account for the CAD 500,000 to CAD 5,000,000 per-transaction used-excavator settlement profile. An offshore EMI (e.g., UK EMD or EU EMI) may not lawfully market deposit accounts into Canada absent a Representative Office or Schedule I license under the Bank Act and would additionally breach FINTRAC MSB registration requirements.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal plus 8% Alberta. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access a combined 11% rate on up to CAD 500,000 of eligible active business income: 9% federal plus 2% Alberta, subject to business-limit and associated-corporation rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to an applicable treaty reduction. Common treaty outcomes include 5% for a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting ownership thresholds and 15% for other qualifying portfolio dividends; the Canada-United States treaty commonly provides 5% for a qualifying 10% corporate owner and 15% otherwise.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not use a single comprehensive local-file threshold for all transactions. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires contemporaneous documentation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions and the documentation must be prepared by the filing deadline and provided to the CRA within three months of request. T106 reporting generally applies where reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed the applicable aggregate threshold, commonly CAD 1 million. A transfer-pricing penalty can apply where the net adjustment exceeds the statutory CAD 5 million or 10% of gross revenue tests, subject to the legislation.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate United Arab Emirates-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Canadian transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse provisions, beneficial-ownership principles, sham and form-versus-substance doctrines, interest-deductibility rules, and specific anti-avoidance rules can nevertheless deny artificial arrangements. A ULC should maintain Canadian commercial purpose, decision-making evidence, personnel or appropriately documented outsourced functions, premises or operational capability, inventory controls, arm's-length pricing, and records demonstrating actual machinery trading activity. A Canadian ULC may be fiscally transparent for foreign tax purposes only under foreign-law classification; Canadian corporate taxation is not automatically converted to pass-through treatment.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Return

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act offences may result in prosecution, fines generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, and imprisonment of up to five years. Civil reassessments, gross-negligence penalties, interest, and loss of deductions may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors may face criminal prosecution where they participated, authorized, acquiesced, or wilfully failed to prevent the offence. Directors can also be assessed personally for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST under statutory director-liability rules, subject to due-diligence defences. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty but may arise from criminal process or enforcement orders; imprisonment is possible after conviction.
Failure to Remit Payroll, GST/HST, or Part XIII Withholding

Statutory sanction: Trust-account assessments, prescribed interest, late-remittance penalties, gross-negligence or failure-to-remit penalties, collection action, and prosecution may apply. The corporation may lose access to certificates and face account garnishment.

Director personal liability: Directors can be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST where statutory conditions are met, generally subject to a two-year limitation period after ceasing to be a director and a due-diligence defence. Personal assets may be seized following assessment and collection.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under section 247, impose transfer-pricing penalties, deny deductions, impute income, assess withholding tax, and apply interest. T106 and contemporaneous-documentation failures create additional penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable solely because of a corporate transfer-pricing reassessment, but personal liability may arise for knowing participation in fraud, statutory remittance failures, or other offences. Civil restitution and criminal sanctions may follow deliberate evasion.
Treaty Abuse and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: The CRA may deny treaty relief, reassess withholding at 25%, impose interest and penalties, and apply the general anti-avoidance rule or treaty anti-abuse provisions.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur personal exposure for knowingly false certifications, fraudulent withholding practices, or unremitted tax. Ordinary corporate treaty reassessment does not by itself pierce the corporate veil.
Shell Company and Economic-Substance Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Benefits may be denied under substance, sham, GAAR, transfer-pricing, residence, and treaty principles. The corporation may face reassessment, withholding exposure, interest, penalties, audit escalation, and loss of GST/HST input tax credits.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure where they knowingly participate in false records, tax evasion, fraud, or statutory remittance defaults. Veil piercing is exceptional and fact-dependent; criminal conviction can result in fines and imprisonment.
AML, Proceeds of Crime, and Sanctions

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include FINTRAC compliance measures, suspicious transaction reporting failures, administrative monetary penalties, seizure, forfeiture, prosecution under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and Criminal Code, and sanctions-related offences.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be prosecuted for knowing participation, counselling, laundering, sanctions evasion, or obstruction. Convictions can result in substantial fines, forfeiture, imprisonment, and court-imposed conditions including passport surrender or travel restrictions.
Corporate Records and Beneficial-Ownership Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Corporate-law penalties, tax reassessment based on available evidence, denial of deductions or credits, audit compulsion, and offence proceedings may apply. Missing records can shift evidentiary risk materially against the corporation.

Director personal liability: Directors may face offence liability for statutory non-compliance and personal exposure for intentional destruction or falsification of records. Criminal proceedings can generate bail conditions, travel restrictions, fines, and imprisonment.
Customs and Import-Valuation Fraud

Statutory sanction: CBSA reassessment, seizure, forfeiture, monetary penalties under customs legislation, interest, administrative penalties, and criminal prosecution may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers can face personal criminal liability for knowing customs fraud or false declarations. Corporate liability is primary for ordinary reassessment, but fraud, obstruction, and conspiratorial conduct can result in fines, imprisonment, forfeiture, and travel restrictions.
