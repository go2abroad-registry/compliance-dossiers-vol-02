Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; Business Registration Online (BRO) portal and My Business Account portal at cra.gc.ca

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada or making taxable supplies in Canada must obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN) and a GST/HST program account (RT prefix) by filing Form RC1 through CRA Business Registration Online. An Alberta Unlimited Liability Company (ULC) incorporated under the Alberta Business Corporations Act is registered with the CRA through the integrated corporate incorporation/registration process; Alberta corporate income tax registration is automatically administered by the CRA under the Alberta Corporate Tax Act collection agreement. GST/HST registration is mandatory once a non-resident's worldwide taxable supplies exceed C$30,000 per calendar quarter or over the preceding four quarters; voluntary registration is available below this threshold. The BN is the universal tax identification number, and the RT program account is the GST/HST identifier for returns, assessments, and rebates.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Check-the-box election under IRS Form 8832 and Article IV(6)(a) of the Canada-United States Income Tax Convention permitting US-resident shareholders to treat the Alberta ULC as a disregarded entity or partnership for US federal tax purposes. For Canadian domestic income tax purposes, the ULC is deemed a 'foreign corporation' under s. 248(1) of the Income Tax Act and remains subject to entity-level Part I tax and Part XIII non-resident withholding; Canada has no domestic check-the-box regime, and hybrid mismatch rules (ss. 18.4 and 18.5 ITA) apply to deny or recharacterize deductions for interest or other payments where the ULC is fiscally transparent in a foreign jurisdiction but treated as a corporation in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of tangible personal property are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act (ETA) where the vendor exports the goods from Canada in its own name and delivers them to a non-resident buyer outside Canada; the vendor must retain proof of export. For a ULC re-invoicing hub under ISIC 4690, zero-rating applies only where goods are physically present in Canada at the time of supply and subsequently exported; pure offshore re-invoicing transactions where goods never enter Canada fall outside the scope of Canadian GST because the supply is not made in Canada. Services supplied to non-residents, including intermediary, commissionaire, and brokerage services, are zero-rated under ss. 5-7 of Schedule VI where the service is supplied to a person who is not resident in Canada, is not received by the recipient in Canada, or relates to real property situated outside Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A) filed electronically with the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) within 15 days of exportation
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical shipment from Canada to the foreign destination
  • Commercial Invoice and Sale or Agency Contract evidencing the legal terms and foreign buyer identity
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank payment confirmation evidencing receipt of proceeds from the non-resident buyer

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls, or digital stamping of invoices. CRA accepts standard electronic and paper invoices; the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Framework based on the Peppol standard is being phased in from 2024 and remains voluntary for B2B transactions. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically through CRA GST/HST NETFILE, and the Books and Records requirements in GST/HST Memorandum 17-1 mandate that vendors retain complete export-evidence documentation sufficient to substantiate zero-rating.

CRA may reassess within the normal 4-year reassessment period, which is unlimited where misrepresentation, neglect, carelessness, wilful default, or fraud is involved, to claw back zero-rated treatment and assess unreported GST at 5% plus arrears interest and penalties. A 25% gross-negligence penalty under s. 285.1 ETA applies to net-tax overstatements or rebate overclaims attributable to gross negligence; knowingly making a false statement in a return attracts civil penalties and potential criminal prosecution under s. 239 of the Income Tax Act and the Excise Act, 2001. Directors and officers of the ULC may be jointly and severally liable for unpaid GST/HST, interest, and penalties under s. 323 ETA where they knew or ought to have known of the failure to remit or the fraudulent export documentation.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

CAD is fully convertible on both current and capital account; Canada accepted IMF Article VIII obligations in 1952 and maintains zero exchange-control legislation. Profit remittance hurdles are purely tax-driven rather than regulatory: dividends payable by an Alberta ULC to a foreign parent attract Part XIII withholding tax of 25% statutory, reduced under treaty (Canada-US Treaty Article X to 15% generally and to 5% for a corporate parent owning 10% or more of the voting shares; for an Alberta ULC electing under Canada-US Treaty Article IV(6)/(7), treaty benefits are preserved as if the ULC were a US corporation). No central-bank approval or capital-account authorization is required for dividend repatriation or liquidation proceeds transfer. The only mandatory friction is statistical: any single international transaction or aggregate monthly flow exceeding CAD 30,000 must be reported to Statistics Canada under the Balance of Payments reporting framework; FINTRAC requires reporting of virtual-currency and EFT transfers of CAD 10,000 or more where applicable.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion timeline, and no retention cap on export proceeds. A Canadian exporter may hold foreign currency indefinitely in any currency. For the re-invoicing hub, the operative rules are: (i) Income Tax Act section 39(2) requires all foreign-currency income, receivables and payables to be measured in CAD at the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate, with FX gains/losses recognized on revenue account; (ii) Statistics Canada Form BP-104 must be filed by the ULC where monthly cross-border receipts exceed CAD 30,000; (iii) FINTRAC reporting applies to physical cross-border currency movements of CAD 10,000 or more; (iv) no surrender-to-bank obligation exists for invoices settled in USD, CNY or other currencies.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC) is the monetary authority, wholesale settlement operator (LVTS/CLS) and lender of last resort; the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) is the prudential supervisor of Schedule I/II federally chartered banks; FINTRAC is the financial intelligence unit for AML/ATF; Statistics Canada administers international transaction reporting (Form BP-104); the Department of Finance Canada sets exchange-rate policy and holds statutory powers under the Currency Act and the Financial Administration Act. Canada maintains no dedicated centralized foreign-exchange control agency.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Low-to-Medium as a primary treasury rail; Medium as a supplementary collection and FX-optimization accelerator. Legal constraints: Canada has no EMI authorization class analogous to the UK FCA regime; under the Bank Act, only Schedule I/II banks and licensed trust companies may take deposits. Offshore digital EMIs (Wise Business, Airwallex, Qonto, XTransfer) can register with FINTRAC as Money Services Businesses to perform currency exchange and remittance, but they cannot legally hold the ULC's settlement liquidity or provide overdraft/escrow services. For a re-invoicing hub projecting annual throughput above CAD 5 million, a pure-EMI architecture creates sanctions-screening discontinuity, counterparty settlement risk, and liquidity coverage gaps. Recommended structure: Schedule I bank as the single treasury spine; a FINTRAC-registered EMI (e.g., XTransfer for CNY-denominated supplier payments) used only where all-in FX plus fee cost is at least 15 basis points cheaper than the bank corridor, with nightly auto-sweep of balances back to the bank and a contractual cap of 20% of monthly disbursement volume.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Equity injection is executed by SWIFT MT103 (telegraphic transfer) from the foreign parent to the ULC's CAD-denominated operational account. Remittance narrative must state precisely: Capital subscription - issuance of no-par common shares of [ULC Name] - and must be matched to a board resolution and an entry in the Alberta share register. Statistics Canada reporting obligation attaches when the credit exceeds CAD 30,000 in a calendar month (Form BP-104); for parent financing that gives the foreign holder a voting interest over 33.3%, the annual TFP-D foreign direct investment survey also applies. Use purpose-of-payment code INV (direct investment) on the SWIFT cover; a shareholder loan should be documented by a promissory note at the CRA prescribed rate, credited under code SAFE (shareholder advance), and subordinated to bank debt; both routes require the ULC to maintain a capital continuity schedule showing funds received are traceable to the corporate treasury and not to third-party omnibus sources. No reporting to the Bank of Canada is required, but the ULC must retain the stamped SWIFT confirmation, the share certificate register, and the parent's board resolution to defend against future source-of-funds questions.Treasury spine: (i) SWIFT MT103 individual wires for supplier settlements below CAD 250,000; (ii) Commercial Letters of Credit (sight LC under UCP600, or usance 30-90 days) for strategic suppliers above CAD 250,000, issued through the BMO, Scotiabank, or RBC trade finance desks; (iii) MT202 COV for nostro inter-bank settlement only where required by the receiving correspondent. Buyer collections: inbound MT103 wires from customers; documentary collections (DP/DA) for emerging-market buyers; open-account terms with invoice discounting or forfaiting on the ULC's receivable purchase facility to compress the 45-90 day cash conversion gap between LC payable and open-account receivable maturity; ancillary rail: XTransfer (XT) or a FINTRAC-registered EMI for CNY-denominated supplier payments where bank CNY corridor pricing is non-competitive, capped at 20% of monthly disbursement and subject to nightly auto-sweep. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility Rating: Low-to-Medium as a primary treasury rail; Medium as a supplementary collection and FX-optimization accelerator. Legal constraints: Canada has no EMI authorization class analogous to the UK FCA regime; under the Bank Act, only Schedule I/II banks and licensed trust companies may take deposits. Offshore digital EMIs (Wise Business, Airwallex, Qonto, XTransfer) can register with FINTRAC as Money Services Businesses to perform currency exchange and remittance, but they cannot legally hold the ULC's settlement liquidity or provide overdraft/escrow services. For a re-invoicing hub projecting annual throughput above CAD 5 million, a pure-EMI architecture creates sanctions-screening discontinuity, counterparty settlement risk, and liquidity coverage gaps. Recommended structure: Schedule I bank as the single treasury spine; a FINTRAC-registered EMI (e.g., XTransfer for CNY-denominated supplier payments) used only where all-in FX plus fee cost is at least 15 basis points cheaper than the bank corridor, with nightly auto-sweep of balances back to the bank and a contractual cap of 20% of monthly disbursement volume.
