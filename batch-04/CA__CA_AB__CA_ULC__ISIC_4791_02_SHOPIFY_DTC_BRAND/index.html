Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada — national tax authority for federal income tax and GST/HST. Primary portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-individuals/business-account.html) and Business Registration Online (BRO). Alberta corporate income tax is administered by CRA on behalf of the Province of Alberta Treasury Board and Finance under a federal-provincial Tax Collection Agreement. The statutory identifier is the 9-digit CRA Business Number (BN) plus program account suffixes (e.g., RT0001 for GST/HST; RC0001 for corporate income tax).

E-government filing portal

(1) Incorporate the Alberta Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) under the Alberta Business Corporations Act; the provincial corporate registry transmits incorporation data electronically to CRA, which auto-establishes the federal BN and corporate income tax program account. (2) Register the ULC for GST/HST through CRA Business Registration Online or by filing Form RC1 (and, for non-residents, Form GST355 'GST/HST Registration for Non-Residents'); CRA then issues the BN with an RT program account. (3) A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada through an establishment, warehouse, employee, or agent soliciting orders is deemed to carry on business in Canada (Excise Tax Act subsection 174(1)) and must register once gross worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 over four consecutive calendar quarters. (4) Exporters must also create a Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) exporter account through the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) portal and obtain a linked exporter identifier for filing B13A export declarations. (5) The ULC must open a Canadian-dollar bank account, display the BN on all invoices and customs documents, and maintain non-resident director/ownership records for CRA beneficial-ownership verification.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — the Alberta ULC is a taxable Canadian corporation for domestic Income Tax Act purposes (entity-level taxation on worldwide income), but it is eligible for fiscally transparent/pass-through treatment under statutory elections and treaty rules. It qualifies for the Excise Tax Act subsection 256.1(4) election to be treated as a partnership for GST/HST purposes, and, where all members are residents of treaty countries, it may be treated as a flow-through entity for foreign tax purposes, giving rise to hybrid entity characteristics that are constrained by Canadian hybrid mismatch rules. It is not a Tax-Exempt Zone entity and has no territorial exemption; Canada taxes resident corporations on worldwide net income.

Available election forms

(1) ETA subsection 256.1(4) check-the-box-style election: eligible entity election filed with CRA (Form GST26 series) to deem the ULC a partnership for GST/HST purposes, allowing members to be treated as partners; conditions include all members being individuals, or corporations resident in Canada, or residents of a country with which Canada has a tax treaty. (2) Income Tax Act subsections 18.2 and 18.4 hybrid mismatch defensive rules: deny interest deductibility where a deductible payment is made through a hybrid entity structure that produces a deduction without corresponding inclusion, including ULCs treated as disregarded entities in the United States. (3) Income Tax Act subsection 128.1(1) deemed disposition election upon change of residence status of the corporation. (4) Subsection 256.1(5) revocation election. (5) US federal Form 8832 check-the-box classification of the Canadian ULC may create a disregarded entity for US members; Canadian rules apply the MLI principal purpose test and treaty limitation-of-benefits provisions to counteract such mismatches.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — small-supplier threshold under ETA subsection 148(1): registration is mandatory once total worldwide taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated supplies) in the current quarter and preceding four calendar quarters exceed CAD 30,000; voluntary registration is available below the threshold. Non-resident suppliers of digital goods/services to Canadian consumers are subject to the simplified GST/HST registration regime without a Canadian permanent establishment, but generally retain the small-supplier exception only in narrow, Fact-limited cases.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods: zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, section 12 of the Excise Tax Act where the supplier exports the goods in compliance with the Customs Act, delivers them to a common carrier for export, or transfers possession to a purchaser who immediately exports them. Services and intangible property: zero-rated under Schedule VI, section 23 (intangible personal property) and section 34 (services) when supplied to a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST and the supply is not for consumption or use in Canada, excluding services related to real estate or tangible personal property situated in Canada. For the DTC brand: shipments of physical goods from Alberta/Canada to foreign ship-to addresses, downloads or cloud deliveries of digital products to non-resident customers, and subscription services rendered to non-residents all qualify, subject to proof of export, foreign destination, and settlement of foreign currency proceeds into the Canadian bank account of the ULC.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA B13A Export Declaration / CADEX electronic declaration where value exceeds CAD 2,000 or goods are regulated, with confirmation number and port of exit)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill naming the ULC as exporter and showing the foreign port of discharge or destination
  • Inward Remittance Certificate — SWIFT MT103, PayPal/Stripe settlement statements, or bank credit advice evidencing receipt of export proceeds in the ULC's Canadian bank account in foreign currency
  • Commercial invoice and Shopify order report showing the foreign ship-to address, product description, quantity, and declared value
  • Carrier tracking and proof-of-delivery records (terminal receipt, port loading document, or courier 'delivered abroad' scan) establishing physical departure from Canada
  • Customs broker confirmation and the exporter's books and records retained for at least six years as required by the Excise Tax Act and the Income Tax Act record-retention rules

Canada does not impose a real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control (CTC), or digital stamping mandate. No statutory API clearance or fiscal e-invoicing authorization is required at federal or Alberta level; however, GST/HST registered suppliers must issue tax invoices with prescribed content (ETA section 223) and retain records for six years. E-invoicing (EDI/PEPPOL or Shopify-generated invoices) is accepted, and returns must be filed electronically once thresholds are met. Effective July 2022, non-resident digital platform operators are deemed suppliers of GST/HST on marketplace sales made through their platforms; they must register, collect and remit tax, and provide prescribed seller data to CRA. A DTC brand selling through Shopify must verify whether the ULC or the Shopify platform operator holds the registration obligation depending on fulfillment location and seller-residency status.

If export proof is missing or defective, CRA denies the zero-rating, reassesses the supply as taxable, and claws back uncollected GST/HST at 5% on the gross selling price, plus compound daily interest under ETA section 280.1. The normal reassessment limitation period is four years (ETA section 296); the period is unlimited in cases of misrepresentation, fraud, or wilful default. Penalties include: failure-to-file penalty of 5% plus 1% per month (ETA section 280); gross-negligence penalty of the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the tax amount (ETA section 285); false-statement penalties and criminal tax evasion sanctions under ETA section 327 and Income Tax Act section 239, with fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and possible imprisonment. CRA's Exports Verification Unit and CBSA audits may deny related input tax credits, invoke the General Anti-Avoidance Rule, and reopen more than four prior years where documents are falsified; the Voluntary Disclosures Program may reduce penalties only if a qualifying disclosure is filed before the exporter is contacted by CRA.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is a fully convertible currency with no exchange controls or surrender obligations on current account transactions, trade settlements, dividend remittances, or capital account flows. Profit repatriation by a non-resident-owned Alberta ULC is generally unrestricted once statutory tax withholdings and arm’s-length transfer pricing documentation are satisfied; dividends, management fees, interest, and royalties are subject to Canadian withholding tax under the Canada-foreign treaty or Part XIII rates as applicable. There are no restrictions on maintaining foreign-currency bank accounts outside Canada; however, foreign-source capital inflows above CAD 10,000 are reportable to FINTRAC if in cash or virtual currency, and cross-border transfers may be subject to Canadian tax reporting (T1135 foreign income verification if applicable). Sanctions and anti-money-laundering measures (United Nations Act, Special Economic Measures Act, Freezing Assets of Corrupt Foreign Officials Act) can block transfers involving sanctioned jurisdictions or persons, so compliance screening is mandatory.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender requirements and no mandatory conversion of export receipts into CAD. Exporters may retain foreign currency in foreign or domestic bank accounts; however, the Canada Border Services Agency and Statistics Canada require accurate customs export declarations and trade-data reporting for goods movements, and FINTRAC requires transaction reporting for cross-border currency/electronic funds transfers exceeding CAD 10,000. For digital services sold via a Shopify DTC store, export proceeds are not deemed exports of physical goods for customs surrender purposes; the ULC must simply record revenue in CAD or functional currency under IFRS/ASPE and comply with the Income Tax Act’s section 12(1)(b) accrual rules where applicable.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC) together with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential regulation, and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for anti-money laundering/anti-terrorist financing compliance; no separate foreign-exchange control authority exists because Canada maintains a liberalized capital account.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium. Offshore digital EMI/neo-bank providers (e.g., Wise, Interpago, Airwallex) may serve as payment-collection or foreign-currency settlement layer, but they are not licensed to take domestic Canadian deposits. To hold CAD deposits or operate as a payment service provider in Canada, a foreign EMI must be registered as a money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC and, depending on activities, may need to comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) administered by the Bank of Canada. Using a foreign EMI as the sole bank is not viable for ULC treasury needs; a tier-1 Canadian bank is required for CAD clearing, credit facilities, and regulatory confidence. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% federal rate plus 8% Alberta provincial rate for a combined 31% general corporate income tax rate; Alberta's 2% provincial rate applies to income eligible for the small business deduction, resulting in a 25% combined rate on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income for a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC). A ULC is not a CCPC unless it meets the CCPC definition under subsection 125(7) of the Income Tax Act (Canada) (ITA), so the general rate of 31% is the default for most ULCs.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Under subsection 212(2) of the ITA, a dividend paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder is subject to Part XIII withholding tax at a statutory rate of 25% on the gross amount. For a ULC that is resident in Canada, this applies to distributions to non-resident shareholders, including deemed dividends on redemption or wind-up under subsection 84(2) and (3) of the ITA. Alberta does not impose a separate dividend withholding tax. For a non-resident ULC with a PE in Canada, a remittance of branch profits is treated as a dividend under subsection 219(1) of the ITA and is subject to the 25% Part XIII rate, reduced by treaty branch tax provisions to 5% under the Canada-US treaty if the non-resident owner is a US resident corporation and the business income is attributable to a PE.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed within six months after the end of the corporation's taxation year. For a December 31 year-end, the statutory filing deadline is June 30 of the following calendar year. A ULC with non-resident directors or shareholders must still file within six months of its fiscal year-end; no extension is available for non-residents except for a standard six-month filing deadline. If the corporation is non-resident and carries on business in Canada through a permanent establishment, the same six-month rule applies to the portion of income taxable in Canada. Balance due payment deadline: the full tax balance is due no later than the last day of the second month following the taxation year-end (i.e., February 28 for a December 31 year-end), and monthly instalments may be required if the corporation's federal/provincial tax payable exceeds CAD 3,000 in the current or prior year. Interest on late or deficient instalments compounds daily at the prescribed CRA rate (currently approximately 9% for 2025, set quarterly).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Section 247 of the ITA and the Income Tax Regulations (Canada) require a ULC that enters into transactions with non-resident non-arm's length persons to prepare a contemporaneous Transfer Pricing Master File and Local File if the total value of the controlled transactions exceeds CAD 1,000,000 gross for the taxation year for a Canadian participant, or if the value of transactions with a single non-resident associated enterprise exceeds CAD 50,000 for those involving intangible property or management fees, CAD 100,000 for financial transactions, and CAD 250,000 for other goods and services. For an Alberta ULC using a Shopify DTC store, the relevant thresholds are: CICA-based TP documentation requirement arises if the total amount paid or payable to non-resident non-arm's length persons (including payments for Shopify app subscriptions, service fees, trademarks, software licenses, and purchasing agencies) exceeds CAD 1,000,000. For intercompany services, the threshold is CAD 50,000 for specified services. The CRA may also require a Master File if the multinational enterprise group (MNE) has annual consolidated gross revenue of at least CAD 500,000,000, and the Local File must be filed if the Canadian entity has controlled transactions exceeding the CAD 1,000,000 threshold. Although Alberta has no separate transfer pricing legislation, the CRA administers the Canadian transfer pricing penalties and documentation requirements under sections 247(3) and 247(4) of the ITA. The penalty is a reduction of the adjustment amount, commonly 10% of the gross denial, and is reduced by the amount of any documentation that is provided within a specified period. The penalty is imposed in addition to the tax, under subsection 247(3), and can be as high as 10% of the adjustment amount, plus interest.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a general Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime like the United Arab Emirates, but the CRA has anti-avoidance rules and the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section 245 of the ITA that deny the tax benefit of transactions that would artificially reduce tax without real economic substance. Alberta does not have a separate economic substance doctrine. For a ULC, the CRA examines whether the entity is a 'shell company' or a conduit with no real commercial activity, which can trigger reassessment under GAAR, the 'sham' doctrine, and the 'agency' doctrine. A ULC must demonstrate an adequate level of substance through actual operations, business premises, employees, customer contact, bank accounts, and inventory or service capacity to sustain the tax filing position. The Economic Substance Doctrine in Canada is not an explicit legal statute; rather, the CRA applies the 'place of effective management' and 'business purpose' tests under common law and the OECD BEPS principals. The Canada Revenue Agency's administrative policy in IC 88-2, 'General Anti-Avoidance Rule', states that the GAAR may be applied when a transaction is an avoidance transaction, the avoidance transaction results directly or indirectly in a tax benefit, and the transaction is void or substantially void as regards to its real business purpose. If the ULC is viewed as a 'treaty shopping' entity, the Canada-US treaty Limitation on Benefits clause (Article 29) may deny treaty benefits and subject the ULC to 25% withholding. The Canada Revenue Agency also has the authority under subsection 247(2) to recompute the arm's length price where the terms and conditions of the transaction would not have been entered into by arm's length persons.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Wilful Non-Filing or False Return)

Statutory sanction: Wilful evasion is an indictable offence under section 239(1) of the ITA: fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and/or imprisonment for up to 2 years. Civil penalties under subsection 163(2) increase to 50% of the understated tax if the conduct is grossly negligent. The CRA may also impose the 'penalty of the highest amount' under subsection 163(1) for repeated omissions. Alberta will impose a corresponding provincial penalty on the provincial portion of the tax. CRA's Criminal Investigations Program may freeze bank accounts and seize assets.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for the corporation's assessed tax, interest, and penalties under subsections 227.1(1) and 160(1) of the ITA if they failed to deduct, withhold, remit, or pay source deductions and GST/HST. For a ULC, shareholders may also have unlimited liability; the corporate veil does not shield sole shareholders from the debts of the corporation under the Alberta Business Corporations Act. Directors who committed or acquiesced in the evasion may also be personally liable for the 50% penalty under subsection 163(2) and be subject to a travel ban under the Canada Evidence Act and the Mutual Legal Assistance in Criminal Matters Act, and to extradition. Imprisonment for up to 5 years is possible for tax evasion where fraud is proven.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure / Thin Capitalization

Statutory sanction: TP adjustment under section 247(2) in the amount of the difference between the arm's length price and the price charged; the adjustment is added to income. Penalty under subsection 247(3) is 10% of the gross adjustment amount, without a reduction for any offsetting adjustment, if the taxpayer fails to make or provide accurate records. Denial of interest deduction under the thin capitalization rules (subsection 18(4)) and an additional recharacterization penalty of 50% under subsection 18.2(5) if the interest is disallowed and not properly reported. Failure to file the required information return may also result in penalties under section 162(7) and the CRA can require a contemporaneous valuation study.

Director personal liability: Directors who signed the TP documents and were aware of the false information may be liable as 'persons who made or signed the return' under subsection 247(3) for the penalty. The CRA can assess directors personally under subsection 160(1) only for the unpaid tax of the corporation, not for the TP penalty unless the director is a person who participated in the avoidance transaction. Shareholders of a ULC are personally liable for the corporation's unpaid tax debts if the corporation has been struck from the register or if the shareholders received a distribution while insolvent, under the Alberta ABCA fraud provisions.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance / Sham

Statutory sanction: Under the sham doctrine and GAAR (section 245 of the ITA), the CRA may disregard the ULC and attribute all income, gains, expenses, and credits to the person who really earned them, resulting in full taxation at the owner's personal rate plus interest and the 50% gross negligence penalty. The CRA may also apply the 'Canadian-controlled private corporation' rules and revoke treaty benefits under the Canada-US Treaty Limitation on Benefits provision. The corporate entity may be considered not to have a permanent establishment, and the non-resident may be assessed under section 2(3) as carrying on business in Canada, attracting retroactive Part I tax, Part XIII withholding, branch tax under section 219, and a 10% penalty under section 247(3) if pricing is adjusted. Striking a ULC off the Alberta register for failure to file annual returns (Alberta Corporate Registry) does not extinguish shareholder liability; the corporate veil is ignored when the 'piercing the corporate veil' doctrine applies in equity or for tax fraud.

Director personal liability: A director or controlling shareholder who knowingly operates a sham ULC may be jointly and severally liable under the ABCA for torts, fraud, and trust fund breaches. The CRA can impose personal liability under subsection 160(1) for any transfer of property from the corporation to a shareholder for less than fair market value while the corporation owed tax, which occurs if the ULC pays no dividends while transferring cash to a parent. The corporate veil is pierced for fraud, and directors of the ULC may be personally liable for unpaid GST/HST and payroll source deductions, including fines up to CAD 50,000 and imprisonment for 2 years under subsection 227.1(1). Travel bans and surrender of passports can be ordered in criminal tax proceedings.
Profit Repatriation / Withholding Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: The non-resident payee is liable for Part XIII tax of 25% on the gross amount of the dividend or deemed dividend. The payer (the ULC) is liable for withholding the tax and remitting it by the 15th day of the month after the payment; failure to remit results in a penalty equal to 10% of the amount not remitted, plus interest, and a second penalty of 20% if the CRA sends a demand and the amount is not remitted within 90 days. The ULC is also liable for the gross amount of withheld tax, and the non-resident can be assessed under section 227(8) of the ITA. Failure to file NR4 summaries and information returns attracts a penalty of CAD 100 per day, capped at CAD 2,000, and penalties for false statements. Unpaid Part XIII tax can be assessed against the ULC directors personally under subsection 227.1(1).

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly participate in the non-withholding of Part XIII tax are personally liable for the full amount of the withholding, plus penalties and interest. In addition, the transfer of profits to a non-resident owner without withholding contravenes subsection 160(1); the non-resident transferee may be liable for the amount of the tax that the ULC failed to remit. If the ULC is used as a flow-through vehicle and the division of profits is a sham, the non-resident owner will be assessed personally under the ITA and the directors may be prosecuted. Alberta provincial penalty: late remittance of the Alberta provincial portion if any, and the CRA may issue a 'penalty for failure to remit' up to 20% of the unremitted amount. Imprisonment up to 2 years applies to each count of currency concealment under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act if the transaction is designed to avoid the reporting threshold.
