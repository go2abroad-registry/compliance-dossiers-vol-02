Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses/business-account.html); corporate registry: Alberta Corporate Registry (Alberta Registries)

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned Alberta ULC must first complete incorporation under the Alberta Business Corporations Act, then obtain a federal Business Number (BN) via online registration or Form RC1 ('Request for a Business Number'). The registration process establishes program accounts: RC0001 (corporation income tax), RT (GST/HST), RM (import/export), and payroll accounts where applicable. Supporting identification includes certificate of incorporation, Alberta registry extract, principal/director IDs, and non-resident ownership declarations. CRA issues the 9-digit BN and assigns a CRA program identifier immediately upon validation.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no domestic check-the-box election; the ULC is statutorily deemed a corporation under the Income Tax Act and files a T2 corporate return. The ULC is pass-through eligible only for foreign tax purposes: a U.S. federal Form 8832 election may treat the ULC as a disregarded entity or partnership, subject to the Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty tie-breaker (Article IV(6)). Canadian hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.4-18.6) may restrict deductions arising from any asymmetric classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies made in Canada (excluding GST/HST), measured over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters or a single calendar quarter; non-residents may voluntarily register below threshold

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Tangible personal property exported by the ULC in the course of commercial activity is zero-rated under section 23 of the Excise Tax Act. The supplier must ship the goods to a non-resident destination, transfer title and risk outside Canada, and retain evidence of export within a reasonable time (CRA administrative benchmark: within 60 days of sale/shipment). Services supplied to non-residents on a mail-order/COD e-commerce basis may be zero-rated under section 22 where the performed service is delivered for enjoyment outside Canada.

  • Canadian Export Declaration (B13A) or CERS electronic export transmission where export value equals or exceeds C$2,000, or for controlled/regulated goods
  • Commercial invoice / sales invoice indicating zero-rated (0% GST/HST) status and mail-order retail terms (ISIC 4791)
  • Bill of lading / airway waybill / carrier manifest covering the outbound shipment
  • Proof of export: carrier tracking, port manifest, destination customs entry or import clearance evidence
  • Foreign-buyer identification and destination evidence (address, contact details, buyer account records)
  • Cash-on-delivery settlement records: logistics/courier payout statements, local remittance reconciliation reports
  • Inward remittance certificate or bank/Airwallex FX statement demonstrating repatriation of export proceeds within 180 days
  • Sales contract / purchase order evidencing passage of title and risk to the non-resident purchaser

No real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control mandate, or digital stamping in force. GST/HST registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding C$1,500,000 must file electronically via GST/HST NETFILE or EDI. Pan-Canadian Peppol e-invoicing is voluntary.

CRA may deny zero-rating and reassess GST at 5% plus interest when export substantiation is incomplete. False or deceptive statements attract penalties under Excise Tax Act ss. 281.1 and 327: gross-negligence penalty of $100 plus 25% of the net tax sought, and criminal liability with fines and/or imprisonment up to five years for wilful evasion. Misrepresentation extends the normal four-year reassessment period to up to ten years and permits retrospective clawback of export refund claims. Canada imposes no statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline, but failure to evidence 180-day repatriation raises audit risk of diverted proceeds or fabricated export transactions.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is a freely floating currency fully convertible on both the current and capital accounts. Canada observes IMF Article VIII obligations and imposes no exchange-control restrictions on profit remittance, dividend distribution, loan repayment, or liquidation proceeds. The operative hurdles are tax and documentary only: (i) Part XIII non-resident withholding tax applies at a statutory rate of 25%, generally treaty-reduced to 5% to 15% on dividends and 0% to 10% on arm's-length interest under the 1980 Canada-United States Tax Convention (as amended by the 2023 Protocol); (ii) an Alberta ULC owned by a US person can elect flow-through treatment under subsection 216.3(1) of the Income Tax Act so that the US shareholder bears the Canadian tax on the ULC's income; CRA form T2054 and a Canadian tax memorandum must be in place before any distribution; (iii) combined federal (15%) and Alberta (8%) corporate income tax reaches approximately 23% on Alberta-allocated taxable income, and the ULC will not qualify for the federal small business deduction when its controlling shareholder is a US resident. The only material convertibility constraint in this scope is source-country: emerging-market cash-on-delivery collections denominated in restricted currencies must first be exchanged by a locally licensed money changer into USD or EUR through a regional clearing hub before the balance can be wired to Canada.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no mandatory surrender, conversion timeline, or conversion ratio for export proceeds. The exporter may hold foreign currency balances indefinitely in any Canadian Schedule I bank account or FINTRAC-registered multi-currency wallet. Obligations that do apply: (i) a CBSA B13A Export Declaration must be filed through the Canadian Export Reporting System for each shipment exceeding CAD 2,000, matched to invoice and airway-bill data; (ii) Statistics Canada's Canadian International Merchandise Trade and Balance of Payments surveys require periodic reporting of export values, currencies, and financial flows; (iii) FINTRAC cross-border currency reports are due on physical cash or monetary instruments crossing the border in either direction above CAD 10,000, including cash carried by third-party logistics agents in COD corridors; and (iv) for a ULC with a US shareholder, the controlled foreign affiliate rules (sections 91 to 95 of the Income Tax Act) may attribute income of any foreign incorporated collection entity, and Part XIII deems dividends on certain shareholder-level loans. There is zero forced conversion ratio; banks report FX conversions for statistical purposes only.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) - the central issuing and monetary authority - in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential supervision of Schedule I banks and authorized foreign-exchange dealers; the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML/CFT surveillance of cross-border remittances and money services businesses; the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) and Statistics Canada for export declarations and balance-of-payments statistics; and Global Affairs Canada for economic sanctions administration. Canada maintains no dedicated foreign-exchange-control commission; exchange policy under the Bank of Canada Act and the Currency Act is market-based, requiring no license or approval for private FX conversion or cross-border settlement.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File and Master File required if gross revenue exceeds CAD 50 million, or assets exceed CAD 10 million, or transactions with non-resident related parties exceed CAD 1 million (for services) or CAD 5 million (for goods). For this e-commerce entity, if cross-border related-party transactions (e.g., logistics or payment processing) exceed CAD 1 million, documentation is mandatory.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a separate ESR regime, but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and the 'permanent establishment' concept require real economic substance. A ULC must demonstrate actual business operations, employees, and decision-making in Alberta to avoid recharacterization. The CRA may apply the 'substance over form' doctrine to deny treaty benefits if the entity is a shell.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act (s. 239): fines up to 200% of tax evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years. Civil penalties: 50% gross negligence penalty on understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unpaid corporate tax withholdings (e.g., payroll, GST) under s. 227.1. For tax evasion, directors may face personal fines and imprisonment if they directed or acquiesced.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA can deny treaty benefits, reallocate income under s. 247 (transfer pricing), and apply GAAR to disregard the entity. Penalties: 10% of the adjustment amount for transfer pricing mispricing.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for the corporation's tax liabilities if the CRA pierces the corporate veil due to fraud or sham. Travel bans under the Income Tax Act (s. 238) for non-payment.
AML / Cash on Delivery (COD) Money Laundering

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA): fines up to CAD 2 million for corporations, criminal charges for directors/officers. FINTRAC administrative penalties up to CAD 1 million per violation.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally fined up to CAD 500,000 and imprisoned up to 5 years for knowingly failing to report or for laundering proceeds.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust taxable income and apply penalties of 10% of the net adjustment (if documentation incomplete). Interest on reassessments applies.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for penalties if they signed false or misleading documents. In severe cases, criminal prosecution for tax evasion.
