Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada, Business Registration Online portal and My Business Account at canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html; Alberta corporate registration administered by Service Alberta through Corporate Registry.

E-government filing portal

Register the Alberta ULC with Service Alberta Corporate Registry to obtain an Alberta Corporation Access Number, then register with CRA via Business Registration Online using the Certificate of Incorporation, Articles of Organization, Alberta Extra-Provincial Registration Certificate (if the ULC was formed in British Columbia and extra-provincially registered in Alberta), and director/officer identity records. CRA issues a 9-digit Business Number (BN) with sub-accounts: RT (GST/HST), RP (payroll), RC (scientific research) and RM (import/export). A non-resident officer of the ULC must appoint a Canadian resident representative for CRA correspondence; registration for GST/HST is mandatory under section 240 of the Excise Tax Act where the ULC carries on business in Canada, with the $30,000 small-supplier threshold generally available to resident Canadian entities. Foreign ownership of a ULC is permitted without Canadian residency requirements.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation: a ULC incorporated in Alberta is a 'corporation' under subsection 248(1) of the Income Tax Act (ITA) and must file a T2 Corporation Income Tax Return; it is subject to federal Part I tax at 15% and Alberta provincial corporate income tax at 11% (flat rate), with an 8% non-resident surcharge component in Alberta for non-resident-controlled corporate structures (Alberta Corporate Tax Act section 27) and no provincial capital tax. It is not eligible for flow-through treatment under Canadian domestic law; however, for US member purposes, the ULC may be treated as a disregarded entity under IRC § 7701 check-the-box, mitigating double taxation through ITA subsection 115.2(3) which deems the ULC's income to be that of its foreign member. Territorial scope: the ULC is taxable in Canada on worldwide income as a resident of Canada; Alberta taxation applies to allocable income under the Alberta Tax Act. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: a ULC incorporated in Alberta is a 'corporation' under subsection 248(1) of the Income Tax Act (ITA) and must file a T2 Corporation Income Tax Return; it is subject to federal Part I tax at 15% and Alberta provincial corporate income tax at 11% (flat rate), with an 8% non-resident surcharge component in Alberta for non-resident-controlled corporate structures (Alberta Corporate Tax Act section 27) and no provincial capital tax. It is not eligible for flow-through treatment under Canadian domestic law; however, for US member purposes, the ULC may be treated as a disregarded entity under IRC § 7701 check-the-box, mitigating double taxation through ITA subsection 115.2(3) which deems the ULC's income to be that of its foreign member. Territorial scope: the ULC is taxable in Canada on worldwide income as a resident of Canada; Alberta taxation applies to allocable income under the Alberta Tax Act. No tax-exempt zone applies to the POD business.

Available election forms

Election under ITA subsection 115.2(1) (deemed dividend treatment for ULC distributions to US-resident members, preserving the ULC's PUC and avoiding deemed excess distributions); US IRS Form 8832 check-the-box election for ULC classification as a disregarded entity; ETA section 111 election for financial services; CRA advance GST/HST ruling for cross-border POD service characterization; simplified non-resident GST/HST registration election under ETA sections 241.1 and 242 for direct selling; election under Regulation 4600 ITR respecting accrued pension contributions where applicable.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable worldwide revenue for the small-supplier exception (ETA section 148 and 306.1); once gross revenue exceeds CAD 30,000, GST/HST registration is compulsory. For non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada, registration is required regardless of the threshold (ETA section 240(1)); e-commerce and drop-ship supply of tangible personal property to Canadian customers may trigger deemed registration obligations under ETA section 240.1. A POD retailer with a Canadian address and Canadian fulfilment center will generally exceed the threshold and must charge GST at 5% on domestic supplies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under Schedule VI, Part V, section 1 of the Excise Tax Act is available for tangible personal property exported by the supplier (the Alberta ULC) where: (a) the ULC is the registered GST/HST entity; (b) the goods are delivered outside Canada by the supplier or by a common carrier/consignee; (c) the export occurs within 60 days of the tax otherwise being payable; and (d) the ULC retains sufficient documentary evidence of export within 6 years. POD merchandise shipped to a foreign retail customer (e.g., via USPS, UPS, DHL, FedEx cross-border service) qualifies. Where the POD platform contracts directly with a non-resident customer and the ULC only provides print production services in Canada, those services are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, section 10 only if they are performed directly on goods that are exported; digital POD software supplied to non-residents is zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, section 23 (supply of intangible property to non-residents not for use in Canada).

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (B13A) / Canadian Export Reporting System (CAED) report for shipments valued in excess of CAD 2,000; for low-value POD parcels, a courier-generated export manifest or e-commerce postal export record
  • Bill of Lading, Airway Bill, or courier certificate of export issued by a licensed carrier evidencing pickup in Canada and destination outside Canada
  • Commercial invoice and detailed packing list specifying the POD item description, HS classification, quantity, unit value, and foreign consignee name/address
  • Proof of delivery to the foreign address (courier tracking history, postal customs release record, or signed delivery confirmation from the international carrier)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank/fintech settlement statement showing receipt of foreign consideration from the non-resident customer or marketplace (Stripe or PayPal payout records in foreign currency)

No statutory real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or CRA pre-clearance API mandate applies in Canada. The CRA has adopted the Pan-Canadian Peppol network for electronic invoicing to federal government buyers, and the Business Payment Network as the voluntary interoperability standard; for private-sector B2B and B2C POD transactions, graded CRA-compliant SAP/ERP integration is expected under the record-keeping requirements in ITA section 230 and ETA section 286. POD invoices issued to Canadian customers must show the GST registration number, amount of tax charged, and supplier identity; no mandatory QR code or stamps apply.

CRA may reassess for 4 years, extended to 7 years for negligence or misrepresentation (ITA section 152(4)); for export zero-rating claims, CRA audits POD e-commerce exporters exclusively by document review — failure to produce the export evidence bundle described above within 30 days of a CRA request results in disallowance of the 0% rate with: (i) reassessment of GST at 5% on every unreported export; (ii) arrears interest at 10.25% compounded daily; (iii) a 'false statements or omissions' penalty of CAD 250 to CAD 25,000 under ETA section 285; (iv) a gross-negligence penalty of 25% of the net tax evaded plus 50% of any third-party civil penalty under ETA section 280; and (v) criminal prosecution under ETA section 327 for willful evasion with fines up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment up to 2 years. The Block Transfer and Anti-Avoidance Rules under ETA section 274 may also recharacterize artificial zero-rating structures; clawbacks apply retroactively to all input tax credits claimed on the underlying print and shipping costs.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility without prior exchange-control approval; Canada accepted IMF Article VIII obligations (Sections 2, 3, and 4) with effect from March 25, 1994. Dividend, interest, royalty, management-fee, and capital-repatriation flows are freely remittable, subject only to Part XIII withholding tax of the Income Tax Act (statutory 25%, treaty-reduced to 5%-15% under the Canada-U.S. Income Tax Convention and 10%-15% under the Canada-China Agreement) and to FINTRAC reporting thresholds. Under section 12 of the Currency Act, foreign-currency debts may be settled in Canadian dollars at the Bank of Canada spot rate; no exchange-control approval or FX allocation is required for a CA_AB ULC engaged in export POD retailing.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Statutory timeline: none. Mandatory conversion ratio: 0%. Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender, no repatriation deadline, and no compulsory conversion of export receipts; 100% of USD/EUR/CNH proceeds may be retained offshore or onshore indefinitely. Administrative duties are limited to: (a) balance-of-payments reporting on the Statistics Canada foreign direct investment and international transactions questionnaires; (b) FINTRAC cross-border EFT reporting for wire transfers exceeding CAD 10,000; and (c) the requirement under the Currency Act to maintain books and records in Canadian dollars or, alternatively, in the functional currency under a CRA-acceptable methodology.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), operating the Exchange Fund Account under the Minister of Finance; prudential banking supervision is delegated to OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions of Canada); AML/CTF supervision is delegated to FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada). Governing statutes: Bank of Canada Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. B-2), Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52), and Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (S.C. 2000, c. 17).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility: MEDIUM-HIGH as a supplementary treasury and payout layer only. Offshore digital EMIs (Wise, Payoneer, Airwallex, Mercury) may lawfully service the Canadian ULC only if the EMI is registered as a money services business with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA and is registered as a retail payment activity provider with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (S.C. 2021, c. 23, s. 233), effective September 30, 2024. Legal constraints: (i) these platforms are not Canadian Schedule I banks and do not hold CDIC-insured deposits; balances constitute uninsured stored value; (ii) an unregistered offshore EMI is prohibited from providing payment services to Canadian end-users; (iii) EMIs cannot issue commercial letters of credit, open escrow or trust accounts, participate in the LVTS clearing system, or act as a CRA reporting deposit institution; (iv) capital escrow for the CA_ULC must remain with a Schedule I bank to satisfy FINTRAC, CRA, and commercial lending due diligence. Recommended deployment: multi-currency batch payouts to POD suppliers, affiliate print shops, and minor trade disbursements below CAD 50,000.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate annual fair-market value of non-arm's length cross-border transactions. Master File is expected for MNE groups subject to CbC reporting (consolidated revenue ≥ EUR 750 million) and may otherwise be requested by CRA for high-value related-party transactions.

Economic substance scope: Alberta/Canada does not impose offshore Economic Substance Regulations. However, GAAR (ITA s.245), treaty anti-abuse rules under OECD MLI/PPT, and CRA's 'real business' tests require ongoing commercial substance. An Alberta ULC engaged in POD retail must maintain decision-making functions, employees, premises, contracts, and operational records in Alberta to resist treaty shopping or tax-avoidance challenges.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.239(1) criminal penalties up to 200% of evaded tax and imprisonment; Criminal Code fraud (s.380) up to 14 years; civil gross negligence penalty of 50% of understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false returns or direct concealment are personally prosecutable; they face criminal fines, imprisonment, CRA civil restitution, court-imposed travel bans, and personal asset garnishment. Fraud pierces the corporate veil.
Unremitted Part XIII Withholding Tax

Statutory sanction: Liability for the unwithheld amount plus interest and a 10% failure-to-remit penalty; director liability certificates are issued by CRA under ITA s.227.1.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are jointly and severally liable for the unremitted Part XIII amounts; CRA may pursue personal assets, place liens, and obtain travel bans against directors for wilful non-compliance.
ULC Shell Abuse / GAAR

Statutory sanction: GAAR denial of tax benefits; transfer pricing reassessment; net adjustment penalty of 10% of understated income where thresholds are exceeded; gross negligence penalty of 50%; ULC shareholders face unlimited liability for corporate obligations.

Director personal liability: Shareholders/directors of an unlimited liability company may be personally liable for all corporate debts and CRA assessments; directors who orchestrate the artificial arrangement may be subject to civil restitution and criminal prosecution for tax evasion.
AML / Payment Rail Abuse

Statutory sanction: Under the PCMLTFA and Criminal Code s.462.31, monetary penalties up to CAD 1,500,000 (up to CAD 2,000,000 for repeat violations) and imprisonment up to 10 years for laundering criminal proceeds; FINTRAC administrative penalties may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly facilitate money laundering are personally subject to fines and imprisonment; the corporate veil is pierced for AML violations, and CRA may impose director liability for taxes arising from illicit payments.
