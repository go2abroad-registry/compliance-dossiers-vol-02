Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; sole federal tax administration for GST/HST and for federal plus Alberta corporate income tax under the Federal-Provincial Tax Collection Agreement (Alberta files no separate provincial return; Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration handles only provincial non-income levies such as fuel and tobacco taxes). Registration portal: CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) at https://bro-cra-clrc.cra-arc.gc.ca and the federal Business Number portal at https://www.canada.ca/business-number.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate the Alberta ULC under the Business Corporations Act (Alberta) through the Alberta Corporate Registry; the federal 9-digit Business Number (BN) is issued automatically via the Integrated Business Registration one-window system. Step 2: Open program accounts under the BN through CRA BRO (or Form RC1) - RC for corporate income tax, RT for GST/HST (the RT account is the Canadian VAT-type identifier), RP for payroll, and RZ for import-export transactions. Step 3: A foreign non-resident entity not incorporated in Canada applies for a BN via BRO or Form RC1 and registers as a non-resident registrant where it carries on business in Canada or makes taxable supplies (including digital supplies) to Canadian consumers; Canada has no separate VAT ID - the BN serves as the universal TIN. Step 4: Obtain independently the Health Canada Natural Product Number (NPN) and site licence under the Natural Health Products Regulations; this is not a tax identification condition but is a statutory prerequisite to lawful e-commerce sales of dietary supplements.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: an Alberta ULC is a Canadian-resident corporation under the Income Tax Act by default, subject to federal Part I corporate tax and Alberta corporate income tax at the general combined rate of approximately 23% (15% federal basic rate + 8% Alberta general rate) where foreign-controlled; the CCPC small-business deduction (13% combined on first CAD 500,000 of active business income) is unavailable to a ULC controlled by non-residents. The entity is simultaneously Pass_Through_Eligible for U.S. federal tax purposes (disregarded-entity status) and, via the Canadian s. 216(5) election, may be re-characterized as a partnership domestically; absent that election, entity-level taxation is the statutory default.

Available election forms

1) Hybrid partnership election - subsection 216(5) of the Income Tax Act (Canada): a resident ULC may elect to be deemed a partnership for Canadian tax purposes using prescribed Form T26, provided all shares (except qualifying non-voting shares) are held by persons resident in Canada, the United States, or another tax-treaty country; the election is revocable via Form T27 and requires the deemed partnership to file T5013 information returns with members taxed directly on Canadian-source shares. 2) U.S. check-the-box - under IRC Section 7701 and Form 8832, the foreign member may elect disregarded-entity treatment of the ULC, creating the Canada-U.S. hybrid and aligning with the s. 216(5) domestic pass-through. 3) GST/HST does not automatically follow the income-tax election: the ULC remains the registrant and the person making taxable supplies unless the deemed partnership separately registers, and the general anti-avoidance rules (Income Tax Act s. 245 and Excise Tax Act s. 274) apply to elections lacking genuine commercial substance.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: the small-supplier threshold under ETA s. 148(1), tested on the registrant's and associates' worldwide taxable supplies in the current calendar quarter and the preceding four quarters, excluding supplies to non-residents. Canadian-resident e-commerce sellers exceeding CAD 30,000 in combined domestic and export supplies must register; non-resident digital suppliers to Canadian consumers are subject to the same CAD 30,000 threshold and must register under the simplified non-resident obligor framework (effective July 1, 2021). Voluntary registration is available below the threshold and permits full input tax credit recovery, which is advisable where significant zero-rated export purchases and Canadian operating expenses exist.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to goods exported from Canada under Schedule VI, Part V, s. 1 of the Excise Tax Act: a supply of tangible personal property made by way of sale is zero-rated where the supplier delivers or ships the property to a non-resident person for export and retains prescribed evidence of export. Nutraceutical and dietary supplement shipments qualify under this provision, provided physical export occurs and the seller has no basis to believe the goods will be returned. Services rendered to non-residents, including e-commerce platform, logistics-coordination, and marketing services, are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7(a) where they do not relate to real property in Canada or tangible personal property located in Canada at the time of performance. Digital and intangible supplies (e.g., online wellness content) are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, s. 10 when supplied to a non-resident other than for use in Canada. Zero-rated exporters with only exported supplies register voluntarily to recover input tax credits; a zero-rating claim requires the evidence of export to be obtained before the return is filed and retained for six years. Health Canada NPN/NHP licensing does not alter GST/HST character - supplements are taxable at 5% if sold domestically and zero-rated only on genuine export.

  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / postal or courier receipt evidencing physical export from Canada, showing shipper and consignee identity, description, weight, destination, and carrier details;
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank foreign-exchange/remittance statement evidencing payment and repatriation of export proceeds (Canadian banks do not issue statutory IRCs; CRA accepts SWIFT MT103 credits, bank statements, or treasury foreign-exchange conversion records)

Canada does not impose real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or mandatory tax-authority API invoice validation. Mandatory invoice content is prescribed by ETA s. 28(f1) and s. 84.1: supplier name or trading name, GST/HST registration number, invoice date, vendor name and city, buyer name and address, terms of payment, description of goods, amount, and tax payable. Electronic invoices and books must be retained in CRA-readable format for six years. CRA has voluntarily adopted the PEPPOL e-invoicing standard for business-to-government exchange; adoption is optional in the private sector. Mandatory digital filing applies to customs transactions: exporters and importers must file electronic export declarations and customs accounting data through the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) single-window portal (with the Canadian Export Reporting System for non-CARM filers), and low-value e-commerce parcels not exceeding CAD 2,000 are commonly exported using simplified postal (CN 22/CN 23) or courier customs documentation instead of the formal B13A declaration.

Canada imposes no statutory capital-control or FX-repatriation deadline under the Currency Act; the 180-day figure is adopted as the commercial benchmark applied by Canadian export financiers, insurers, and CRA transfer-pricing examiners under Income Tax Act s. 247 for arm's-length settlement of intercompany and third-party export receivables. Outstanding export balances beyond approximately 180 days without documented collection effort will trigger CRA audit scrutiny of economic substance, transfer-pricing adjustments, deemed interest, and denial of the zero rate. Substantive penalties: GST/HST reassessment at 5% on the exported supplies with compounded daily arrears interest (ETA ss. 280, 296); gross-negligence penalties of up to 50% of the tax sought (ITA s. 163(2); ETA s. 285); misrepresentation penalties under ITA s. 163 and ETA s. 285 extend the normal four-year reassessment window to indefinitely; criminal tax evasion fines up to 200% of tax evaded and imprisonment up to five years (ITA s. 239; ETA s. 327); false customs declarations are punishable by fines up to CAD 25,000 plus seizure or forfeiture of goods (Customs Act ss. 110, 153.1). Fraudulent export zero-rating schemes, including round-tripping goods back to Canadian consumers, are actively targeted by the CRA's GST/HST International and Large Business Audit Directorate, with retrospective clawback of ITCs and export zero-rating for a full look-back period.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility under IMF Article VIII, and the Canadian dollar is freely tradable with no surrender, pre-approval, or repatriation permit regime. Inbound equity and debt capital, operating cash flows, and profit remittances are unrestricted; the sole structural hurdle for a ULC is the Part XIII dividend withholding tax (statutory 25% on Canadian-source dividends, reduced to 5% or 15% for qualifying U.S. corporate shareholders holding at least 10% voting control under the Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty (1980)). No central-bank convertibility licensing, mandatory hedging, or prior-repatriation authorization applies to current-account payments or capital movements.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory timeline or mandatory conversion ratio exists for export receipts: a Canadian exporter may retain all proceeds in CAD or any foreign currency indefinitely. The only compliance obligations are (i) the CBSA Export Reporting System filing for statistical/economic data, (ii) FINTRAC reporting of international EFTs at or above CAD 10,000, and (iii) scheduling of receipts as business income under the Income Tax Act. No central-bank re-invoicing, compulsory acceleration, or surrender of foreign currency is imposed on nutraceutical export sales.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). Canada operates no dedicated foreign-exchange-control administration; the Minister of Finance and the Bank of Canada jointly supervise monetary and payment-system stability, while FINTRAC administers AML/EFT reporting thresholds and the Investment Canada Act (administered by ISED) governs foreign-investment notifications.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-to-High for settlement and disbursement rails only. Canada has no standalone EMI licensing regime; non-bank payment service providers must register with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (in force November 2024) and enroll with FINTRAC as money services businesses. Airwallex and Wise Business operate through Canadian licensed deposit partners and can provide CAD transaction accounts for supplier disbursements and marketplace payouts, but they cannot hold escrow capital, issue documentary letters of credit, or provide committed CAD/USD/CNH hedging lines. Legal constraint: e-money balances are not CDIC-insured deposits, and the ULC must maintain its primary relationship with a Schedule-I bank to secure trade-finance and hedging capacity.

Route the injection as a SWIFT MT103 from the foreign parent to the ULC's CAD-denominated operating account at a Schedule-I bank. The beneficiary narrative must include the purpose code 'EQUI' or 'CORP' and state 'Capital subscription to authorized share capital of ULC / shareholder loan – Part XIII compliance pending', together with the Alberta corporate registration number. The remitting bank must classify the transaction as a capital account transfer, not a goods/services payment, to avoid market-convertibility mis-coding. Incoming EFTs of CAD 10,000 or above are reportable to FINTRAC; where the foreign investor is a WTO-member national and the enterprise value exceeds the Investment Canada Act notification threshold (C$5 billion for 2024), a pre-implementation notification must also be filed with ISED. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium-to-High for settlement and disbursement rails only. Canada has no standalone EMI licensing regime; non-bank payment service providers must register with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (in force November 2024) and enroll with FINTRAC as money services businesses. Airwallex and Wise Business operate through Canadian licensed deposit partners and can provide CAD transaction accounts for supplier disbursements and marketplace payouts, but they cannot hold escrow capital, issue documentary letters of credit, or provide committed CAD/USD/CNH hedging lines. Legal constraint: e-money balances are not CDIC-insured deposits, and the ULC must maintain its primary relationship with a Schedule-I bank to secure trade-finance and hedging capacity.
