Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC). Business registration and tax administration are conducted through the CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO) portal and My Business Account service; customs and export controls are administered by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) and the Canadian Export Reporting System.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned Alberta Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) and applicable program accounts. The standard procedure is to submit Form RC1, 'Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts', or register online via Business Registration Online (BRO). Non-resident persons carrying on business in Canada must open a GST/HST program account if taxable supplies exceed the small supplier threshold or if they are non-resident digital-economy suppliers subject to the simplified GST/HST registration net. Alberta has no provincial corporate income tax collection system and no provincial sales tax, so no additional provincial tax registration is required for GST/PST; however, the ULC must separately file with the Alberta corporate registry for provincial corporate existence and obtain an Alberta incorporation number. Foreign ownership documentation, Canadian resident agent or service address, and beneficial ownership information must be provided to CRA as part of the BN registration and anti-money-laundering verification protocol.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation for Canadian federal income tax purposes, because the Alberta ULC is a resident corporation under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and is taxable on its worldwide income unless a treaty or domestic exemption applies. For U.S. federal tax classification purposes, the ULC may be treated as a Pass_Through_Eligible entity if a 100% U.S. owner files a check-the-box election to be respected as a disregarded entity or partnership. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation for Canadian federal income tax purposes, because the Alberta ULC is a resident corporation under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and is taxable on its worldwide income unless a treaty or domestic exemption applies. For U.S. federal tax classification purposes, the ULC may be treated as a Pass_Through_Eligible entity if a 100% U.S. owner files a check-the-box election to be respected as a disregarded entity or partnership. Alberta's territorial tax treatment is subsumed under the federal corporate income tax regime because Alberta uses the federal tax base and there is no provincial corporate income tax in Alberta. No tax-exempt zone treatment applies to standard e-commerce activity in Alberta.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a domestic check-the-box classification election akin to the U.S. regime. The principal classification election available for an Alberta ULC is the U.S. Form 8832 entity classification election, enabling wholly owned Canadian ULCs to be treated as disregarded entities for U.S. federal income tax purposes. For GST/HST purposes, a ULC may elect under section 239 of the Excise Tax Act to file a combined GST/HST registration with closely related corporations or branches under limited circumstances. Hybrid mismatch and treaty-shopping elections must be assessed under the Canada-U.S. Income Tax Convention (1980), OECD BEPS Action 2 rules, and the CRA's general anti-avoidance rules; no unilateral check-the-box election is available from the Canada Revenue Agency.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies made in Canada, measured over the current calendar quarter and the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters, for GST/HST small supplier registration purposes. Once exceeded, registration is mandatory. Non-resident digital-economy sellers and marketplace suppliers with a Canadian sales threshold may also be required to register under the simplified GST/HST non-resident registration framework.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A GST/HST registrant exporting tangible property may claim the 0% GST/HST rate under Division V and Schedule VI, Part V of the Excise Tax Act when the supplier has caused the goods to be exported, by shipment or transport, to a destination outside Canada and the exporter has kept sufficient evidence of export. The pet supplies must not be resupplied or used in Canada before export. Offshore services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated only where the service is performed wholly outside Canada or falls within the place-of-supply rules; online service components ancillary to the pet-products sales should be analyzed separately.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A or electronic Canada Export Reporting System export declaration where required)
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill showing the goods left Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank foreign-exchange settlement receipt evidencing payment from overseas buyer
  • Commercial invoice showing seller, buyer, description, quantity, value, and zero-rated tax status
  • Proof of export such as courier manifest, customs broker notes, or port/terminal release documents
  • Canada-United States export documentation (CUSMA-related manifest or cross-border trucking evidence) when applicable

Canada does not operate a national real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, or digital stamping requirement. GST/HST invoices must contain the required information under section 223 of the Excise Tax Act when the total consideration is over CAD 30, including supplier name or business number, date of invoice, total consideration, and amount of tax or a clear indication that the supply is zero-rated. Electronically generated invoices are acceptable to CRA, and digital records must be retained in machine-readable format. Alberta imposes no additional provincial e-invoicing mandate. Marketplace facilitators, payment processors, and fulfillment warehouses may be required to collect and report GST/HST on qualifying online retail transactions; those obligations should be independently verified in the e-commerce commercial model.

CRA may reassess a zero-rated export claim within the normal four-year period or beyond if misrepresentation, neglect, or fraud is established. False export zero-rating or refund claims are subject to clawback of the uncollected GST/HST, arrears interest, and civil penalties. Under section 285.1 of the Excise Tax Act, a false or negligent statement can attract a penalty equal to the greater of CAD 250 and 5% of the excess tax or credit claimed. Deliberate cases may be assessed under Gross Negligence sections of the Income Tax Act, exposing the taxpayer to a 50% gross negligence penalty. Criminal prosecution under section 327.2 of the Excise Tax Act or section 239 of the Income Tax Act can result in fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for repeat offenders. CRA also audits export substantiation through electronic commerce transaction files, inventory reconciliations, and cross-border payment data; failure to provide complete documentary support within the retention period is treated as a denial basis.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully convertible floating-currency regime with no general exchange control legislation, no currency rationing, and no prior approval requirement for capital account transactions; the Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely convertible on current and capital account, and non-resident shareholding and profit remittance by an Alberta ULC are unrestricted, with the sole direct frictions being the Investment Canada Act notification/review obligation for acquisitions of control and the Part XIII dividend withholding tax of 25% (default) reducible to 5% under the Canada-US Income Tax Convention if the ULC is interposed with the appropriate look-through certification, together with dividend withholding leakage rules affecting a ULC's deemed cost amount; current-account payments for e-commerce merchandise imports are fully liberalised, and no administrative exchange control license is required for royalty, commission, or trade payments

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender, conversion, or repatriation requirement exists under Canadian law: exporters may retain 100% of export proceeds in any currency, with no mandatory timeline for conversion; the only compliance obligations are (i) FINTRAC large-transaction reporting (CAD 10,000 or more), (ii) CBSA currency and monetary instruments reporting (CMIR, CAD 10,000 or more upon physical cross-border conveyance), and (iii) standard bank-held treasury covenants requiring repatriation within a commercially agreed number of days as a credit-risk overlay, not as a legal mandate

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) acting as the central monetary authority, with the Department of Finance Canada as policy lead on the Investment Canada Act (ICA) and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as the Financial Intelligence Unit for cross-border transaction monitoring

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Moderate-to-high feasibility for transactional throughput but not as a substitute for at least one Tier-1 Canadian clearing account: offshore FinTech payment service providers generally operate in Canada either as foreign money services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC or, if they offer Canadian-facing payment services to residents, as Retail Payment Activity Providers (RPAPs) requiring registration with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (effective 8-Sep-2024); PayPal and Stripe, the target payment rails, are each compliant RPAP-registered or agented through Canadian clearing banks, so the ULC may retain them as merchant-acquiring rails provided that settlement is routed into a Tier-1 Canadian bank account to satisfy source-of-funds traceability; offshore EMIs that are not FINTRAC-registered and not RPAP-registered are not viable primary banking rails because Canadian banks will treat them as unregulated conduits and reject downstream transfers

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal general corporate income tax rate: 15%. Alberta general corporate income tax rate: 8%. Combined general rate: 23%. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access a combined small-business rate of approximately 11% on eligible active business income up to the applicable CAD 500,000 business limit, subject to association, passive-income, and other statutory restrictions.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The Canadian Part XIII withholding rate on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is generally 25%, subject to treaty reduction. Under the Canada-United States treaty, the rate is generally 5% where the beneficial owner is a company owning at least 10% of the voting power of the Canadian payer, and 15% in other ordinary cases. Treaty entitlement depends on residence, beneficial ownership, limitation-on-benefits rules where applicable, and anti-avoidance principles.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general transaction-value threshold that eliminates the statutory contemporaneous-documentation requirement for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions under Income Tax Act section 247. A T106 information return is generally relevant where reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed the applicable aggregate CAD 1,000,000 threshold or other statutory conditions are met. Canada does not impose a universal OECD-style Master File filing threshold for all corporations; additional country-by-country reporting obligations may apply to qualifying multinational enterprise groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is relevant through Income Tax Act section 245 general anti-avoidance rules, the sham doctrine, treaty anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership requirements, transfer-pricing rules, recharacterization principles, and the requirement that expenses be incurred to earn income. The ULC should maintain Canadian commercial substance proportionate to its functions, including decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational control, inventory and fulfillment records, customer-contract evidence, banking activity, accounting records, and genuine risk assumption.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act section 239, conviction may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to two years, or both. Civil reassessment, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties, and denial of deductions may apply independently.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal criminal exposure where they personally participate, authorize, or direct the conduct. There is no automatic corporate-veil piercing merely because the corporation is insolvent. Courts may impose restitution or asset-related orders where authorized; a general automatic travel ban does not arise from a tax assessment, although release conditions, warrants, or court orders may restrict travel.
Unremitted Payroll, GST/HST, and Non-Resident Withholding

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the principal, prescribed interest, penalties, and enforcement measures. Director liability under Income Tax Act section 227.1 may apply to unremitted source deductions, subject to statutory due-diligence and limitation rules. GST/HST director liability may arise under Excise Tax Act section 323. Part XIII failures may attract penalties and payer liability for the unpaid withholding.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally for specified trust amounts if statutory conditions are met. Resignation alone does not eliminate liability for periods of directorship. Civil collection measures may include liens, garnishment, seizure, and litigation; imprisonment generally requires a separate criminal conviction.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust income under section 247, deny or recharacterize deductions, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the adjustment in qualifying circumstances, charge interest, and require amended filings. T106 failures may generate separate information-return penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable for a corporate transfer-pricing adjustment solely because they approved a commercial transaction. Personal exposure may arise from knowing false records, tax-evasion conduct, fraudulent conveyances, or trust-amount liabilities.
Shell Company, Sham, and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty relief, withholding at 25%, reassessment, interest, GAAR application, penalties, and possible criminal prosecution where intentional deception is established.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may incur personal criminal or civil exposure for deliberate participation in sham arrangements. No automatic veil piercing or travel ban applies solely from a GAAR reassessment.
Corporate Registry and Beneficial-Ownership Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Corporate dissolution, administrative penalties, prosecution, inability to obtain certificates of status, and regulatory or financial-institution escalation may result under applicable Alberta corporate legislation and federal anti-money-laundering requirements.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally prosecuted for statutory offences or misleading filings. Ordinary corporate registry non-compliance does not automatically create personal liability for corporate tax debts.
AML, Sanctions, and Payment-Processor Abuse

Statutory sanction: Account termination, freezing or seizure of funds, suspicious-transaction escalation, administrative monetary penalties, regulatory investigation, and criminal sanctions may apply under applicable Canadian AML, sanctions, and criminal laws. A normal pet-supplies retailer is not automatically a FINTRAC reporting entity solely because it accepts card or PayPal payments.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct or knowingly facilitate laundering, sanctions evasion, fraud, or proceeds-of-crime conduct may face criminal prosecution, fines, imprisonment, forfeiture, and court-ordered restrictions. Travel restrictions are fact-specific and not automatic.
GST/HST Place-of-Supply and Marketplace Misclassification

Statutory sanction: Assessment of unremitted GST/HST, denial of input tax credits, interest, late-filing and gross-negligence penalties, audit adjustments, and potential director liability for amounts held in trust.

Director personal liability: Personal liability may arise under the Excise Tax Act for qualifying unremitted trust amounts. Directors may also face criminal liability for intentional fraud or false records.
Corporate Insolvency and Fraudulent Preference

Statutory sanction: Transactions may be challenged, unwound, or subordinated under federal insolvency law, provincial fraudulent-conveyance law, or corporate law. Directors may face civil damages, contribution orders, and disqualification consequences where statutory duties are breached.

Director personal liability: Personal liability may arise for breaches of fiduciary duty, oppression remedies, fraudulent conveyances, illegal distributions, or statutory trust obligations. Imprisonment requires separate criminal conduct and conviction; a travel ban is not automatic.
