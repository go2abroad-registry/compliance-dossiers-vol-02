Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both the current account and the capital account. Canada is an IMF Article VIII member and imposes no exchange-control restrictions on payments or transfers for current international transactions or on capital flows. There are no statutory approvals for profit remittances (dividends, interest, royalties, management fees) or for capital repatriation by a non-resident shareholder. The practical hurdles are tax-driven, not exchange-control-driven: (i) Part XIII withholding tax of 25% on deemed dividends, interest, and royalties paid by the ULC to non-residents, reduced to 15% (or 5% for direct-dividend shareholders) and 0% on arm's-length interest under the Canada-US Treaty, subject to limitation-on-benefits and special ULC hybrid-mismatch rules under Treaty Article IV(7); (ii) E-commerce GST/HST registration and collection obligations on Canadian sales; and (iii) reporting obligations to Statistics Canada through the International Transactions Reporting System and to FINTRAC for wire transfers exceeding CAD 10,000. An Alberta ULC with a US shareholder that has made a check-the-box election may present treaty-mismatch complexity that Canadian tax counsel must document before the first dividend distribution.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender obligation and no mandatory conversion ratio; Canadian exporters may hold foreign currency indefinitely in domestic or offshore foreign-currency accounts. The binding statutory obligations are: (i) CBSA export reporting through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) for every export shipment with a value exceeding CAD 2,000, filed at the time of export for sea, air, and road modes, and within 15 calendar days after the date of exportation for goods shipped by mail or courier; (ii) FINTRAC Cross-Border Currency and Monetary Instruments Report (CCMIR) for physical import or export of currency or monetary instruments equal to or exceeding CAD 10,000; and (iii) for income-tax purposes, export receipts must be translated into CAD at a reasonable exchange rate (Bank of Canada daily rate or noon rate) on the relevant taxation date, and accrued foreign-exchange gains are taxable on income account for an e-commerce retailer. No central-bank approval is required to receive, hold, or disburse foreign-currency trade proceeds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); exchange-rate policy authority formally rests with the Minister of Finance / Department of Finance Canada; prudential regulation of deposit-taking institutions is administered by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); cross-border AML/CTF transaction reporting is administered by FINTRAC. Canada has no dedicated Foreign Exchange Administration because the Foreign Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium-Low; legally constrained. Canada does not recognize the EU/UK electronic money institution (EMI) licence, and the Bank Act restricts deposit-taking and payment-system clearing to licensed banks (Schedule I/II) and regulated credit unions. An offshore fintech EMI cannot hold CAD deposits for an Alberta ULC or settle through Payments Canada rails (Lynx, Real-Time Rail). The ULC may use an offshore EMI only as a supplementary card-acquiring and payouts gateway, provided the EMI obtains FINTRAC foreign money-services-business (FMSB) registration where applicable and its services do not constitute deposit-taking in Canada. Core treasury clearing, FX execution, and corporate credit must remain hosted by a Schedule I Canadian bank; Stripe and PayPal are acceptable only as front-end settlement rails that finally settle into the Schedule I operating account.
