Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

      
Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

    

    

      
Tax ID Application Infrastructure

      

        
Competent tax authority
Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; central portal: CRA My Business Account and Business Registration Online (BRO) via canada.ca. Corporate income tax and GST/HST accounts are administered by CRA; Alberta corporate income tax is also administered federally by CRA.

        
E-government filing portal
1) Form the Alberta ULC through Alberta Corporate Registry (no minimum paid-up capital required); obtain the Alberta incorporation certificate and corporate registry number. 2) Apply to CRA for a Business Number (BN) using Business Registration Online or, for non-resident applicants, the applicable CRA non-resident registration path; complete Form RC1 if required. 3) Open or register the GST/HST account (RT) once registration becomes mandatory, or voluntarily if zero-rated export refunds are sought. 4) Submit the entity's constitutive documents, foreign corporate or shareholder tax identification numbers, Alberta registered office and director information, and a Canadian contact or representative where applicable. 5) Activate access through CRA My Business Account or Represent a Client for the appointed tax representative.

      

      
Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible (for U.S. federal check-the-box purposes when the ULC is wholly owned by a U.S. person and Form 8832 is filed); however, for Canadian federal and Alberta tax purposes the ULC is an Entity_Level_Taxation corporation taxed under Part I of the Income Tax Act. A Canadian-resident ULC is taxed on worldwide income; a non-resident-owned ULC remains taxable on Canadian-source income and may be subject to Part XIV branch tax. It is not in a Tax_Exempt_Zone. — 1) Form the Alberta ULC through Alberta Corporate Registry (no minimum paid-up capital required); obtain the Alberta incorporation certificate and corporate registry number. 2) Apply to CRA for a Business Number (BN) using Business Registration Online or, for non-resident applicants, the applicable CRA non-resident registration path; complete Form RC1 if required. 3) Open or register the GST/HST account (RT) once registration becomes mandatory, or voluntarily if zero-rated export refunds are sought. 4) Submit the entity's constitutive documents, foreign corporate or shareholder tax identification numbers, Alberta registered office and director information, and a Canadian contact or representative where applicable. 5) Activate access through CRA My Business Account or Represent a Client for the appointed tax representative.

    

    

      
Default Tax Classification & Elections

      

        
Default tax classification
Pass_Through_Eligible (for U.S. federal check-the-box purposes when the ULC is wholly owned by a U.S. person and Form 8832 is filed); however, for Canadian federal and Alberta tax purposes the ULC is an Entity_Level_Taxation corporation taxed under Part I of the Income Tax Act. A Canadian-resident ULC is taxed on worldwide income; a non-resident-owned ULC remains taxable on Canadian-source income and may be subject to Part XIV branch tax. It is not in a Tax_Exempt_Zone.

        
Available election forms
Canada has no equivalent to the U.S. check-the-box regime for corporations. Relevant elections include: (a) U.S. Form 8832 entity classification election to treat the ULC as a disregarded entity or partnership; (b) the section 216 election for a non-resident corporation to be taxed on net Canadian investment income instead of Part XIII withholding tax; (c) Part XIV branch tax return under section 219 for foreign-owned Canadian corporations; and (d) GST/HST registration elections under ETA sections 240 and 241, including voluntary registration. Canadian tax law does not permit the ULC itself to be fiscally transparent, and hybrid mismatch provisions may apply to cross-border structures.

      

    

    

      
VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

      

        
Standard VAT / GST rate
5%

        
Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)
CAD 30,000. A person is a small supplier until total taxable consideration from supplies made in Canada, or introduced into Canada, exceeds C$30,000 in a single calendar quarter or in the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Once exceeded, GST/HST registration and collection are mandatory. Non-resident e-commerce suppliers of goods such as baby and maternity supplies must register when the same threshold is met and they make taxable supplies to consumers in Canada.

      

      
      

        Export 0% zero-rating conditions
        
Under the Excise Tax Act and Schedule VI, a supply of tangible goods is zero-rated when the supplier exports the goods, or ships them to a destination outside Canada, as part of the supply and retains documentary proof of export. Services are generally zero-rated when supplied to a non-resident and performed wholly or mainly outside Canada. The sale of baby and maternity goods by the Canadian ULC to foreign customers qualifies for the 0% GST/HST rate when title passes outside Canada and the goods are delivered to a foreign address.

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, e-invoicing API reporting, or digital stamping. CRA accepts electronic invoices provided they contain the statutory GST/HST invoice information: supplier name or legal trade name, GST/HST registration number, invoice date, description of the supply, taxable amount, GST/HST rate, and tax amount. Records must be retained in accessible electronic or paper form for at least six years and produced to CRA on audit.

      
Export zero-rating is a high-risk CRA audit area. If the exporter cannot substantiate physical export and receipt of the proceeds, CRA will reassess the supply at 5% GST/HST, deny the zero-rating and any related input tax credits, and charge interest. False or negligent statements can trigger gross-negligence penalties of up to 50% of the tax at issue; deliberate evasion may lead to prosecution, fines and imprisonment, and re-assessment beyond the normal four-year limitation period. CRA may also claw back refunds through assessment offsets and impose penalties under the Excise Tax Act for failure to maintain adequate books and records.

Stage 04
Banking & Treasury Architecture

      
Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

    

    

      
Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

      

        
Current-account convertibility
Canada is an IMF Article VIII member with a freely floating Canadian dollar and fully unrestricted current-account and capital-account convertibility. The former Exchange Control Act has been suspended since 1952 and no approval is required from the Bank of Canada for capital injections, dividend remittances, interest payments, loan repayments, or profit repatriation by a non-resident shareholder of an Alberta ULC. The only operational hurdles are: (1) Part XIII Income Tax Act withholding at up to 25% (treaty-reduced to 15% or 5% under the Canada-US Income Tax Convention for qualifying US shareholders) on dividends, interest, and royalties remitted by the ULC, requiring filing of NR4/NR6 returns and remittance of the withheld amount to the Canada Revenue Agency; (2) FINTRAC/PCMLTFA reporting by financial intermediaries for electronic funds transfers and large cash transactions of CAD 10,000 or more; (3) Canada Border Services Agency form E677 reporting for physical cross-border movement of currency or monetary instruments of CAD 10,000 or more. No distinction exists between capital and current account convertibility for the ULC, and profit remittance may be executed by ordinary SWIFT wire without regulatory vetting. The ULC may freely hold interest-bearing USD and CAD accounts in Canada, and the corporate foreign-currency account regime imposes no deposit ratios or conversion mandates.

        
Export proceeds surrender rules
None. Canadian law imposes no statutory timeline, mandatory conversion ratio, or surrender obligation for export proceeds of e-commerce, goods, or services. Exporters may retain foreign currency indefinitely in domestic or offshore accounts. Administrative reporting is statistical only: merchandise exports must be reported on a CBSA B13A Export Declaration; the underlying data is compiled by Statistics Canada and the Bank of Canada for balance-of-payments purposes. Cross-border transfers of CAD 10,000 or more are subject to FINTRAC EFT report filing by the financial institution, and physical movement of monetary instruments of CAD 10,000 or more must be declared to the CBSA on form E677. No credit to a controlled dollar account or conversion window is applicable.

      

      
Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) under the Bank of Canada Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. B-2), in coordination with the Department of Finance Canada and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI). Canada maintains no dedicated foreign exchange administration; cross-border capital and currency movements are governed by the Currency Act, the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), the Customs Act, and the Canada Border Services Agency regulations.

    

    

      
Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

      

        
        

          

            Stripe
            Fit: High
          

          
Medium, commercially conditional and legally constrained. Canada is not an EEA member; EU/UK electronic money institutions (Wise, Revolut Business, Airwallex, Currencycloud) cannot passport a European EMI license into Canada. To service a Canadian-resident ULC, any non-bank fintech must obtain registration as a money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA and maintain CAD settlement accounts at a Schedule I chartered bank. Several international platforms operate in Canada via MSB registration or through Canadian chartered-bank partnership (Bank-as-a-Service). Legal constraints: (1) the EMI cannot take deposits or provide deposit insurance absent a Bank Act charter; (2) MSB float restrictions and FINTRAC reporting obligations, including a 5-day hold on suspicious large-value transactions and mandatory large-value transaction reports at CAD 10,000; (3) the ULC's corporate treasury could expose non-resident shareholder assets to counterparty insolvency, negating the unlimited-liability trust/escrow purity relied upon in the ULC tax plan; (4) for ISIC 4791 e-commerce receivables, PayPal and Stripe are best used as collection-only rails, with daily sweeps of accumulated proceeds to a regulated Tier-1 bank account. Use of an offshore digital EMI as the primary treasury ledger is Low feasibility and is discouraged for a CA_AB ULC holding escrow capital.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

      

        Route the non-resident shareholder's capital injection for the Alberta ULC through SWIFT MT103 (customer credit transfer) or, for high-value CAD transfers interbank, SWIFT MT202/MT202 COV via the Canadian correspondent. Remittance field 70 (Remittance Information) must specify the legal purpose, e.g., 'Share subscription payment under agreement dated [date]' or 'Shareholder loan disbursement under loan agreement dated [date]'. Canada does not impose statutory bank-deposit purpose codes of the type used in controlled FX jurisdictions; the description is descriptive but must match the underlying instrument in the bank's documentary due-diligence file. The ULC must obtain a GLEIF Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) prior to account opening and derivative onboarding; the LEI is mandatory for all Canadian bank counterparty records and OTC FX/derivatives. For equity injections, deliver the executed share-subscription agreement, corporate director resolutions, and source-of-funds statements to the receiving bank. For shareholder loans, provide the signed loan agreement, interest-rate terms, and evidence of the Canada Revenue Agency withholding arrangement (NR6/NR4 regime) for Part XIII interest withholding, where applicable. Inbound wires of CAD 10,000 or greater are reported by the receiving Schedule I bank to FINTRAC as an electronic funds transfer report; no central-bank approval is required. If the contribution is physically moved as currency, CBSA form E677 applies at the threshold of CAD 10,000 or more.The ULC should use Payments Canada ACSS for routine low-value CAD disbursements and LVTS (Large Value Transfer System) for CAD wires above CAD 300,000 or when real-time finality is required. USD trade settlements execute via the Canadian bank's US correspondent and clear through Fedwire or CHIPS. For supplier and inventory payments to overseas beneficiaries (maternity and baby product purchases from China, India, or Southeast Asia), use SWIFT MT103 with full beneficiary details, SWIFT/BIC, and IBAN or account number; MT104 for direct debit collections; MT700 for issuance of Commercial Letters of Credit, MT707 for amendments, and MT710 for credit confirmation by a third bank. A sight documentary letter of credit with a 10% quantity tolerance, clean-on-board bill of lading, commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, and where required a certificate of analysis is recommended for bulk cross-border inventory procurement. E-commerce payouts from Stripe and PayPal should be swept daily to the ULC's operational account via Payments Canada automated clearing (EFT) in CAD or via the platform's standard payout schedule in USD, followed by an intraday LVTS wire if conversion is needed at the corporate treasury desk. All outbound trade wires of CAD 10,000 or more are FINTRAC-reportable by the financial institution.Deliverable Outright Forward (USD/CAD, EUR/CAD, CNY/CAD) — fully permissible for residents and non-residents; requires an ISDA Master Agreement or Canadian Bankers' Acceptance master forward agreement and an approved credit line or collateral support annex.Non-Deliverable Forward (NDF) — legally permissible for CAD crosses because Canada has no exchange control regime; offered by the major Schedule I banks primarily for exotic currency pairs such as USD/CNH-CAD or for clients without bilateral credit facilities, with settlement in CAD equivalent.Cross-Currency Swap — permissible under the Bank Act and OSFI regulatory capital rules; available for long-dated conversion of USD-denominated e-commerce proceeds into CAD and for matching the ULC's funding currency with its operating currency; governed by an ISDA Master Agreement together with a Credit Support Annex providing daily margin for an unrated corporate.
      

      
E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium, commercially conditional and legally constrained. Canada is not an EEA member; EU/UK electronic money institutions (Wise, Revolut Business, Airwallex, Currencycloud) cannot passport a European EMI license into Canada. To service a Canadian-resident ULC, any non-bank fintech must obtain registration as a money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA and maintain CAD settlement accounts at a Schedule I chartered bank. Several international platforms operate in Canada via MSB registration or through Canadian chartered-bank partnership (Bank-as-a-Service). Legal constraints: (1) the EMI cannot take deposits or provide deposit insurance absent a Bank Act charter; (2) MSB float restrictions and FINTRAC reporting obligations, including a 5-day hold on suspicious large-value transactions and mandatory large-value transaction reports at CAD 10,000; (3) the ULC's corporate treasury could expose non-resident shareholder assets to counterparty insolvency, negating the unlimited-liability trust/escrow purity relied upon in the ULC tax plan; (4) for ISIC 4791 e-commerce receivables, PayPal and Stripe are best used as collection-only rails, with daily sweeps of accumulated proceeds to a regulated Tier-1 bank account. Use of an offshore digital EMI as the primary treasury ledger is Low feasibility and is discouraged for a CA_AB ULC holding escrow capital.
