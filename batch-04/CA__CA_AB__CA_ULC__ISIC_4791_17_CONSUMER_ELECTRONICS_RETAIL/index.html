Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA); online portals: CRA 'My Business Account' and 'Business Registration Online' (BRO) via https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html. Alberta corporate income tax is administered by the CRA under the federal-provincial collection agreement with Alberta Finance; no separate provincial corporate tax filing is required.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate an Alberta Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) under the Business Corporations Act (Alberta) at the Alberta Corporate Registry, producing articles of incorporation and a Corporate Access Number; alternatively, register an existing British Columbia ULC as an extra-provincial corporation in Alberta. Step 2: Apply online through CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) or file Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts' to obtain the federal Business Number (BN); the BN is the statutory TIN used for income tax and all CRA program accounts. Step 3: Activate the 'RC' corporate income tax program account (mandatory for all resident corporations), the 'RT' GST/HST program account (mandatory once taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in a calendar quarter or four consecutive quarters, or on a voluntary basis), and the 'RM' import/export program account if the ULC will ship goods into or out of Canada. Step 4: For a non-resident-owned ULC, provide the registered office address, Alberta law agent details, the foreign owner's home-jurisdiction TIN, and a statement of any delegation of central management and control; Alberta's ABCA permits non-resident directors, and no minimum capital is prescribed. Step 5: CRA issues the BN instantaneously through BRO and sends a confirmation letter listing the program accounts; the ULC must then file a combined federal/Alberta T2 Corporation Income Tax Return within six months after each taxation year-end and file GST/HST returns according to its reporting period (usually annual for small suppliers, monthly or quarterly for larger registrants).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Under Canadian domestic law, the Alberta ULC is a corporation taxable at the entity level under Part I of the Income Tax Act and no domestic check-the-box election can make it fiscally transparent for Canadian tax. The pass-through attribute applies at the foreign shareholder level: U.S. shareholders may file IRS Form 8832 to classify the ULC as a disregarded entity or partnership, and Article IV(7) of the Canada-U.S. Income Tax Convention (2007 Protocol) treats a fiscally transparent hybrid entity as flowing its income to the resident owner for treaty purposes, thereby limiting Canadian withholding and enabling foreign tax credit claims. For GST/HST, the ULC is a single 'person' under the Excise Tax Act; however, the closely related corporations election under ETA s. 156 can nil-value intercompany supplies between the ULC and affiliated CCPCs, and the election under ETA s. 211.1? no longer applies; registrants should also consider voluntary filing elections under ETA s. 237.1? none apply. Alberta does not separate corporate tax elections: federal and Alberta taxable income are computed in the single T2 return, and Alberta provides a 8% corporate income tax rate on general income and 12% on investment income, but no provincial election is available under Alberta's Tax Statutes. Additionally, for hybrid-mismatch exposures, sections 18.4 to 18.6 of the Income Tax Act may deny a deduction for an entity-level payment if the corresponding receipt is not taxable in the hands of a transparent foreign shareholder; foreign check-the-box elections should be disclosed and monitored.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of world-wide taxable supplies in a single calendar quarter or in four consecutive calendar quarters (CTA s. 240); mandatory registration within 29 days after the calendar month in which the threshold is exceeded (ETA s. 241). For the online retail of consumer electronics, the supply of physical goods to Alberta consumers is taxable at 5% at the point of sale; voluntary registration below the threshold is permitted to recover input tax credits. For non-resident suppliers of digital services, software, and similar intangible property, Canada's 2021 simplified GST registration regime applies when annual supplies to Canadian recipients exceed CAD 30,000; for this Alberta-resident ULC, the ordinary threshold applies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of goods exported from Canada may be zero-rated under Part V (Exports) of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act. For an online retailer of personal electronics and smart gadgets, the sale qualifies at 0% GST/HST where: (i) the supplier exports the goods from Canada in a continuous manner, (ii) the goods are not acquired for consumption, use, or supply in Canada before export, and (iii) the supplier ships the goods to a non-resident purchaser by common carrier or air waybill to a foreign destination, or delivers them to a customs warehouse or satisfying the documentary requirements of ETA Schedule VI Part V and CRA GST/HST Memorandum 17-10. Services and intangible personal property supplied to non-residents (e.g., software updates, cloud support not connected with Canadian real property) are also zero-rated when the recipient is a non-resident. The exporter must obtain proof of export within two years of the supply, including the export declaration, the carrier waybill, the commercial invoice, and payment or other evidence that the customer is a non-resident. Supplies delivered to Canadian addresses, shipped to a Canadian freight-forwarder for later sale in Canada, or picked up by the customer in Canada are domestic supplies subject to 5% GST.

  • Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) Export Declaration (Form B13A or electronic export reporting in the Canadian Export Reporting System, CESAR) for controlled or regulated shipments; for low-value consumer e-commerce parcels, the carrier's consolidated electronic export data is normally accepted.
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill issued by the common carrier evidencing shipment from Canada to the foreign destination and identifying the foreign consignee.
  • Commercial invoice describing the goods, HS codes (e.g., 8471, 8517 for electronics), quantity, value in CAD, currency, incoterms, seller, and buyer details.
  • Proof of foreign settlement and inward remittance: Stripe or PayPal settlement reports, bank credit advices, credit card capture records, or an inward remittance certificate from the Canadian account bank evidencing payment by/on behalf of the non-resident customer.
  • Signed recipient declaration, foreign address verification, or customer registration records used by the e-commerce platform to confirm the purchaser's non-resident status where CRA requests alternative evidence.

Canada has no mandatory real-time electronic fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls, e-invoicing API, or digital stamping regime. CRA accepts paper or electronic invoices and does not require clearance before issuance; however, every registrant must retain books and records for six years and make invoices, payment processor settlement reports, shipping manifests, and customs declarations available on audit. Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing based on the Peppol PINT framework is voluntary and does not govern input tax credit eligibility. GST/HST returns are transmitted through CRA NETFILE/GST/HST forms; for large e-commerce merchants, no additional pre-clearance filing is imposed.

CRA's international audit and GST/HST audit teams scrutinize export zero-rating claims for electronic goods by testing waybills, courier manifests, customs export data, customer IP/address records, and settlement provenance. If the export conditions are not substantiated, CRA may: (1) reassess the supply as taxable at 5%, deny input tax credits attributable to the export, and charge compound daily interest on the net tax; (2) impose a false-statement penalty under ETA s. 285(1) of the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the understated net tax, and, where gross negligence is found, a misconduct penalty of 50% of the understated tax under ETA s. 285.1; (3) apply penalties for failing to register or file under ETA ss. 280 and 283; (4) reopen the period beyond the standard four-year reassessment window where misrepresentation, fraud, or neglect exists (ETA s. 296; ITA s. 152(4)(a)(i)); and (5) in egregious cases, refer the matter for criminal prosecution under the Excise Tax Act or the Criminal Code. Because the entity is an Alberta ULC with unlimited shareholder liability, shareholders may be jointly and severally exposed to unremitted GST/HST and associated penalties under applicable Alberta corporate law, and directors may be liable under ETA s. 323 for unremitted amounts. CBSA may additionally impose administrative monetary penalties for inaccurate export declarations. For income tax, the CRA administrative benchmark for ITA s. 17(1) treats an unrepatriated non-resident sales receivable outstanding beyond 180 days as an imputed-interest loan unless commercial collection practices justify a longer term, so timely FX repatriation and documentary support are essential to defend both the GST zero-rating and the income tax position.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible with no statutory restrictions on current account or capital account transactions. Profit remittances, dividend payments, and capital repatriation to non-resident shareholders may be executed without exchange control approval, subject to corporate law solvency tests (e.g., Alberta Business Corporations Act) and to AML reporting thresholds. No requirement for administrative licensing of ordinary trade payments or investment flows.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender of export proceeds and no obligatory conversion to CAD. Export receipts may be retained in foreign currency, repatriated under open account timing negotiated by the exporter, and credited to a corporate foreign-currency account. However, all receipts exceeding CAD 10,000 in a single transaction or series must be reported to FINTRAC, and transactions with sanctioned jurisdictions or entities are prohibited under the Special Economic Measures Act and the United Nations Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC), in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for bank supervision and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for cross-border currency transaction reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium feasibility, subject to structural constraints. Canadian federal banking law prohibits unlicensed foreign 'electronic money institutions' (EMIs) from taking deposits or issuing stored-value instruments unless registered as a Canadian payment service provider under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) or operating through a chartered bank partnership. Offshore EMIs (e.g., UK FCA-licensed or EU EMI-licensed) are not directly recognized as banks in Canada; they can provide payment facilitation and cross-border payout services but cannot legally maintain settlement accounts in an entity's name that replicate Canadian deposit insurance or full banking privileges. For a consumer-electronics e-commerce ULC, an offshore EMI is viable for disbursement rails (e.g., supplier payments, affiliate payouts) but not for primary treasury custody.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity or shareholder loans to the Alberta ULC must be remitted in CAD or USD via SWIFT MT103 or MT202 for larger interbank transfers. Each wire must include a clear payment reference (e.g., 'Shareholder loan - [Agreement Date]' or 'Equity injection - [Resolution Date]') to align with the CRA reporting and the entity's books. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium feasibility, subject to structural constraints. Canadian federal banking law prohibits unlicensed foreign 'electronic money institutions' (EMIs) from taking deposits or issuing stored-value instruments unless registered as a Canadian payment service provider under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) or operating through a chartered bank partnership. Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Statutory rate under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (section 212(2)): 25% of the amount of a dividend paid or credited by a corporation resident in Canada to a non-resident person. For a ULC resident in Canada, any dividend paid to a non-resident shareholder is subject to 25% withholding unless a lower rate is available under an income tax treaty. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory transfer-pricing documentation (Local File/Master File) is required under section 247(4) of the Income Tax Act for every taxpayer resident in Canada that transacts with a non-resident non-arm's-length person and where the total value of such transactions in the taxation year exceeds CAD 1 million. Master File documentation is required for groups with consolidated revenue of CAD 100 million or more. Country-by-country (CbC) reporting is required for Canadian ultimate parent entities of multinational groups with consolidated revenue of EUR 750 million or more (or approximately CAD 1 billion). The ULC must file Form T106 (Information Return of Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents) for each non-arm's-length transaction exceeding CAD 1 million, and must prepare a Local File in accordance with the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines and the CRA's Information Circular IC87-2R. Failure to maintain contemporaneous documentation can lead to a penalty of CAD 1,000 per month for each month, up to CAD 24,000, and a penalty of 10% of the amount of the adjustment where the transfer price is subsequently adjusted by the CRA.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not impose a specific 'Economic Substance Regulations' (ESR) regime akin to certain offshore financial centres. Instead, economic substance is evaluated through the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section 245 of the Income Tax Act, which denies a tax benefit resulting from an avoidance transaction lacking bona fide business purpose. The CRA also applies the concept of a 'sham' at common law where transactions are presented as different from their actual legal effect. A ULC with limited or no Canadian substance is exposed to challenge if it is interposed primarily to obtain treaty benefits or to defer Canadian tax. The CRA will scrutinize the entity's residence (central management and control), the place of board meetings, the location of assets, and the degree of decision-making in Canada. For transfer-pricing purposes, the arm's length principle requires that the ULC's intercompany transactions be consistent with functions performed, assets used, and risks assumed; mere invoicing without operational substance is fatal. The OECD/G20 BEPS action items, including the Multilateral Instrument (MLI), may deny treaty benefits to entities that lack sufficient connection to the treaty jurisdiction; for a ULC, this can arise if the ULC is used as a conduit for U.S. or other foreign investors without local employees, premises, or active management.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Income Tax Act, s. 239)

Statutory sanction: On summary conviction: a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to 2 years. On indictment: a fine of 100% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to 5 years. The CRA may also assess a civil penalty of 50% of the understated tax for gross negligence under subsection 163(2), plus interest at the prescribed rate.

Director personal liability: CRA may assess directors for the corporation's unpaid withholding tax and other amounts under section 227.1 if the corporation fails to remit and due diligence is not exercised. Personal liability includes the amount of tax withheld and not remitted, interest, and penalties. Directors can be subject to criminal prosecution for abetting evasion, leading to imprisonment and fines. The CRA may also apply a 'director's liability' assessment without a prior court order, and directors may face travel bans where a court orders seizure of passports for non-cooperation with a tax investigation.
Shell Company / Artificial Transaction (GAAR, s. 245)

Statutory sanction: Under section 245(2), the CRA may disregard the transaction and reassess tax as if it had not occurred, denying treaty benefits and imposing interest. An additional penalty of 10% of the tax benefit may apply if the transaction was not disclosed on Form RC4517 (for aggressive tax avoidance transactions) or where the taxpayer fails to participate in the avoidance transaction disclosure regime. The CRA may also levy a 50% gross-negligence penalty if the arrangement is found to be a sham and the taxpayer had intent to deceive.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or cause the ULC to enter a sham transaction may be personally liable for the resulting tax deficiencies under the negligent-representation doctrine. Where the transaction involves a fraudulent conveyance, directors may be liable to creditors and to the CRA. The CRA may seek a compliance order to compel the corporation and directors to produce records, and may initiate criminal proceedings against directors for counselling the commission of an offence under subsection 239(1).
AML / Anti-Money Laundering (PCMLTFA)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 500,000 for individuals and CAD 1,000,000 for entities per violation. Criminal liability for knowingly making false statements or obstructing a FINTRAC examination includes fines up to CAD 2,000,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorized, acquiesced in, or participated in a violation of the PCMLTFA may be personally liable for penalties if they exercised control, and may face criminal charges where they were willfully blind. FINTRAC may impose a compliance order, and the CRA may share information with FINTRAC leading to a joint tax and AML investigation. Travel bans may be imposed where a director is subject to a criminal investigation and flight risk.
Dividend Stripping / Treaty Shopping (ss. 212.1, 245; MLI Article 7)

Statutory sanction: The CRA can recharacterize a transaction as a dividend under section 212.1 and apply the 25% Part XIII withholding tax, deny treaty benefits under the principal purpose test of the MLI, and impose penalties under the GAAR. The non-resident is also subject to tax on the deemed dividend without a step-up in the cost base of the shares, and interest can compound.

Director personal liability: Directors who advise on or implement a dividend-stripping plan may be subject to the same penalties as the corporation, including a 50% gross-negligence penalty if they knowingly participate. If the treaty-shopping arrangement is found to be a sham, directors may be held personally liable for the unpaid tax as a result of the corporation's insolvency and may be precluded from being directors of a Canadian corporation for a period of up to 5 years under corporate law sanctions.
Late or Non-Filing of GST/HST and Payroll Remittances

Statutory sanction: GST/HST penalties: 1% of the amount of net tax owing, plus 25% of the amount, for late remittances; interest at the prescribed rate. Failure to file a GST/HST return attracts a penalty of CAD 100 to CAD 250 per return. Payroll penalties include 10% of the amount of unremitted source deductions, plus interest; a failure to withhold Part XIII tax carries a penalty of 10% of the amount if the payer was grossly negligent.

Director personal liability: Directors are liable for the corporation's failure to remit GST/HST and payroll source deductions under section 323 of the Excise Tax Act and section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act. The CRA may issue a director's liability assessment and may enforce it through garnishment and bank seizures; directors cannot claim due diligence if they failed to exercise reasonable care in overseeing remittances, and may be personally sued by the CRA for the full unpaid amounts.
