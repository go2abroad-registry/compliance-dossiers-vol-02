Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (domestic rule: the Alberta ULC is a corporation resident in Canada under Income Tax Act ss. 2 and 250(4), taxed on worldwide income with foreign tax credits); Pass_Through_Eligible (cross-border only: fiscally transparent treatment available solely under the Canada-US Tax Treaty Article IV(6) and US check-the-box, never for domestic Canadian tax); Territorial_Scope (not applicable - worldwide taxation); Tax_Exempt_Zone (not applicable)

Available election forms

i) US Check-the-Box Election (Form 8832) to treat the ULC as a disregarded entity or partnership for US federal tax purposes with treaty look-through relief; ii) Subsection 216(1) ITA election enabling a non-resident shareholder to file net-basis Canadian tax returns for Canadian-source rental/income streams instead of Part XIII gross-basis withholding; iii) Excise Tax Act s. 156 election (related corporations) to ignore GST/HST on inter-corporate supplies; iv) ETA s. 150 election to treat certain corporate reorganizations as non-supplies; v) Hybrid-mismatch deduction coordination under new ITA ss. 256.1-256.8 (BEPS Action 2) requiring documented elections and ordering rules; vi) Treaty-based reduced-withholding relief filings (NR301/NR302) for dividends, interest, and royalties paid to resident shareholders.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) in any single calendar quarter or in the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (small supplier threshold, ETA s. 148(1); a non-resident carrying on business in Canada must register once the threshold is exceeded, with mandatory registration for non-residents making taxable supplies in Canada unless the small-supplier exemption applies)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Excise Tax Act Schedule VI Part V (ss. 7-9) and Part VII (ss. 20-26), the supply is zero-rated if (a) it is a supply of a transportation service by a common carrier by water of goods from a place in Canada to a place outside Canada; (b) the international Ro-Ro carriage of export vehicles is supplied to a non-resident; or (c) the service is performed for a non-resident in respect of exported goods. The domestic (cabotage) leg within Canada, stevedoring at Canadian ports, and services supplied to Canadian-resident principals remain taxable at 5% GST. Zero-rating also applies to fuel, provisions, and vessel supplies exported for use on the international voyage when properly documented.

  • Customs Export Declaration (G13) filed electronically via the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS)
  • Bill of Lading (through sea waybill or negotiable bill evidencing Ro-Ro carriage from Canadian port to foreign destination)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank SWIFT MT103 evidencing export proceeds repatriation
  • Commercial Invoice issued to the foreign consignee
  • Marine Cargo Report / Cargo Control Document filed under eManifest (ACI)
  • Packing List and Contract of Affreightment / fixture note describing the zero-rated international carriage

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, API stamping, or digital stamping regime is currently in force at the federal level. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically by registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 1.5M; all commercial export declarations must be transmitted electronically via CERS (CBSA), and ocean carriers must file eManifest / Marine Cargo Reports. Electronic records must be retained in readable CRA-prescribed format under ETA s. 286. The Pan-Canadian e-Invoicing framework (Peppol/BIS 3.0-based) is under phased voluntary adoption, with large suppliers increasingly required to receive electronic documents, but no statutory clearance or digital validation of invoices is imposed.

CRA audit systems cross-match CERS export declarations against GST/HST returns and Input Tax Credit claims (ETA s. 296(3.1)). Inadequate export documentation triggers reassessment of the full freight revenue at 5% GST, denial of corresponding ITCs, prescribed-rate interest, a 25% gross-negligence penalty under ETA s. 285.1, and false-statement penalties under ETA s. 285; criminal tax evasion under ETA s. 327 carries fines up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to 5 years. The normal reassessment window is 4 years (ETA s. 298), but it is unlimited where misrepresentation, negligence, wilful default, or fraud is established. Export vouchers, bills of lading, and inward remittance certificates must be retained for 6 years under ETA s. 286, and retrospective clawbacks can be asserted against refunds, rebates, and ITCs claimed during the entire open period.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully liberalized foreign exchange regime with unrestricted capital account convertibility and free current account transactions. Profit remittances to non-resident shareholders are permitted without prior approval, subject only to standard reporting obligations under the Income Tax Act, Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), and FINTRAC registration requirements. No capital controls or surrender mechanisms apply to most corporate transfers.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender or conversion of export proceeds is required under Canadian law. Exporters must file export declarations with the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) and maintain full compliance with PCMLTFA anti-money laundering obligations. Proceeds may be retained in foreign currency accounts or converted to CAD with appropriate documentation for tax and regulatory purposes.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined general corporate income tax rate for Alberta corporations: 15% federal and 8% Alberta provincial tax. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may access a combined small-business rate of approximately 11% on eligible active business income within the applicable business limit. A ULC is generally a corporation for Canadian income-tax purposes and is not automatically fiscally transparent in Canada.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to an applicable income-tax treaty or other statutory relief. Common treaty outcomes include 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting ownership thresholds and 15% for other qualifying portfolio dividends; the applicable treaty must be checked separately.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 and AT1 returns are generally due within six months after the end of the corporation's taxation year. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end; a qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally obtain a three-month balance-due period if statutory conditions are satisfied. Tax instalment deadlines remain applicable independently of the return deadline.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no universal transaction-value threshold that eliminates the contemporaneous-documentation obligation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. Form T106 reporting generally applies where the aggregate reportable transactions with a non-resident non-arm's-length party exceed CAD 1,000,000, subject to the detailed statutory rules. A Canadian corporation with gross revenues of CAD 10,000,000 or more may face an additional transfer-pricing penalty exposure where adequate contemporaneous documentation is not prepared by the filing deadline and the transfer-pricing adjustment exceeds the statutory threshold. Canada generally does not impose a separate mandatory OECD Master File filing requirement, although multinational groups should maintain group-level documentation where relevant to risk management and other-country obligations.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a standalone United Arab Emirates-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Substance is nevertheless relevant under the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse provisions, beneficial-ownership principles, transfer-pricing rules, foreign-affiliate rules, thin-capitalization rules, and residency and permanent-establishment analysis. A Canadian ULC should maintain real Canadian governance, commercially credible decision-making, accounting records, banking controls, personnel or appropriately documented service arrangements, vessel-operating evidence, contracts, insurance, and risk-control evidence. A ULC established solely to obtain treaty benefits, create deductible related-party charges, or exploit a foreign hybrid classification presents elevated reassessment risk.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and deliberate misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act offences can result in fines generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment of up to five years on indictment, or both. Civil reassessment, gross-negligence penalties, arrears interest, and prosecution costs may apply separately.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorized, participated in, or knowingly acquiesced in the conduct may face criminal prosecution and personal fines or imprisonment. The corporate veil is not ordinarily pierced merely because tax is unpaid, but personal liability may arise from criminal conduct, torts, fraudulent transfers, statutory director-liability provisions, or failure to remit source deductions and certain indirect taxes. Travel restrictions are not automatic but may arise from bail, court orders, or enforcement proceedings.
Unremitted payroll deductions and source withholding

Statutory sanction: The responsible corporation may face assessments for the unremitted amount, interest, late-remittance penalties, failure-to-file penalties, and prosecution. Statutory penalties can be significant and may apply even where the corporation lacks cash.

Director personal liability: Directors can be jointly and severally liable for certain unremitted source deductions and other trust amounts if statutory conditions are met, subject to the due-diligence defence. Personal assets may be pursued after corporate recovery steps or where the statutory regime permits direct assessment.
Transfer-pricing manipulation and thin capitalization

Statutory sanction: The Canada Revenue Agency may adjust income under section 247 of the Income Tax Act, deny deductions, recharacterize transactions, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment in qualifying circumstances, apply withholding tax, and charge interest. Thin-capitalization rules may deny interest deductions and deem denied interest to be a dividend subject to withholding.

Director personal liability: Ordinary transfer-pricing reassessment normally remains a corporate liability. Personal exposure can arise where directors knowingly participated in fraud, asset stripping, false records, or trust-withholding failures.
Treaty abuse, conduit financing, and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: Treaty relief may be denied under beneficial-ownership, principal-purpose, limitation-on-benefits, or general anti-avoidance principles. The payer may be assessed for under-withheld tax, interest, penalties, and information-return failures. Recharacterization as a dividend, management fee, or taxable royalty may increase exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly approve false residency certificates, sham transactions, or fraudulent withholding records may face civil claims, director assessments where statutory conditions apply, and criminal tax consequences.
Shell company, corporate residency, and economic-substance failure

Statutory sanction: The corporation may lose treaty access, be reassessed on residence, permanent-establishment, transfer-pricing, or anti-avoidance grounds, and face denial of deductions or credits. Corporate-law filings, beneficial-ownership records, and tax-return information may be audited or required by competent authorities.

Director personal liability: Directors may face breach-of-duty claims, regulatory sanctions, personal liability for fraudulent or negligent conduct, and enforcement consequences if the entity was used to conceal assets or evade tax. Mere lack of profitability or outsourcing does not itself establish personal liability.
Anti-money-laundering, sanctions, and proceeds-of-crime violations

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act can impose administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, and criminal penalties, including substantial fines and imprisonment. Criminal Code proceeds-of-crime and sanctions offences can trigger asset forfeiture, significant fines, and imprisonment. Canadian sanctions legislation may impose severe corporate and individual penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, beneficial owners, and compliance personnel may be prosecuted where they participated in, directed, consented to, or were wilfully blind to unlawful conduct. Assets may be frozen or forfeited, and court-imposed travel restrictions may apply in criminal proceedings; no automatic travel ban follows solely from a tax filing error.
Marine licensing, customs, GST/HST, and shipping-record non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Separate transport, customs, GST/HST, environmental, and marine-safety penalties may apply, including assessments, interest, administrative monetary penalties, seizure, detention, licence suspension, and prosecution. Tax deductions may be denied where expenses or revenue cannot be substantiated.

Director personal liability: Directors may face statutory liability for certain GST/HST and trust amounts, and personal prosecution for knowing regulatory offences or obstruction. Corporate insolvency does not automatically eliminate regulatory, environmental, or fiduciary liabilities.
