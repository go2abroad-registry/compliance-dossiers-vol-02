Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); Business Registration Online (BRO) portal and My Business Account portal

E-government filing portal

Foreign entities must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) using CRA's Business Registration Online or Form RC1. The ULC must provide its certificate of incorporation or registration from Alberta, director and authorized officer details, Canadian business address or agent/representative for service, and an estimate of annual Canadian taxable revenue. After the BN is issued, the entity must separately register for a GST/HST account (RT) through the same portal. Non-resident ULCs may be required to appoint a resident individual or Canadian entity as representative for excise tax purposes; CRA generally issues the BN for income tax and GST/HST immediately upon online completion or after form review.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canadian federal income tax does not provide a check-the-box election; an Alberta ULC is a taxable Canadian corporation in Canada. For foreign tax purposes, notably under IRS Form 8832 and Revenue Ruling 2002-77, an Alberta/BC ULC may be treated as a flow-through or disregarded entity because shareholders have unlimited liability. Canada's hybrid mismatch rules under Income Tax Act ss. 18.4 and 18.5 may deny deductions on payments or supply arrangements designed to exploit entity classification mismatches. No separate GST/HST entity-level election is available; the ULC is itself a registrant for GST/HST.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies made in Canada over any four consecutive calendar quarters; non-resident ULCs carrying on business in Canada must register once this threshold is exceeded. Voluntary registration is permitted, and registration is compulsory for non-residents making taxable supplies of digital or e-commerce services even below the threshold in certain circumstances under the simplified regime.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exported goods qualify for 0% GST/HST if possession of the goods transfers to a non-resident recipient and the goods are exported from Canada as part of the sale, typically by the supplier, recipient, or a common carrier. Supplies of cross-docking, overseas warehouse order fulfillment, and logistics services qualify for 0% GST/HST if the recipient is a non-resident who is not registered under the Excise Tax Act and the service is in respect of goods exported from Canada or is otherwise supplied to the non-resident outside Canada. The ULC must retain contemporaneous commercial and customs evidence demonstrating the goods left Canada and payment was received.

  • Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) Export Declaration / Canada Customs Coding Form B13A
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing foreign destination
  • Commercial Invoice and contract of sale / service agreement
  • Proof of export issued by customs broker, freight forwarder, or carrier
  • Inward remittance certificate / FX settlement record and bank payment evidence

Canada has no real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or API-based clearance mandate. CRA requires GST/HST registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 1,000,000 to file GST/HST returns electronically. Electronic invoicing is permitted and recognized, but no CRA pre-authorization or fiscal stamp is required. Invoices must contain the supplier's BN and GST/HST registration number.

CRA may reassess zero-rated exports within the normal four-year assessment period, extended indefinitely for misrepresentation, fraud, or willful default. False zero-rating claims invalidate the 0% rate and expose the ULC to excise tax reassessment, interest, and penalties under Excise Tax Act ss. 280-291, including 5% penalties for failure to comply, gross negligence penalties of up to 25% of the tax sought to be evaded, and fines or imprisonment in serious fraud cases. Input tax credits previously claimed on exported goods and services may be clawed back retroactively with compounded interest.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full capital and current account convertibility for the Canadian dollar. There are no exchange controls, no surrender requirements, and no restrictions on the conversion, holding, or cross-border movement of funds. Profit remittance, dividends, and repatriation of capital are freely permitted, subject to corporate income tax, withholding tax, and reporting requirements under the Income Tax Act and FATCA/CRS disclosure norms.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline for export proceeds repatriation, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no requirement to sell export receipts into local currency. Exporters may hold foreign exchange proceeds indefinitely in Canadian or offshore accounts, but must comply with FINTRAC large transaction reporting, tax disclosure of foreign assets (e.g., T1135), and general record-keeping obligations.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada; foreign exchange administration is decentralized and shared with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as prudential regulator and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as AML/CFT supervisor under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium with structural limitations. Canada does not issue electronic money institution (EMI) licenses; only chartered banks under the Bank Act may take deposits. Offshore digital EMIs cannot open primary deposit accounts for a Canadian entity and cannot act as local clearing banks. They may operate as money service businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC and, in Alberta, hold a provincial money-services business license. The ULC should use a Schedule I Canadian chartered bank as the primary clearing counterparty and a fintech EMIs such as Airwallex as a supplementary payment corridor for cross-border e-commerce settlements, FX conversion, and vendor disbursements, but never as the sole treasury repository.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal general corporate income tax rate: 15%. Alberta general corporate income tax rate: 8%. Combined general rate: 23%. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally obtain the small business rate on eligible active business income up to CAD 500,000: federal 9% plus Alberta 2%, for a combined rate of 11%. A non-resident-controlled ULC generally does not qualify for the Canadian-controlled private corporation small business regime.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The Canadian statutory Part XIII withholding rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is 25%. A treaty may reduce the rate. Under the Canada-United States treaty, the rate is generally 5% where the beneficial owner is a company owning at least 10% of the voting stock of the payer, and 15% in other cases. Treaty eligibility depends on residence, beneficial ownership, limitation-on-benefits or equivalent anti-abuse provisions, and the specific treaty text.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

The T2 and AT1 returns are generally due within six months after the end of each taxation year. A corporation's balance of tax is generally payable two months after year-end. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally receive a three-month balance-due period where the statutory conditions are satisfied. Instalment obligations apply during the year and are not replaced by the annual return deadline.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Deadline: · Frequency:

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a universal CAD monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is unnecessary for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. Section 247 requires contemporaneous documentation for relevant non-arm's-length transactions, subject to statutory conditions and reasonable-efforts requirements. Form T106 generally applies where reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000 in aggregate or other prescribed conditions apply. Country-by-Country Reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year, measured using the Canadian-dollar equivalent prescribed for the relevant year. Canada has not adopted a separate domestic Master File and Local File regime using the OECD labels, but OECD-style functional and economic analysis is expected for section 247 documentation.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone offshore Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to the UAE or certain other jurisdictions. Substance is nevertheless material under the Income Tax Act, the general anti-avoidance rule, transfer-pricing rules, treaty anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership requirements, sham-transaction doctrine, principal-purpose analysis, thin-capitalization rules, foreign-affiliate rules, and the common-law business-purpose and factual-context analysis. The ULC should maintain real Canadian management and control where Canadian residence is claimed, personnel or outsourced operational capacity appropriate to the fulfillment activity, Alberta premises or operational nexus, commercial contracts, inventory and risk records, independent pricing support, bank-account control, and contemporaneous board and accounting records.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act section 239, conviction may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to two years, or both, in addition to reassessed tax and interest. False statements or omissions may also trigger civil penalties, including gross-negligence penalties generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable under section 227.1 for unremitted source deductions and certain amounts where statutory due-diligence defenses fail. Criminal conviction can produce fines, imprisonment, asset-restraint orders, and reputational consequences. Travel restrictions are not automatic solely because of a tax reassessment; court orders, bail conditions, or passport measures may arise in criminal proceedings.
Unremitted Payroll, Part XIII, GST/HST, or Other Trust Amounts

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unpaid amount, prescribed interest, late-remittance penalties commonly ranging from 10% to 20%, director assessments, collection action, garnishment, liens, and possible prosecution. GST/HST failures may also produce penalties for incorrect returns, failure to register, and failure to collect or remit.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for unremitted source deductions and certain trust amounts if the corporation failed to remit and the director cannot establish due diligence. Corporate insolvency does not automatically discharge these liabilities.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust income under section 247, deny or recharacterize deductions, assess withholding tax, impose transfer-pricing penalties where the statutory conditions are met, and charge interest. Failure to provide reasonable contemporaneous documentation may materially increase penalty exposure and audit risk.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable for a corporate transfer-pricing reassessment solely because they approved a transaction. Personal exposure may arise for knowing participation in fraud, false records, tax evasion, breach of fiduciary duty, or statutory source-deduction liabilities.
Treaty Abuse and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: The CRA may deny treaty relief, assess withholding at 25%, impose arrears interest and penalties, apply the general anti-avoidance rule or treaty anti-abuse provisions, and pursue gross-negligence or criminal sanctions where facts support intentional deception.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability for unremitted withholding tax and possible criminal exposure where they knowingly participate in false documentation or evasion. Civil veil piercing is exceptional and requires recognized legal grounds such as fraud or abuse.
Shell Company, Sham, or Artificial Alberta Presence

Statutory sanction: Potential denial of Alberta allocation or incentives, corporate-residence reassessment, application of the general anti-avoidance rule, transfer-pricing adjustments, interest, penalties, and reassessment of Canadian or foreign permanent-establishment consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil claims for breach of fiduciary duty and personal exposure where the arrangement is fraudulent or knowingly unlawful. A ULC's unlimited-liability status may expose shareholders under applicable corporate law, but it does not automatically make directors personally liable for ordinary corporate tax debts.
Corporate Record and Beneficial-Ownership Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Corporate-law penalties, administrative fines, inability to defend tax positions, information-demand penalties, reassessments based on incomplete records, and potential beneficial-ownership disclosure enforcement may apply. Record retention is generally required for at least six years after the end of the relevant taxation year, subject to longer periods for unresolved matters.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for statutory corporate-law breaches, obstruction, or failure to exercise reasonable care. Personal liability is fact-dependent and is not automatic for an ordinary recordkeeping deficiency.
AML, Proceeds of Crime, and Sanctions Exposure

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act may impose compliance, reporting, recordkeeping, and administrative monetary penalties where the entity is a reporting entity. Criminal Code money-laundering or proceeds-of-crime offences can result in substantial fines and imprisonment. Canadian sanctions legislation may impose asset freezes, prohibitions, and criminal penalties.

Director personal liability: Officers and directors may face personal criminal liability for knowing participation, counselling, directing, or wilful blindness. A standard third-party logistics operator is not automatically a FINTRAC reporting entity solely because it handles commercial payments, but payment, remittance, virtual-currency, factoring, or financial-intermediation activities may change the analysis.
Customs, Import, Export, and GST/HST Misclassification

Statutory sanction: CBSA assessments, duties, GST, penalties, interest, seizure, import privileges suspension, administrative monetary penalties, and prosecution may apply. False customs declarations can also create criminal exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for knowing customs fraud, obstruction, or source-deduction liabilities. Corporate customs debt generally remains corporate unless statutory or criminal grounds support personal liability.
Corporate Insolvency and Creditor-Diversion Risk

Statutory sanction: Transactions may be set aside under federal insolvency legislation or provincial corporate law. Recipients may be required to repay transfers, and directors may face statutory claims, oppression remedies, disqualification, or fraud-related prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors may face restitution, contribution claims, disqualification, and personal judgments for unlawful dividends or knowing creditor prejudice. Travel bans or imprisonment are not routine civil consequences but may follow criminal proceedings or court orders.
