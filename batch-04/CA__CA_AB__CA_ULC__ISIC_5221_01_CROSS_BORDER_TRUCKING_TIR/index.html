Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses/business-account.html)

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada or registering under the Excise Tax Act (ETA) must obtain a Canada Revenue Agency business number (BN) via the CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) service. The BN is a 9-digit number optionally followed by a program account identifier (e.g., RT for GST/HST, RP for payroll). Non-resident entities may also apply by completing Form RC4591 (Request for a Business Number from a Non-Resident) or by mail/fax to the CRA International and Large Business Directorate. The entity must provide the legal name, Alberta corporate registration number or foreign registration certificate, director/officer details, business address, mailing address, and estimated Canadian revenue. CRA will issue a BN/RT account for GST/HST upon registration. For Alberta provincial tax purposes, the ULC as an extraprovincial-registered unlimited liability corporation must register under the Alberta Corporate Registry; for Alberta corporate income tax (which is effectively administered by CRA under the Tax Collection Agreements) no separate provincial TIN is used beyond the federal BN. The same BN with program account codes is used for GST/HST, payroll, import/export duties, and fuel charge (where applicable).

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible. An Alberta/BC Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) is treated for Canadian federal income tax purposes as a person that is required to file an income tax return, but it is a hybrid entity: it is a taxable Canadian person for Canadian domestic law, yet for US tax purposes it may elect under the US check-the-box rules to be treated as a disregarded entity or partnership, and under subsections 212(13.1) and 216(1) provisions of the Income Tax Act, a ULC formed under Alberta law (as a provincial unlimited liability company) may elect under subsection 216(1) for the gross-basis Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on Canadian-source dividends, interest, and rents, subject to the terms of applicable tax treaties. For GST/HST purposes, a ULC is a 'financial consumer' only in limited cases; generally it is a 'person' that is a registrant or a person that is required to be registered. The default classification for the entity is a pass-through eligible entity to the extent that it is not converted to a taxable Canadian corporation; however, for Canadian domestic tax administration, the ULC is generally taxed as a corporation unless a special election applies. The target entity is within Alberta, a jurisdiction where ULCs are incorporated under the Business Corporations Act (Alberta), and the default is entity-level taxation unless the non-resident member has made a valid 'branch or subsidiary' classification for foreign tax purposes and where the Canada-US treaty allows hybrid entity relief. For the purposes of this dossier, the classification is Pass_Through_Eligible in the sense that the ULC may choose pursuant to the Income Tax Act and the Canada-US tax treaty to be treated as a flow-through or as a corporation for treaty purposes, but the Canadian domestic default is entity-level taxation. Territorial scope: Alberta is a province within Canada; for GST/HST, the ULC is subject to federal GST at 5% and Alberta has no provincial sales tax. Non-resident ULCs carrying on a trucking business in Canada are taxed under section 115 of the Income Tax Act on Canadian-source business income. The export zero-rating provisions apply to goods and certain services dispatched outside Canada. — A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada or registering under the Excise Tax Act (ETA) must obtain a Canada Revenue Agency business number (BN) via the CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) service. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible. An Alberta/BC Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) is treated for Canadian federal income tax purposes as a person that is required to file an income tax return, but it is a hybrid entity: it is a taxable Canadian person for Canadian domestic law, yet for US tax purposes it may elect under the US check-the-box rules to be treated as a disregarded entity or partnership, and under subsections 212(13.1) and 216(1) provisions of the Income Tax Act, a ULC formed under Alberta law (as a provincial unlimited liability company) may elect under subsection 216(1) for the gross-basis Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on Canadian-source dividends, interest, and rents, subject to the terms of applicable tax treaties. For GST/HST purposes, a ULC is a 'financial consumer' only in limited cases; generally it is a 'person' that is a registrant or a person that is required to be registered. The default classification for the entity is a pass-through eligible entity to the extent that it is not converted to a taxable Canadian corporation; however, for Canadian domestic tax administration, the ULC is generally taxed as a corporation unless a special election applies. The target entity is within Alberta, a jurisdiction where ULCs are incorporated under the Business Corporations Act (Alberta), and the default is entity-level taxation unless the non-resident member has made a valid 'branch or subsidiary' classification for foreign tax purposes and where the Canada-US treaty allows hybrid entity relief. Non-resident ULCs carrying on a trucking business in Canada are taxed under section 115 of the Income Tax Act on Canadian-source business income. The export zero-rating provisions apply to goods and certain services dispatched outside Canada.

Available election forms

1) US Check-the-Box Election (Form 8832): a Canadian ULC owned by a US person may elect to be disregarded or a partnership for US federal tax purposes, which creates hybrid status; 2) Subsection 216(1) Election: a ULC resident in Canada but owned by non-residents may elect to file a return under Part I on the basis of Canadian-source income from property or business and be subject to tax under Part I instead of Part XIII withholding, and in appropriate cases under the Canada-US treaty to obtain treaty benefits; 3) GST/HST Group Registration election under section 240 of the ETA, which permits closely related corporations to file a combined GST/HST return and to treat inter-group supplies as not taxable, subject to CRA approval; 4) Provincial corporate tax allocation election: under the Alberta Corporate Tax Act, the ULC is allocated business income between provinces based on the Tempered Formula (gross revenue and payroll) as set out in Part 4 of the Regulation, and the entity may be required to file an Alberta corporate income tax return; 5) Election under section 1502 of the Income Tax Regulations for the use of a non-calendar taxation year if the entity is a 'foreign-owned investment corporation' or if the principal business is trucking and it is reasonable; 6) For specific trucking TIR operations, the Cross-Border Trucking Services election under the Canada-US Tax Treaty Article V (Permanent Establishment) rules and the Mutual Agreement Procedure (MAP) may be used to clarify whether a fixed place of business in Canada or a US carrier's temporary presence creates a permanent establishment; 7) Hybrid mismatch election: under the Income Tax Act and the Multilateral Instrument (MLI), a ULC with a US shareholder may elect to apply the transparent treatment to avoid double non-taxation, but statutory hybrid rules (including section 18.4(3)(b) of the Income Tax Act for excessive interest and interest deductibility restrictions) apply to deny deduction for hybrid mismatches. The ULC should execute a valid continuation or amalgamation election if the entity is continued into Alberta from another province; 8) ATIP/road user charge registration for International Fuel Tax Agreement (IFTA) through Alberta Transportation, which is a registration not an income tax election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 per calendar quarter and CAD 30,000 per single quarter for non-resident vendors under the simplified GST/HST registration regime; but a non-resident engaged in commercial activity in Canada (including carrying on a trucking business) is required to register if it makes taxable supplies in Canada in the ordinary course of business and the total consideration from worldwide taxable supplies exceeds CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter or over the last four consecutive calendar quarters (ETA subsection 240(1.1) and section 241). For transportation services to or from Canada, there are special rules for the place of supply; where the transportation is considered supplied in Canada, the threshold applies. A non-resident carrier that is registered may be eligible for the streamlined 'non-resident simplified GST/HST registration and filing' process. There is no registration threshold for big businesses: a person required to register must do so, and failure to register is an offence.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A cross-border trucking service that is a supply of a transportation service in respect of goods that are delivered to a destination outside Canada is generally zero-rated under Part VII of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act (section 7, paragraph 7(a): a supply of a service of transporting goods from a place in Canada to a place outside Canada is zero-rated). In addition, a supply of a freight transportation service in respect of goods transported from a place outside Canada to a place in Canada is zero-rated when the goods are not acquired for consumption, use, or supply in Canada (ETA Schedule VI, Part VII, section 7(b)). If the ULC provides freight trucking services that are part of a continuous international transport of goods (i.e., from point in Canada to point outside Canada), the entire service is zero-rated to the extent it is in respect of goods that are exported, even if the portion of the service within Canada is performed by a Canadian sub-carrier. The zero-rating is available only when the supplier maintains documentary evidence substantiating that the goods were delivered to a destination outside Canada. If the service is provided to a Canadian resident for goods that are delivered to a destination outside Canada but the service is not rendered to the ULC in the ordinary course of the transport (e.g., warehousing ancillary to freight), the supply may be exempt or taxable; the criteria require that the direct or substantial purpose of the service is the international transport of goods. Additionally, the supply of a TIR carnet-related customs escort or security service is not zero-rated; only the freight transportation service and incidental loading/unloading are zero-rated. For US-origin TIR trucks entering Canada and then leaving, the Canadian portion of the inbound service may be zero-rated under ETA subsection 123(1) definition of 'export' if goods are not released into Canada. The ULC must be a GST/HST registrant to claim zero-rating; it may claim input tax credits (ITCs) for the GST/HST paid on Canadian fuel, maintenance, and depot expenses, and any non-creditable provincial fuel charge is a cost. For zero-rating of the fuel surcharge or sub-carrier services, the entity must issue an invoice that clearly indicates zero-rated supplies. A transport of empty trailers by a Canadian carrier for a US carrier may not qualify because no 'goods' are transported; such service is generally taxable at 5% GST in Canada unless it qualifies as a supply of a vehicle (which is not zero-rated).

  • Customs Export Declaration: a completed CBSA Form B13A (Export Declaration) or an electronic export declaration through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CAERS), including the carrier's BN/RT account number, exporter's name and address, the destination, the classification of the goods under the Canadian Export Control List or ECL, quantity, value, and the customs office of exit.
  • Bill of Lading (through bill of lading or TIR carnet U.S. Customs and Border Protection/SAD or international TIR carnet as the transit document): a non-negotiable or negotiated through BOL clearly identifying the ultimate destination and the consignee outside Canada, and for TIR carnet shipments, the TIR Carnet number, holder, and guarantee chain.
  • TIR Carnet (if used): the international customs transit document issued under the TIR Convention, with the TIR carnet status plate, issued by the Canadian Chamber of Commerce as the national guaranteeing association, and countersigned or e-sealed by CBSA at the office of departure; this is the core proof of bonded transit and customs clearance.
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (or equivalent bank statement/proof of payment): a bank document from the Canadian or foreign financial institution showing that the freight payment for the export transport was received from the non-resident customer in foreign currency or Canadian dollars; the CBSA does not require an inward remittance certificate in the same manner as some developing economies, but CRA audits commonly require evidence that the consideration was paid by the foreign recipient, and the Canada Border Services Agency export verification requires proof of the export transaction, including payment terms, invoices, and bank records.
  • Commercial invoice showing the zero-rated description of the international transportation service with a clear statement 'zero-rated under ETA Schedule VI Part VII' or a GST/HST zero-rated code.
  • Continuous International Transport Evidence: if the freight is a through-shipment under a single contract that includes pick-up in Canada and delivery outside Canada, the carrier must retain the primary contract or its electronic equivalent.
  • Carrier's log and customs manifest (eManifest submitted by highway carrier) confirming the actual movement of the truck across the border with the goods and the TIR seal integrity.

Canada does not currently operate a mandatory national real-time e-invoicing or fiscal clearance regime comparable to Latin American countries. The CRA accepts electronic invoices and requires record-keeping in an electronic format that must be accessible. The federal GST/HST regime requires registrants to issue simplified invoices or full invoices containing the vendor's BN, the date, the consideration, the GST/HST paid or payable, and the tax status (zero-rated, exempt, or taxable). For the trucking industry, the Alberta Motor Transport Board and CRA do not require digital stamping. However, mandatory electronic reporting applies to certain accounts with the CRA when using the GST/HST NETFILE process, and the Government of Canada mandates electronic payment for amounts over CAD 10,000 to the Receiver General. For customs and export cargo, the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) operates the Canadian Single Window via the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) system, which requires the electronic filing of the Customs Cargo Declaration and export reports through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CAERS). CARM enforces a principal/registered importer/exporter bond and requires a Business Number (BN) plus carrier codes. For highway carriers crossing the border under the TIR Carnet, the CBSA requires an electronic Advance Commercial Information (ACI) for highway cargo, transmitted via approved EDI service providers. Under the CBSA 'eManifest' regulatory framework, highway carriers must transmit cargo and conveyance data in electronic format before arrival. The TIR carnet itself is a paper or electronic international customs transit document, with digital data exchange through the IRU's TIR-EPD (Electronic Pre-Declaration). There is no 'digital stamping' under GST/HST, but the CBSA uses the eManifest to clear and control bonded shipments. Invoicing to US clients for cross-border trucking should comply with CBSA export documentation and any US customs requirements (ACE e-Manifest), not a Canadian fiscal clearance system.

CRA and CBSA apply strict audit scrutiny to zero-rated export transportation claims. If a supplier makes a zero-rated supply of a freight transportation service without maintaining adequate evidence of the goods' delivery outside Canada, the supply is reclassified as taxable at 5% GST, and the supplier is liable for the tax not collected, plus interest from the date the tax was due (current prescribed interest rates, compounded daily, generally can add 5% or more per annum). Under ETA subsection 280(1), a person who knowingly, or under circumstances of gross negligence, makes a false statement or omission that results in a lower assessment is liable to a penalty of the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the tax that would have been payable had the person known the true facts. Under section 275, failure to comply with the Excise Tax Act can result in fines on summary conviction up to CAD 50,000. The CBSA may also issue administrative monetary penalties under the Customs Act and the DPF (Detail Penalties) scheme for incorrect export declarations: penalties of up to CAD 1,000 per declaration for errors, up to CAD 4,000 for negligence, and up to CAD 8,000 for false statements if the goods' value is under CAD 25,000, with higher amounts for higher values, plus the potential for seizure of goods or loss of the exporter's 'low-risk' corridor privileges. For TIR Carnet operations, the guaranteeing association (Canadian Chamber of Commerce) may invoke the TIR guarantee and require the carrier or the national association to pay the customs duties and taxes in the country of irregularity, and the CBSA can cancel the TIR Carnet authorization, suspend the carrier from the TIR system, and impose a penalty up to CAD 10,000 per occurrence under the Customs Act. The Income Tax Act also applies: if a tax benefit arises from the mischaracterization of a zero-rated supply, the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section 245 may apply, and gross-negligence penalties under subsection 163(2) of the Income Tax Act can be up to 50% of the understated tax. In the case of a ULC that has a US member, the Canada-US treaty's limitation-on-benefits provision and the hybrid entity rules can expose the entity to Part XIII withholding tax on deemed dividends or to denial of treaty benefits, with retroactive assessment within the normal reassessment period (4 years for income tax, 8 years for GST/HST in certain situations, and no time limit for misrepresentation or fraud). The Criminal Code of Canada also criminalizes willful evasion of federal taxes, with penalties of imprisonment up to 5 years and fines of up to 200% of the evaded tax (CRA's prosecution policy). To limit exposure, the ULC should implement a zero-rating substantiation file for at least 6 years, including the export declaration, eManifest ACI data, TIR carnet status, BOL, commercial invoice, and proof of FX receipt. An inward remittance certificate from a Canadian bank or SWIFT confirmation is not mandatory by CRA regulation but is the strongest evidence in a cross-border contract audit; the file must be retained in either paper or electronic form at the carrier's principal place of business in Canada or with its Canadian tax representative. Failure to produce the evidence on audit triggers the assessment of GST/HST, interest, penalties, and potential prosecution for misrepresentation.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility under the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act. There are no foreign-exchange controls, no surrender requirements, and no administrative licensing for ordinary trade-related conversions between CAD, USD, and other major currencies. Profit remittance by an Alberta ULC is permitted without exchange-control approval, but is subject to Income Tax Act Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on dividends, interest, royalties, and management fees, subject to any applicable tax treaty. CAD is a freely floating currency; settlement of CAD/USD is cleared through CLS Bank and the Bank of Canada's large-value system. Investment Canada Act review applies only to acquisitions of control, not to day-to-day treasury flows. FINTRAC reporting obligations apply to cross-border EFTs of CAD 10,000 or more, but these are reporting thresholds, not convertibility barriers.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender rule applies in Canada. There is no mandated timeline and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. A Canadian exporter may lawfully retain USD or other foreign-currency proceeds in offshore or onshore accounts. Conversion to CAD, if desired, is executed at the prevailing interbank or bank-offered market rate. All such transactions remain subject to standard FINTRAC reporting, sanctions screening, and the bank's internal compliance thresholds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); prudential supervision by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); provincial registry and regulatory oversight for Alberta-incorporated entities by the Alberta Corporate Registry and ATB Financial under applicable Alberta statutes.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium viability for auxiliary payments, low viability as principal treasury bank. Canadian-regulated financial technology firms with money-services-business registration may provide card settlement, cross-border payout, and multi-currency wallet services, but only a bank or a recognized cooperative with Payments Canada membership can provide direct access to CAD clearing and settlement rails. Foreign electronic-money institutions (EMIs) without a Canadian banking charter generally cannot open a primary corporate account for an Alberta ULC on a standalone basis; if used, they must operate through a sponsor Canadian bank holding the ultimate cleared funds. This creates concentration and operational risk for a TIR-bonded cross-border trucking operation. The recommended structure is a Schedule I bank for operational and escrow accounts, supplemented by a regulated fintech or EMI for specialized fuel-card and US-dollar settlement layers.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Non-resident control of an Alberta ULC, particularly if immediate shareholders reside in a jurisdiction the bank classifies as high risk or with unusual source-of-wealth documentation.

Root cause: Non-resident control of an Alberta ULC, particularly if immediate shareholders reside in a jurisdiction the bank classifies as high risk or with unusual source-of-wealth documentation.

Defense evidence: Alberta Certificate of Status; certified charter and by-laws; UBO register; corporate resolution authorizing account opening and capital contributions; Canadian legal opinion confirming valid ULC existence and unlimited liability; shareholder identification with government-issued IDs; three years of personal or corporate bank statements; shareholder tax returns; source-of-wealth declaration; commercial references from home-jurisdiction banking counterparties.
TIR Carnet bonded-traffic status or customs-bond exposure indicating high-value road cargo movements across multiple jurisdictions.

Root cause: TIR Carnet bonded-traffic status or customs-bond exposure indicating high-value road cargo movements across multiple jurisdictions.

Defense evidence: Valid TIR Carnet holder contract issued through the Canadian Chamber of Commerce under IRU regulations; TIR Carnet guarantee confirmation from the national guaranteeing association; Customs Bond compliance certificate; Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) business number and customs-account validation; carrier registration under the Canada Transportation Act; ISIC 5221 trade license; standard transport contracts; driver and vehicle register; Cargo liability insurance certificate.
High transaction velocity involving fuel-card clearing, US-dollar receivables, and multiple contractor settlements that may resemble layering or unusual payment patterns.

Root cause: High transaction velocity involving fuel-card clearing, US-dollar receivables, and multiple contractor settlements that may resemble layering or unusual payment patterns.

Defense evidence: Fuel-card program agreement with an accredited issuer (WEX, Comdata, EFS, or equivalent); daily electronic fuel-card transaction logs; settlement statements; truck fleet ownership or lease agreements; contractor agreements with CDL verification; periodic invoice-to-cash reconciliation reports; general ledger extract showing business purpose of each disbursement class; financial statements reviewed by a Canadian accountant.
Large scheduled USD receipts from a concentrated set of US freight forwarders or brokers, creating counterparty-pattern concerns.

Root cause: Large scheduled USD receipts from a concentrated set of US freight forwarders or brokers, creating counterparty-pattern concerns.

Defense evidence: Executed US freight contracts and broker agreements; US DOT operating authority registration; Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) identification; BOC-3 process-agent designation; US carrier insurance certificate; ageing receivable reports; freight-bill audit trail; bank reference from the US collection bank; explanation memo on cross-border corridor lanes and trade-party relationships.
Capital escrow account funding from non-resident shareholders after initial incorporation, whether by equity subscription or shareholder loan.

Root cause: Capital escrow account funding from non-resident shareholders after initial incorporation, whether by equity subscription or shareholder loan.

Defense evidence: Share subscription agreement or loan agreement; board resolution approving the capital transaction; funds-flow diagram from source bank to Alberta ULC account; source-bank statements; tax characterization note for Part XIII withholding or treaty relief; confirmation funds are not derived from proceeds of crime; economic-substance summary showing fleet acquisition, terminal lease, and Canadian operational commitments.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

All inbound capital injections, whether equity contributions, shareholder loans, or intercompany funding, should be routed through the SWIFT network as MT103 messages to the primary Canadian Schedule I bank. The recommended clearing path is originator bank SWIFT correspondent to RBC/TD/BMO/CIBC Canadian clearing channel, with final CAD settlement via Payments Canada Lynx or the CLS CAD settlement layer. Canada does not operate a mandatory foreign-exchange purpose-code regime; instead the remittance must be described in SWIFT field /70 Remittance Information and, when applicable, field /33B Currency and Amount. The narrative should state precisely 'CA-ULC shareholder capital contribution' or 'CA-ULC shareholder loan per agreement dated [date]'. The bank will complete FINTRAC EFT reporting for CAD 10,000 or greater. To prove source of funds and legal entitlement, provide shareholder resolution, executed contribution or loan agreement, source bank statements, and an excerpt of the Alberta corporate registry confirming the ULC's active status. For USD injections retained as foreign currency, use a USD-denominated account at the same bank with a distinct account number to avoid FX conversion until required. More complex injection round-tripping should be avoided; all funds should flow directly from the shareholder's external account to the Alberta ULC operating account to prevent anti-money-laundering typology concerns.The outbound treasury rails comprise four distinct channels. First, SWIFT MT103 for high-value Canadian-dollar or US-dollar payments to foreign vendors, customs brokers, and non-resident truckstop fuel-card issuers; such wires should be executed before 14:00 Eastern Time to confirm same-day Lynx settlement in CAD or US Fedwire/Eagle correspondent settlement for USD. Second, domestic large-value and bulk payments within Canada are cleared through Payments Canada Lynx for transactions at or above CAD 30 million and through Retail Batch for smaller trade disbursements; standard supplier invoices, insurance premiums, and Canadian payroll are routed through the bank's commercial payments platform. Third, commercial letters of credit should support high-value rolling-stock acquisitions, trailer purchases, and warehouse equipment; the LC should be issued by the principal Canadian bank as a confirmed or unconfirmed documentary credit in USD or EUR depending on supplier jurisdiction, with full Marine/Overland Transport insurance documents and TIR Carnet references in the transport document field. Fourth, fuel-card clearing is settled through branded B2B fuel-card networks (WEX, Comdata, EFS) on a weekly or daily settlement cycle, with drawdown directly from the centralized CAD/USD operating account. All outbound disbursements above CAD 10,000 must be sandboxed for sanctions, PEP screening, and OFAC/European Union restrictive-measure matching before release. Standing instructions should be registered with the bank for recurring disbursements such as customs-bond premiums, TIR guarantee-renewal fees, and fleet insurance installments, using negative-file exceptions only where contractually justified.Outright deliverable CAD/USD forward contracts, executed with a Canadian Schedule I bank under an ISDA or master FX agreement, used to lock conversion rates for known USD-denominated freight receivables and CAD-denominated fuel and payroll obligations.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs), permitted for settlement against less-liquid emerging-market currencies encountered on TIR transit legs where no meaningful deliverable CAD liquidity exists; settlement in USD or CAD at the fixing rate of an agreed reference source.Cross-currency swaps, executed under an ISDA Credit Support Annex, used for material medium-term USD borrowings or intercompany loans to match USD cargo-revenue streams against CAD operational expenditures and debt service.Plain-vanilla FX swaps, for temporary liquidity transformation between CAD and USD within the same banking group, subject to strict back-to-back matching with cargo-related contract cycles.
