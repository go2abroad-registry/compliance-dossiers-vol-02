Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), Business Registration Online (BRO) portal via https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html; Alberta has no provincial sales tax authority and the province imposes no PST, so the federal GST/HST program account is the sole indirect-tax registration. For marine bunkering, the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) administers customs export reporting and the federal excise duty framework under the Excise Act, 2001.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned Alberta ULC must: (1) incorporate or register extra-provincially under the Alberta Business Corporations Act to obtain an Alberta corporate access number; (2) apply to the CRA for a Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 or using Business Registration Online, specifying the GST/HST program account (RT0001); (3) designate a Canadian representative (Form RC59) if the ULC is a non-resident entity without a permanent establishment in Canada; (4) enroll for direct deposit (RC3661). The 9-digit BN plus two-letter program identifier constitutes the statutory TIN / VAT ID for all federal tax filings. Non-resident ULCs may also be required to register with CBSA for a carrier/client identifier coincident with Customs Self-Assessment.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation – an Alberta ULC is a taxable Canadian corporation resident in Alberta under the Income Tax Act; it is subject to federal and Alberta corporate income tax on worldwide income at the entity level. Pass-through treatment is not available domestically; only the U.S. parent may, for U.S. federal tax purposes, elect disregarded-entity or partnership status via IRC §301.7701-3 check-the-box, a foreign classification that the CRA disregards for GST/HST and income tax, resulting in hybrid-mismatch exposure having to be managed.

Available election forms

No Canadian check-the-box or general flow-through election exists for a ULC. The only relevant statutory elections are: (i) the U.S. check-the-box election on IRS Form 8832 for the ULC (flow-through/disregarded/partnership treatment) with treaty-side relief considered under Canada-U.S. Income Tax Convention Article IV(7) (fiscally transparent entity); (ii) the GST/HST joint election under Excise Tax Act s. 273 for closely related GST registrants to consolidate returns; (iii) the Unified Tax Reform hybrid-mismatch election mechanisms under Income Tax Act ss. 211.2–211.9, which recharacterize the ULC's transactions with foreign shareholders to prevent deduction/non-inclusion outcomes. A ULC with only non-resident shareholders must also attend to the special conduit-distribution rules governing deemed dividends (ss. 84(1) and 84(7)) on redemption or windup.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 – the small-supplier threshold measured on worldwide taxable supplies; a non-resident ULC making taxable supplies in Canada in the course of a commercial activity must register once the threshold is exceeded, while voluntary registration is allowable to claim input tax credits on Canadian expenditures. Registration is likewise mandatory for zero-rated bunkering suppliers that cannot demonstrate small-supplier status and whose customers require a GST-registered invoice.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act in two relevant limbs: (a) goods shipped by the supplier to a destination outside Canada and exported as part of the supply contract (Schedule VI, Part I, s. 1); and (b) property and services supplied to the operator of an international commercial marine vessel, including bunker fuel, lubricants, water and ship stores supplied while the vessel is engaged in international voyage operations (Schedule VI, Part I, s. 23). The substantive test for bunkering is that the vessel is operated by a non-resident or, if resident, the fuel is realistically destined to be consumed on an international sailing leg outside Canada. Documentary evidence must prove physical export or international-sea consumption and receipt of consideration.

  • Customs Export Declaration filed electronically through the CBSA Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) / Export Declaration (replacing the former B13A) or the applicable barge/vessel manifest where marine export procedures apply
  • Ocean Bill of Lading or marine Bunker Delivery Receipt (BDN) evidencing the international vessel's name, IMO number, port of delivery, voyage itinerary and quantity/quality of fuel delivered
  • Commercial invoice and bunker sale contract referencing the ISIC 5222 / NAICS 488390 activity and showing the 0% GST rate
  • Inward foreign-currency remittance evidence – SWIFT MT103, bank transfer entry or foreign exchange summary – demonstrating receipt of export consideration from a non-resident party
  • Where applicable, excise-duty-paid records and the CBSA marine vessel fuel refund or duty-free entry documentation supporting international commercial navigation

None – Canada imposes no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, no API-based e-invoice clearance, and no digital stamping requirement for GST/HST. The Pan-Canadian (PEPPOL) e-invoicing framework is voluntary for private-sector transactions, and the CRA only mandates electronic filing of GST/HST returns for registrants above prescribed thresholds. The invoice must nevertheless satisfy ETA s. 169/basic book-and-record requirements (s. 286) with a six-year retention period and show GST registration number on supply invoices.

The CRA routinely audits marine bunkering zero-rating claims; failure to substantiate the export/consumption and foreign payment within the evidentiary window results in reassessment adding 5% GST on the sale, plus prescribed arrears interest (ETA s. 280) and, in the case of false or grossly negligent statements, civil penalties under ETA s. 283 (up to the greater of CAD 250 and 40% of the tax sought to be evaded for knowing misrepresentation). Knowingly evading GST is a criminal offence under ETA s. 327 with fines, on indictment, of 50%–200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years. Input tax credits claimed on bunkering purchases can be recaptured under ETA s. 296, and director/shareholder liability for unrecovered GST can be asserted under ETA s. 323. For ULC structures, CRA may further apply the general anti-avoidance rule (Income Tax Act s. 245) and hybrid-mismatch rules where the foreign parent treats the Alberta ULC as transparent while the bunkering revenue is not effectively included in Canadian taxable income.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is generally fully convertible for current-account and capital-account transactions. Canada does not generally impose capital controls, mandatory repatriation of foreign currency, or restrictions on remitting lawful dividends, interest, royalties, loan proceeds, or sale proceeds. Remittances remain subject to corporate-law authorization, solvency requirements, applicable withholding tax, transfer-pricing rules, beneficial-ownership verification, sanctions screening, anti-money-laundering controls, tax reporting, and bank documentation. An Alberta unlimited liability corporation may create additional parent-company tax classification and withholding-tax analysis, particularly where a non-Canadian shareholder treats the entity as fiscally transparent.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada does not impose a general statutory requirement to surrender export proceeds to a Canadian bank, repatriate export receipts within a prescribed period, or convert a mandatory percentage of export receipts into Canadian dollars. Export proceeds may generally remain in foreign-currency accounts or be converted at the holder's discretion. Transactions remain subject to sanctions, anti-terrorist-financing requirements, customs and export-control rules, tax reporting, bank transaction monitoring, and record-retention obligations. Financial institutions may request commercial invoices, bills of lading, vessel information, counterparty details, and evidence of the underlying bunkering transaction before releasing or converting funds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no standalone foreign exchange administration imposing general exchange controls. Financial institution prudential supervision is principally administered by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions, while anti-money-laundering compliance is administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for payment initiation and foreign-exchange execution; Low for replacing a Canadian operating bank account. Canada does not use a single European-style electronic-money-institution licensing regime. A foreign digital EMI generally cannot accept deposits from the public or provide unrestricted payment-account services in Canada without an applicable Canadian authorization, registration, exemption, or regulated-bank partnership. A payment service provider that falls within Canada's Retail Payment Activities Act framework may require Bank of Canada registration and must satisfy operational-risk, safeguarding, incident-reporting, and funds-handling requirements. Foreign EMI balances may also be unsuitable for capital contributions, payroll, trust-like client funds, or high-value marine-fuel settlements. Use a Canadian federally or provincially regulated financial institution as the primary account bank and treat an offshore EMI as a supplemental rail only after legal, sanctions, settlement, safeguarding, and counterparty review.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Contemporaneous Canadian transfer pricing documentation is mandatory when the aggregate annual value of related-party cross-border transactions (e.g., intercompany fuel purchases, management fees, or vessel leases) exceeds CAD 1,000,000. A Country-by-Country Report and Master File are required for multinational groups with annual consolidated revenue of at least CAD 750,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada and Alberta do not maintain standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) of the UAE/GCC type. However, CRA uses the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR), corporate-residence tests, and treaty-benefit rules to deny tax benefits where an Alberta ULC is used as a shell or conduit without real economic substance. The ULC must have substantive board decision-making, employees, physical premises, invoicing, and risk-bearing functions in Alberta for vessel bunkering and refueling operations; otherwise treaty entitlement is exposed and the entity may be recharacterized.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Gross negligence penalty of 50% of the amount of tax sought to be evaded; interest accrual; criminal prosecution under Income Tax Act s.239 with fines up to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to five years.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false returns or direct concealment can be assessed for unremitted Part XIII or payroll taxes under s.227.1; criminal convictions can trigger travel bans, extradition requests, personal civil restitution, and imprisonment.
Shell Company / Treaty Shopping Conduit

Statutory sanction: Application of GAAR to deny the tax benefit; recharacterization of cross-border payments as dividends or royalties; enhanced 25% GAAR penalty on the denied benefit (for transactions after relevant amendment); Part XIII withholding reassessed at 25% plus interest.

Director personal liability: CRA may disregard the corporate status and trace liability to the controlling person; directors who knowingly participate in the sham may be named in civil fraud proceedings, subjected to asset-seizure orders, and prevented from leaving the country in serious tax-recovery proceedings.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing penalty equal to 10% of the net adjustment for transfer-pricing misstatements (ITA s.247(3)); CRA can reassess at arm's-length prices and charge interest from the original due date; facilities may be subject to garnishment for unpaid balances.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically assessed for the TP penalty, but if the mispricing results in deemed payments subject to Part XIII withholding (e.g., deemed interest/royalty), directors may become personally liable for unremitted withholding tax; criminal charges can follow where false invoices are certified.
AML / Sanctions and Money Laundering

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: up to CAD 500,000 for a first offence and CAD 1,000,000 for a subsequent offence; criminal money-laundering prosecution under the Criminal Code carries up to ten years imprisonment; sanctions violations under the UN Act trigger asset forfeiture and fines.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance officers are personally liable where they knowingly directed or failed to report suspicious transactions; they face criminal conviction, personal asset forfeiture, travel restrictions in extradition/sanctions cases, and permanent debarment from Canadian financial services sectors.
