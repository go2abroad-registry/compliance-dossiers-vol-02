Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no general check-the-box election that converts an Alberta ULC into a Canadian tax-transparent entity. An Alberta ULC is generally a Canadian corporation subject to Canadian corporate income tax, notwithstanding its unlimited shareholder liability. For United States tax purposes, an eligible Canadian ULC may generally elect its classification under U.S. Treasury Regulations using Form 8832, potentially as disregarded or partnership status depending on ownership. That foreign election does not change Canadian tax treatment and may create cross-border hybrid-entity, treaty, foreign tax credit, limitation-on-benefits, or hybrid-mismatch consequences. Canadian corporate tax elections may apply to specific transactions, but they are not entity-classification elections.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding supplies of financial services and certain other excluded amounts, measured under the small-supplier rules over a single calendar quarter or the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters. Registration can be voluntary before the threshold is exceeded. A non-resident making taxable supplies in Canada may be required to register even if the ordinary small-supplier threshold is not met, depending on the applicable place-of-supply and carrying-on-business facts.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of goods may generally be zero-rated where the goods are exported from Canada under the Excise Tax Act and the supplier retains evidence that the goods were shipped or delivered outside Canada and were not used, consumed, or supplied in Canada before export, subject to statutory exceptions. A supply of services to a non-resident may be zero-rated only where the service falls within the statutory non-resident-service rules and is not excluded by the place-of-supply provisions. Dry leasing of aircraft engines is not automatically zero-rated merely because the lessee is non-resident or the payment is received offshore. The GST result depends on the location and delivery of the engine, possession or use in Canada, lease characterization, lessee status, export timing, aircraft qualification, and the specific zero-rating provisions for aircraft, aircraft parts, and related services. Where a leased engine remains in Canada or is used in Canada, GST may generally apply unless a specific relieving rule applies. The lessor must retain contracts, delivery and export evidence, engine serial-number records, lessee residency and use certifications, transport records, customs records, payment evidence, and proof supporting the applicable zero-rating provision. Input tax credits and any GST rebate or refund claim require valid documentary support and must be claimed within the applicable statutory limitation periods.

  • Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) export declaration or other CBSA-approved export reporting record, where required
  • Commercial invoice or lease-related commercial documentation identifying the supplier, lessee, engine description, serial number, value or consideration, destination, and transaction terms
  • Air waybill, bill of lading, cargo control document, delivery receipt, or equivalent carrier evidence showing movement outside Canada
  • Transport, handover, and destination records establishing the date and place of export and absence of Canadian use before export where relevant
  • Customs release, temporary-export, re-export, or other applicable customs records
  • Executed lease, delivery, acceptance, insurance, maintenance, and return-condition documents
  • Evidence of non-resident status and business use, together with records establishing the statutory basis for zero-rating
  • Bank payment or remittance records where commercially available; an inward remittance certificate is not a general mandatory Canadian GST document and Canada has no general foreign-exchange repatriation certificate requirement

Canada has no general real-time electronic-invoice clearance, mandatory government e-invoicing network, or universal digital tax-stamping requirement for GST. Commercial invoices and GST invoices may be electronic if they contain the prescribed information, including supplier identity and BN, invoice date, recipient details where required, description, consideration, GST rate, GST amount, and applicable registration references. GST/HST returns and related payments are filed through CRA electronic services, including GST/HST NETFILE or other mandated electronic channels where applicable. Commercial import and export declarations are processed through the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) CARM Client Portal and applicable electronic reporting systems, including the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS).

Canada has no general statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds. CRA may reassess GST that was incorrectly treated as zero-rated, impose applicable interest from the statutory due date, deny related input tax credits or rebates, and assess penalties for failure to remit, false statements, repeated failures, or gross negligence. The gross-negligence penalty can generally be 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed credit, subject to the applicable statutory calculation. False or deceptive documentation may also create civil and criminal exposure, including prosecution under the Excise Tax Act or Criminal Code. CBSA may impose administrative monetary penalties for inaccurate or late export reporting and may pursue customs enforcement remedies. There is no single fixed export-zero-rating clawback period or penalty; reassessment periods, interest, penalty amounts, and enforcement consequences depend on the transaction, conduct, records, and applicable statutory provisions.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility under IMF Article VIII. There are no general exchange controls, no repatriation permits, and no mandatory conversion rates. Non-residents may freely convert CAD to foreign currency and remit income, dividends, interest, and lease rentals after fulfilling tax withholding and FINTRAC reporting obligations. Dividend and interest distributions from a Canadian ULC to non-resident shareholders are subject to Part XIII withholding tax (typically 25% absent treaty relief, reducible under applicable tax treaties). Aircraft-engine dry-leasing receipts are current-account rental income and are freely remittable. Practical constraints arise only from bank AML/KYC procedures and the requirement to provide accurate wire purpose codes and supporting leasing documentation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement and no mandated conversion ratio in Canada. Export receipts, including engine lease rental payments received in foreign currency, may be retained, converted, or used offshore at the exporter's discretion. However, all cross-border electronic funds transfers and cash transactions of CAD 10,000 or more are reportable to FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, and financial institutions must maintain sufficient audit trails. Export documentation and lease invoices must support the payment narrative for AML and tax-revenue reconciliation.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (monetary authority) and Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) (banking supervision); FINTRAC (anti-money-laundering and cross-border funds transfer reporting); Global Affairs Canada and Finance Canada for sanctions and international financial policy. Canada does not operate a dedicated foreign-exchange control authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-Low. Foreign fintech e-money institutions (EMIs) and digital payment service providers are not regulated as banks in Canada and cannot accept deposits in the ordinary course of a Canadian corporation's treasury operations. They may only operate as money services businesses (MSBs) or foreign money services businesses if registered with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, and they generally lack direct access to the Canadian high-value payment system (Lynx). Such entities are best used as auxiliary collection/currency-conversion tools only, not as primary treasury accounts for a ULC engaged in cross-border aircraft-engine leasing. Legal constraints include compliance with Canadian banking law, Canadian sanctions, and FATF transparency requirements.
