Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); taxpayer portal: My Business Account (CRA's secure online portal for corporate and GST/HST accounts).

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada, including an Alberta Unlimited Liability Company (ULC), must obtain a 9-digit federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA. The BN is the statutory TIN for federal and Alberta-administered tax accounts. Registration is initiated through the CRA's 'Register a Business Account' e-service or by filing Form RC1/RC196 with supporting corporate/foreign registration documents. The entity must activate GST/HST program account (RT suffix) once taxable supplies exceed the CAD 30,000 small supplier threshold, or register voluntarily; an import/export program account (RM suffix) is required for customs transactions. Foreign entities without a permanent establishment may register as non-resident registrants, and may be required to appoint a resident agent or nominee under the Excise Tax Act for service of documents and tax collection. After issuance of the BN, the ULC must file GST/HST returns through CRA's electronic filing channels and corporate income tax returns as applicable.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

An Alberta ULC is generally treated as a taxable corporation under Income Tax Act and Alberta corporate tax law. However, it is eligible for U.S. check-the-box pass-through or disregarded-entity classification for U.S. federal tax purposes, which can create hybrid or transparent mismatches. In Canada, there is no general check-the-box election for federal tax classification, but a taxpayer may elect a non-calendar taxation year under section 249.1, elect under section 216(4) for Canadian rental and royalty income, or file under section 216(1) non-resident election. For GST/HST, non-resident suppliers may elect to use simplified registration procedures or appoint an agent under section 267(1) of the Excise Tax Act. Hybrid mismatch arrangements must be reviewed against OECD BEPS Action 2 and Canada's hybrid mismatch rules in sections 18.4-18.6, with no automatic domestic pass-through election unless the entity is a foreign-owned ULC used for U.S.-style check-the-box planning.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 worldwide taxable supplies in a calendar quarter or over the last four consecutive calendar quarters; once exceeded, mandatory GST/HST registration is triggered. The threshold excludes incidental supplies and supplies of financial services, and a non-resident air cargo charter broker may elect to register voluntarily even below this threshold to claim input tax credits and support zero-rating of exports.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of goods is zero-rated under the Excise Tax Act where the seller exports the goods and provides documentary evidence of export. Services supplied to a non-resident are generally zero-rated when the non-resident recipient is outside Canada at the time the service is rendered and the service is not related to real or tangible personal property situated in Canada. Air cargo charter brokerage services, including arranging the charter or brokerage of aircraft cargo capacity, may be zero-rated where the service is directly in connection with goods exported from Canada, the recipient is a non-resident, and the service is not supplied to a Canadian resident. The ULC must maintain clear contractual and operational evidence that the charter, freight brokerage, and related logistics services relate to export air cargo and that title or risk passed outside Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13 or electronic export report in the Canadian Single Window for the air cargo shipment), including the Canadian exporter name, description of goods, air waybill number, and destination country.
  • Air Waybill (AWB) / House Air Waybill, Bill of Lading, and the cargo charter agreement showing the non-resident shipper or consignee, routing outside Canada, and the nature of the international air cargo movement.
  • Bank payment and inward remittance evidence, including SWIFT wire or Fedwire confirmation, bank credit advice, or an inward remittance certificate presented by the paying bank, proving the non-resident counterparty remitted the consideration within the prescribed 180-day foreign exchange repatriation and documentation period.

Canada does not impose a mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government pre-approval API for every invoice. GST/HST registrants must issue tax invoices containing the supplier's BN, invoice date, supplier and recipient names, description, tax rate, and tax amount. Electronic invoices are valid if their integrity and legibility are preserved. The CRA requires electronic filing for GST/HST returns for most registrants and accepts EDI or XML formats through trusted service providers but does not operate an invoice-level clearance regime. Customs filing for export goods is governed by CBSA through the Canadian Single Window and the Automated Commercial Environment, including advance cargo information for air cargo; this is separate from indirect tax invoice clearance.

The CRA may reassess zero-rated GST/HST treatment and deny input tax credits or refunds where the taxpayer fails to substantiate actual export and non-resident receipt of services within the statutory audit window. Penalties and interest apply under the Excise Tax Act and Income Tax Act: gross negligence penalties can be up to 50% of the understated tax, knowingly false statements can attract penalties of 10% plus interest on the overstated refunds, and section 285 ETA penalties of 25% may be imposed on improperly claimed refunds. CRA may also use section 323 ETA director liability to collect unpaid GST/HST from directors of the ULC, and if the ULC is treated as a pass-through or transparent entity, CRA may seek recovery from shareholders or beneficiaries. Criminal prosecution may be pursued for deliberate export zero-rating fraud involving forged export or remittance documents.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility exists for both current and capital account transactions. Non-resident investors may freely remit dividends, interest, royalties, and capital proceeds without exchange-control approval. The principal frictions are Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (subject to treaty relief) and cross-border reporting of transfers of CAD 10,000 or more under PCMLTFA.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory requirement to surrender, convert, or repatriate export proceeds to Canada. Exporters may maintain foreign currency balances domestically or offshore and convert at market rates. Reporting obligations are limited to statistical trade data to Statistics Canada and record-keeping under tax and AML legislation.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BOC) is the monetary authority; the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises federally regulated deposit-taking institutions; FINTRAC administers the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Canada operates no centralized foreign-exchange-control authority; FX transactions are executed through commercial banks under a free-market framework.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility: Low-Medium. A foreign electronic money institution may not operate as a primary Canadian clearing bank without registration as a Money Service Business with FINTRAC. Canadian-dollar settlement on the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) is only accessible to chartered banks and direct clearing participants. EMIs may serve as supplementary rails for low-value payouts but cannot provide escrow, trade finance, or full liquidity clearing for a ULC air cargo charter operation.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Alberta general corporate income tax is 8%, combined with the 15% federal corporate rate for a 23% general combined rate. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may obtain a combined small-business rate of 11% on eligible active business income within the applicable federal and Alberta small-business limits. A non-resident-owned Alberta ULC is generally a taxable Canadian corporation and is not automatically pass-through for Canadian tax purposes.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The Canadian domestic withholding rate on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is generally 25%. A treaty may reduce the rate. Under the Canada-United States treaty, the rate is generally 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying company holding at least 10% of the voting power, and 15% in other treaty-eligible cases. Treaty entitlement requires residence, beneficial ownership, limitation-on-benefits or equivalent anti-abuse compliance, and satisfaction of the applicable treaty conditions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due six months after the end of each taxation year. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end; a qualifying CCPC may generally obtain a three-month balance-due period if the statutory conditions are satisfied. Instalments are due monthly unless a valid quarterly instalment option applies.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which the contemporaneous documentation requirement in Income Tax Act section 247(4) automatically disappears. A taxpayer must prepare or obtain reasonable contemporaneous documentation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions and provide it to the CRA within three months of a written request. CAD 1 million is principally the T106 reporting threshold for aggregate reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents, not a safe harbor from transfer-pricing analysis. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group meeting the consolidated-revenue threshold of EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year, converted under the prescribed rules.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate United Kingdom-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Economic substance is nevertheless relevant through the general anti-avoidance rule, transfer-pricing rules, beneficial-ownership and treaty anti-abuse principles, sham and agency doctrines, foreign accrual property income rules, interest limitation rules, and common-law residence and management-and-control principles. A Canadian ULC must maintain genuine Canadian decision-making, qualified personnel or properly supported functions, commercial records, bank and payment controls, risk assumption consistent with contracts, and evidence that charter-brokerage functions are actually performed. A corporation with only a registered office, nominee directors, circular payments, and no operational capacity faces reassessment, denial of deductions, treaty-rate denial, penalties, and possible recharacterization.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and false tax reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment of tax, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalty generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, and criminal prosecution. Tax evasion on indictment may result in a fine of up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years; summary-conviction consequences also apply under the Income Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions, including payroll and certain non-resident withholding amounts, subject to statutory due-diligence defenses. Personal criminal liability can arise for directing, authorizing, assenting to, or participating in the offence. Civil restitution, asset recovery, and imprisonment are possible; a travel ban is not an automatic tax penalty but may arise from bail, court orders, immigration consequences, or enforcement proceedings.
Failure to remit non-resident withholding tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the full unremitted amount, arrears interest, failure-to-remit penalties, information-return penalties, and gross-negligence penalties. Directors may be assessed personally under the director-liability provisions for source deductions if the corporation failed to remit and statutory conditions are met.

Director personal liability: Personal assessment is possible after statutory collection steps against the corporation, subject to due-diligence defenses. Deliberate withholding fraud may produce criminal charges, restitution orders, seizure, and imprisonment.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust income under section 247, deny or recharacterize expenses, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, deny penalty protection for inadequate documentation, and impose interest and other penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable merely because a transfer-pricing adjustment is made, but personal liability can arise for knowing participation in false records, fraud, tax evasion, or unpaid source deductions. Civil recovery and criminal imprisonment remain possible for intentional misconduct.
Treaty abuse and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: Treaty relief may be denied, withholding may be reassessed at 25%, interest and penalties may apply, and the general anti-avoidance rule or principal-purpose anti-abuse rule may be invoked. False certifications may support criminal prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly approve false treaty documentation may face civil recovery, penalties, prosecution, restitution, and imprisonment. Travel restrictions are fact-specific and normally require a court or enforcement basis.
Shell company and lack of corporate substance

Statutory sanction: Potential denial of deductions, transfer-pricing adjustments, treaty-benefit denial, residence disputes, GAAR reassessment, corporate-record penalties, and anti-money-laundering reporting or enforcement consequences. A shell characterization alone is not a standalone offence, but fraudulent or evasive conduct is actionable.

Director personal liability: Canadian corporate law generally preserves separate legal personality, but courts and regulators may impose personal liability for fraud, oppression, knowing assistance, breach of fiduciary duty, unlawful distributions, or statutory remittance failures. Veil piercing is exceptional and fact-dependent; imprisonment requires an applicable criminal offence.
AML, proceeds of crime, and sanctions violations

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act may impose administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, record-keeping and client-identification obligations, and criminal prosecution. Criminal Code money-laundering offences may carry imprisonment of up to ten years on indictment. Sanctions violations under the Special Economic Measures Act or related legislation can attract substantial fines and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and employees may be prosecuted for knowing participation, counseling, aiding, or directing prohibited conduct. Conviction may result in fines, forfeiture, restitution, imprisonment, and asset or travel restrictions imposed under applicable proceedings.
Sanctions, export-control, and aviation regulatory breach

Statutory sanction: Possible measures include licence suspension, cargo seizure, administrative penalties, customs assessments, denial of permits, corporate fines, and criminal prosecution under Canadian sanctions, customs, export-control, and aviation legislation.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors and officers may face prosecution where they authorized or knowingly permitted the breach. Consequences may include fines, imprisonment, forfeiture, director disqualification under applicable law, and court-ordered restrictions.
GST/HST and payroll non-compliance

Statutory sanction: The CRA may assess unremitted GST/HST, source deductions, interest, late-filing and late-remittance penalties, director-liability assessments, and gross-negligence penalties. Persistent non-compliance may result in prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted payroll source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts after statutory conditions are met, subject to due diligence. Knowing evasion may lead to fines, restitution, seizure, and imprisonment.
Improper corporate distributions and insolvency misconduct

Statutory sanction: Distributions may be voidable or recoverable, directors may face statutory compensation claims, and insolvency, preference, fraudulent-conveyance, and corporate-law remedies may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil restitution, contribution claims, oppression remedies, disqualification, and liability for knowing approval of unlawful distributions. Imprisonment arises only where the conduct also constitutes a criminal offence such as fraud.
