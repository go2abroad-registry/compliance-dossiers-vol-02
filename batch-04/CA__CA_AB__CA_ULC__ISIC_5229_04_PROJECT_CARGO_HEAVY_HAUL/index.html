Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — GST/HST and corporate income tax registration via Business Registration Online (BRO) portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-individuals/account-registration-online.html); Alberta provincial corporate income tax is administered by CRA under a tax collection agreement (Alberta Corporate Tax Act, RSA 2000, c A-15).

E-government filing portal

Non-resident entities carrying on business in Canada or making taxable supplies in Canada must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) with a Goods and Services Tax/Harmonized Sales Tax (GST/HST) program account and, if applicable, a corporate income tax program account. Step 1: Determine if the non-resident is 'carrying on business' in Alberta and whether GST/HST registration is mandatory (annual worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000). Step 2: Apply for a BN via CRA's BRO portal or Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number), providing entity legal name, jurisdiction of incorporation, directors' information, and a Canadian contact or authorized representative. Step 3: If the entity is an 'Unlimited Liability Corporation' (ULC), CRA will assign the same BN with applicable program accounts, and additional provincial program accounts (e.g., WCB Alberta) may be required separately. Step 4: Retain Canadian customs client identifier (CCI) for import/export purposes where required. Alternative: file Form RC459 (GST/HST Application to Register) for non-resident registrants; processing typically 5–10 business days for non-resident entities with properly completed documentation. Non-residents that are not carrying on business may voluntarily register for GST/HST to claim input tax credits (ITCs), subject to CRA discretion and security deposit requirements.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — The Alberta Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) is a Canadian legal entity that is classified as a corporation for Canadian federal and provincial income tax purposes (Income Tax Act, RSC 1985, c 1 (5th Supp), s 89(1), definition of 'corporation'). Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — The Alberta Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) is a Canadian legal entity that is classified as a corporation for Canadian federal and provincial income tax purposes (Income Tax Act, RSC 1985, c 1 (5th Supp), s 89(1), definition of 'corporation'). However, by virtue of the ULC's unlimited liability structure, where the ULC is resident in Canada and each of its members is a US resident for the purposes of the Canada–US Tax Convention (1980, as amended), the ULC can elect (or be deemed) to be treated as a 'fiscally transparent' entity under Article IV(6) of the Convention. For US federal tax purposes, the ULC is eligible to 'check the box' to be treated as a partnership or disregarded entity (Treas. Reg. § 301.7701-3); this results in hybrid entity treatment: a Canadian tax resident corporation for domestic law purposes but a pass-through for US treaty purposes. Territorial scope: Canada taxes Canadian-resident corporations on worldwide income (residence-based); non-resident ULC shareholders are not subject to Canadian taxation on their share of income unless the ULC is not Canadian-resident (rare) or they receive dividends from the ULC. Specific Alberta rates and rules: see 'statutory_election_mechanisms' below for a detailed breakdown.

Available election forms

1. Check-the-Box Election (US): A ULC is a per se foreign corporation under Treas. Reg. § 301.7701-2(b)(8), but because the ULC is an eligible entity under Treas. Reg. § 301.7701-2(b)(8)(i) (an entity with limited liability of all members), a US shareholder can elect to treat the ULC as a partnership or disregarded entity by filing IRS Form 8832 (Entity Classification Election). This election must be filed within 75 days of formation or acquisition. 2. Canada–US Tax Convention Article IV(6) Election: The ULC (or its members) may file an election with CRA (Form RC4644, or an accepted alternative in writing) to be treated as a fiscally transparent entity under the Convention, enabling US members to be generally treated as carrying on business in Canada directly for treaty purposes. This election is used together with the US check-the-box election to avoid double taxation via the 'flow-through' treatment of Canadian-source business income. 3. Section 216(4) Election (Income Tax Act): A non-resident earning rental or timber royalties through a Canadian ULC may elect to file a return on a net basis; not typical for OOG cargo logistics but available. 4. Section 219 Election (Canadian non-resident-owned investment corporation, NRO): Not applicable to active logistics operations; mentioned for completeness. 5. GST/HST Joint Election (67/33 election) — non-resident partner / branch registration election: Available to a non-resident that is registered for GST/HST and is a member of a qualifying partnership; not a ULC-specific election. 6. ULC Dividend 'Deemed Dividend' Election: Under s 84(1) of the Income Tax Act, capital distributions can be elected to be treated as dividends; in a ULC structure, this can be used to achieve tax-efficient repatriation of funds to US parent shareholders, subject to treaty withholding rates.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: GST registration is mandatory within 30 days of the calendar quarter in which worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated and taxable supplies) exceed this threshold, applied to 'carrying on business' in Canada. For non-resident project logistics providers, this threshold applies to all taxable supplies made in Canada during any calendar quarter, not just Alberta supplies. Supplies made to Canadians are deemed made in Canada for GST purposes if performed in Canada (s 142, Excise Tax Act, RSC 1985, c E-15). Even below threshold, a non-resident may voluntarily register; a security deposit (typically CAD 5,000 to CAD 25,000) may be required from non-resident registrants before approval (Excise Tax Act, s 241(3.1)).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of goods exported from Canada are zero-rated under section 1 of Part V, Schedule VI, Excise Tax Act. Criteria: (a) tangible personal property (TPP) is exported by the supplier or for the supplier's benefit in the course of a commercial activity; (b) the TPP is exported 'as soon after the supply is made as is reasonable having regard to the circumstances' (s 1(b)); (c) the exporter keeps proper records evidencing export (physical export within 60 days, or within 'reasonable time' if delayed by force majeure); (d) the recipient is a non-resident (for certain drop-shipment exceptions) or contract requires export. Services: zero-rating of advisory/project management services supplied to non-residents requires the service to be for a 'recipient who is not resident in Canada', not for consumption in Canada, and performed wholly outside Canada (or in Canada but clearly linked to an export good). For OOG heavy-haul logistics: ground transport service between points in Canada and a port for subsequent export is zero-rated only if the transport is provided in conjunction with the export and is contractually arranged by the exporter (supply of transport service is zero-rated under s 7, Part VII, Schedule VI — but only for 'transportation of goods to or from Canada' or international through transport; the domestic haulageto a Canadian port by a domestic carrier to an export destination does NOT automatically qualify if the carrier has no direct contract with the exporter). Zero-rating applies to (i) freight transportation services that begin or end in Canada (when part of an international shipment), (ii) services, including heavy lift and rigging, but only if the service is supplied to a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST (or to a registered non-resident where the service is in respect of goods not in Canada at the time the service is performed, and the goods will not be delivered in Canada). Key nuance for heavy-haul/project cargo: if a Canadian ULC provides heavy-haul services to a non-resident owner of out-of-gauge equipment (e.g., a US pipeline contractor) while the equipment is physically in Canada en route to the port, the supply is deemed made in Canada and taxable at 5% unless the non-resident recipient is registered for GST/HST and the supply is 'zero-rated under Part V or VII' — this requires the ULC to have evidence that the goods were exported within a reasonable time (s 144.1 and s 142.1 ETA). Legal compliance: the ULC must retain evidence of a contract with the non-resident owner, a bill of lading, and proof that the service was performed solely for export.

  • Customs Export Declaration: CCI (Canadian Customs Invoice) — Form A6A or CIID (Canada Border Services Agency Export Declaration) filed electronically via the CARM Client Portal or as a fully completed Form B13 (required when the value of goods exceeds CAD 2,000 or falls under specific control list requirements)
  • Bill of Lading: Original or electronic negotiable/non-negotiable ocean or air waybill evidencing the out-of-gauge cargo was loaded on a vessel/aircraft for export
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / Foreign Exchange Record: SWIFT MT103 or bank remittance statement evidencing foreign currency payment was received from a non-resident recipient, and if applicable, a bank confirmation letter for export proceeds
  • Proof of Export Certificate (CBSA Form A6A endorsement or customs broker's certificate of shipment)
  • Export Permit (in case the cargo is on the Export Control List — rare for OOG project cargo unless military-grade or controlled items)
  • Carrier's / trucker's proof of destination: police certification, port terminal receipts, and marine terminal origin/outturn weights
  • Cargo Manifest / Waybill with explicit foreign destination
  • Service Contract with non-resident client stating the cargo is for export and identifying ultimate foreign destination and consignee
  • If zero-rating relies on the 'non-resident recipient' rule: a signed GST/HST declaration (Form RC459 or similar) of non-residency
  • Certificate of Origin (only if required for the destination country, not for Canadian GST zero-rating purpose)

CRA does not mandate a government-run real-time electronic invoicing clearance platform; standard commercial e-invoicing (e.g., PDF or EDI) is accepted. However, since April 1, 2023, CRA has required all GST/HST registrants to issue electronic payment requests / invoices via direct deposit (mandatory e-pay for certain large businesses) and has significantly increased e-audit capabilities. A 'fiscal clearance' regime is not in force: Canada relies on traditional invoice documentation and CRA post-filing audits. For export transactions, electronic customs clearance via the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) system is mandatory (projected full implementation for all modes by 2025–2026). For goods exporting, the exporter must transmit an export declaration electronically via the Canadian Integrated Import Declaration (CIID) or via a licensed customs broker. No digital stamping or real-time fiscal invoice encryption exists in the Canadian GST/HST system. Entities using accounting systems like SAP Ariba or Oracle ERP can submit electronic invoice data directly. The successful G7/G20 interoperability standard (Peppol) is being gradually adopted in Canada for public-sector procurement e-invoicing, but remains voluntary for private B2B.

CRA audits export zero-rating claims rigorously. Penalties include: (1) Reassessment of tax plus interest at prescribed rates (Excise Tax Act s 280) from the date the amount should have been remitted; (2) A penalty of 25% of the net tax remitted if the registrant 'knowingly, or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence, participated in the making of a false statement or omission' in the return (ETA s 285); (3) The imposition of a 6% penalty and 6% interest (per annum compound) for failure to remit collected GST/HST (ETA s 280.1); (4) A dollar-for-dollar recapture of input tax credits erroneously claimed. IF a ULC claimed a zero-rating on an export that did not physically leave Canada within the statutory period (or where the exporter misrepresented the export), the full GST becomes payable by the ULC, plus a 25% gross-negligence penalty on the unreported tax, and interest from the due date. In addition to monetary penalties: Section 327.1 ETA imposes a 5% penalty on a registrant who fails to file an information return (including export document listing) in the prescribed manner; Sections 329–330 provide for criminal prosecution with fines up to CAD 250,000 and/or imprisonment up to 2 years if a person 'wilfully evades payment' through fraudulent export documentation. The CRA, under s 296, may issue a 10-year limitation period for a reassessment if the exporter made a misrepresentation that is attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default, or committed fraud.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

CAD is fully convertible; Canada imposes no exchange controls on current or capital account transactions and no currency licensing, retention, or repatriation quotas. The only remittance hurdle is tax-based: ULC dividends, interest, rents, and royalties to non-residents are subject to Part XIII withholding tax (25% statutory rate, reduced to 0-15% under the Canada-United States Income Tax Convention where the ULC is treaty-eligible or treated as a fiscally transparent hybrid under Article IV(6)). Cross-border non-arm's length transactions must be reported on CRA Form T106, and acquisitions above the WTO threshold under the Investment Canada Act require notification; a project-cargo ULC with no material share acquisition sits below the review threshold.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada maintains no statutory surrender requirement, mandatory conversion ratio, or forex surrender timeline for export receipts; exporters may hold and disburse CAD, USD, or any third currency freely. Every electronic funds transfer of CAD 10,000 or more is reportable to FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, and the ULC must retain export and project documentation (CBSA export declarations, bills of lading, invoices, delivery receipts) for five years to substantiate the underlying commercial transaction.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); prudential banking supervision by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); wholesale payment clearing operated by Payments Canada via the Lynx large-value transfer system.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No statutory local-file/master-file threshold is currently codified in Canada. CRA's contemporaneous documentation obligation under subsection 247(4) applies to all cross-border transactions with non-resident related parties; multinational groups with consolidated annual revenue of CAD 750 million or more must file a Country-by-Country Report and are expected to maintain OECD-aligned master file and local file documentation.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR), but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245 of the Income Tax Act) denies tax benefits from transactions lacking a credible commercial purpose or economic substance. An Alberta ULC is taxable as a corporation at the federal level; the unlimited liability of shareholders alone does not confer fiscal transparency unless the provincial ULC election is separately made and accepted.
