E-government filing portal

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to (i) export of tangible goods under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 1 where the supplier maintains proof of export; (ii) international freight transportation services and associated DG-forwarding/arranging services under Schedule VI, Part V, ss. 20 and 24 when the continuous freight journey originates or terminates outside Canada (including Canada-US legs evidenced by through bills of lading); (iii) services supplied to a non-resident that are not performed wholly in Canada and are not tied to Canadian realty/goods, under s. 7; (iv) DG shipments must also comply with Transport Canada TDG Regulations, IATA DGR, and IMDG Code documentation so that the zero-rating claim is not vitiated by unlicensed transport. The supplier must hold 'evidence satisfactory to the Minister' of export within two years of the transaction for ITC preservation.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for ordinary current-account and capital-account transactions. There is generally no statutory requirement to surrender foreign currency, convert export proceeds, obtain prior exchange-control approval, or maintain capital controls for ordinary commercial payments. A Canadian ULC is ordinarily a Canadian-resident corporation for Canadian legal and tax purposes unless specific treaty, residence, or hybrid-entity facts produce a different result. Foreign ownership and non-resident shareholder funding are generally permitted, subject to corporate, tax, sanctions, beneficial-ownership, AML, transfer-pricing, withholding-tax, and sector-specific requirements. Dividends, interest, royalties, service fees, loan repayments, and return of capital may generally be remitted offshore after corporate authorization, accounting support, applicable withholding-tax analysis, and settlement of Canadian tax obligations. Non-resident remittances may be subject to Canadian withholding tax, commonly 25 percent before treaty relief, with reduced rates potentially available under an applicable tax treaty and properly documented beneficial ownership. Banks may require evidence of source of funds, transaction purpose, invoices, contracts, tax residency, treaty forms, and board approvals. Capital-account transfers can be delayed or rejected for sanctions, suspicious-transaction, incomplete beneficial-ownership, export-control, or high-risk dangerous-goods concerns even though no general exchange-control approval is required.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no general statutory export-proceeds surrender rule, mandatory conversion ratio, or universal deadline requiring export receipts to be converted into Canadian dollars. Export proceeds may normally remain in foreign-currency accounts or be converted at the company's commercial discretion. The company must retain commercially reasonable records supporting invoices, bills of lading, customs documentation, contracts, receipts, and intercompany pricing. Exporters of controlled, restricted, or sanctioned goods may require permits, reporting, end-use controls, or export declarations through applicable federal systems. The bank may impose contractual settlement deadlines, documentary requirements, enhanced monitoring, or transaction-specific conversion conditions under its risk policy. Any foreign-currency borrowing, deposit, derivative, or intercompany arrangement must be reviewed for Canadian income-tax, thin-capitalization, transfer-pricing, withholding-tax, accounting, and derivatives implications.

Central bank supervision: The Bank of Canada is Canada's central bank and monetary authority. Canada has no separate foreign exchange administration imposing general exchange controls. The Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) administers anti-money laundering and terrorist financing obligations for reporting entities, including banks and certain money services businesses. The Canada Border Services Agency, the Canada Revenue Agency, Global Affairs Canada, Transport Canada, provincial authorities, and the Alberta Securities Commission may regulate related trade, tax, sanctions, securities, derivatives, and hazardous-material activities.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for supplementary collections, foreign-currency wallets, expense management, and payment initiation; Low to Medium for core banking, hazardous-goods trade finance, and large-value cross-border settlement. An offshore electronic-money institution cannot automatically provide Canadian deposit-taking or payment services to a Canadian ULC. The provider must operate through a legally permitted Canadian structure or exemption, use a Canadian bank or regulated payment partner, satisfy Canadian AML, sanctions, privacy, consumer-protection, safeguarding, and payment-regulatory requirements, and accept bank-specific restrictions on high-risk goods. Payment service providers may be subject to the Retail Payment Activities Act framework and FINTRAC obligations, depending on their activities and implementation status. Offshore EMI accounts should not be treated as substitutes for Canadian operating accounts, documentary trade-finance facilities, or segregated client-money accounts. The ULC must verify safeguarding, insolvency treatment, settlement finality, account ownership, Canadian reporting, data residency, and termination rights before use.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route equity through a Canadian bank account in the exact legal name of the Alberta ULC using a regulated correspondent-bank wire. The remittance should identify the ULC, remitter, beneficial owner, currency, amount, and purpose as paid-up capital, capital contribution, or shareholder loan, as applicable. Use the bank's designated purpose narrative rather than inventing a statutory Canadian FX purpose code; Canada generally does not use a universal mandatory FX purpose-code regime for ordinary corporate wires. Before receipt, provide the bank with the subscription agreement or loan agreement, board and shareholder approvals, ownership chart, source-of-funds evidence, tax-residency information, and expected funds-flow statement. Record the receipt in the corporate ledger, issue shares or book the shareholder loan consistently with the documents, reconcile the SWIFT reference, and retain bank confirmations. For equity, assess paid-up capital, stated capital, return-of-capital restrictions, solvency requirements, and any applicable corporate-law approvals. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate fair market value of controlled transactions with non-arm's-length non-residents; Master File: CAD 50,000,000 consolidated group revenue threshold; Country-by-Country Report: CAD 1,000,000,000 consolidated group revenue threshold.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply the offshore Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) that exist in certain Caribbean/Middle East regimes. Instead, Canada relies on the Income Tax Act s. 245 General Anti-Avoidance Rule, the DTA limitation-on-benefits provisions, and CRA administrative policies that require genuine commercial activity, Canadian board involvement, and actual operational capacity before treaty benefits or intercompany deduction claims will be accepted. A 'thin' Alberta ULC with no physical office or employee substance will be challenged.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Declarations

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s. 239(1): on summary conviction, a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and/or imprisonment up to 2 years; on indictment, a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years. The CRA also imposes civil gross-negligence penalties under s. 163(2) equal to 50% of the understated tax, plus arrears interest and penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false T2 returns, instruct underreporting, or knowingly participate in the fraud face criminal prosecution, restitution orders, CRA collection enforcement against personal assets, and court-imposed travel restrictions while charges are pending. For an Alberta ULC, shareholders are already exposed to unlimited liability for the corporation's obligations, including CRA liabilities, which substantially erodes any corporate veil protection.
Shell Company / Artificial Arrangements

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply the GAAR under ITA s. 245, disregard the entity as an agency or sham, deny treaty relief, recharacterize the payments, reassess 25% Part XIII withholding on diverted funds, and assess the 50% gross-negligence penalty. Alberta corporate registry may strike the ULC for non-compliance, freezing its bank accounts.

Director personal liability: Directors can be jointly and severally liable for unpaid trust amounts (source deductions, GST/HST) under ITA s. 227.1, and seek restitution of diverted corporate assets. Courts may pierce the veil when the ULC is a mere agent or corporation de facto, exposing directors and shareholders to personal judgment. An Alberta ULC's unlimited liability feature means shareholders have no limited-liability shield in any case.
Transfer Pricing Non-Disclosure / Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s. 247(3) imposes a 10% penalty on the net upward transfer-pricing adjustment when the adjustment exceeds the stated threshold and no contemporaneous documentation exists. The CRA may also disallow deductions for non-arm's-length payments, recharacterize intercompany loans as equity, and impose Part XIII withholding on deemed dividends or interest at 25%.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve or sign a T2 return without making reasonable inquiries into transfer pricing may be prosecuted for willful blindness or false statements. Personal civil liability can arise for unpaid withholding tax under s. 227.1, and related-party transaction rules in s. 160 can attach assets held by the director or the ULC, subjecting those assets to CRA garnishment and seizure.
AML / Trade-Based Money Laundering / Sanctions Breach

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act enforcement: administrative penalties up to CAD 500,000 per non-compliance and criminal prosecution on indictment; the United Nations Act and Special Economic Measures Act impose criminal sanctions, including imprisonment for up to 10 years for facilitation of transactions with designated persons or entities. Transport Canada may also suspend DG licenses and impose violation penalties under the TDG Regulations.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers can be criminally prosecuted for authorizing, condoning, or failing to prevent the laundering or sanctions breach. Courts may impose personal fines, confiscation orders, freezing orders, and travel bans. Because the DG forwarding activity has an inherently high-risk AML tier, directors must maintain a compliance program or face unlimited personal exposure.
