Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) - International and Large Business Directorate for cross-border matters; primary digital portals are CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses/business-account.html) and Business Registration Online (BRO). Alberta provincial corporate income tax is assessed and collected by the CRA on behalf of Alberta under the Alberta Corporate Tax Act; no separate provincial corporate tax registration portal exists. Alberta corporate registry formalities (Certificate of Incorporation, registered office) are handled through the Alberta Corporate Registry / Registry Information System.

E-government filing portal

1) Incorporate the Alberta Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) under the Business Corporations Act (Alberta) and obtain the Certificate of Incorporation, articles, and registered-office particulars. 2) Apply to the CRA for the 9-digit Business Number (BN) as the universal tax identifier, using Form RC1 (Application for a Business Number) or Business Registration Online; select the required program accounts: RT (GST/HST), RC (T2 corporate income tax), RP (payroll, if employees are resident in Canada), and RD (Part XIII non-resident withholding). Non-resident directors must authorize a Canadian representative via Form RC59 and, if applicable, file Form RC459 for a foreign-based representative. 3) Voluntarily register for GST/HST at incorporation even if the C$30,000 small-supplier threshold is not yet met, so that zero-rated export supplies and input tax credits (ITCs) on freight and GDP validation inputs can be claimed from inception; Form GST494 may be used to elect an annual filing period where eligible. 4) Activate the CRA My Business Account and, for a ULC with U.S. shareholders, ensure the account reflects the Canadian-resident corporation status (ITA s. 250(4)) and the RT program account for 5% GST. 5) File the T2 corporate income tax return annually (due six months after fiscal year-end) and remit net GST/HST by the statutory due date (one month after the reporting period for monthly/quarterly filers, three months for annual filers).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (Canadian domestic income tax: an Alberta ULC is a corporation resident in Canada for purposes of ITA s. 250(4) and is subject to Part I corporate income tax at the entity level; unlimited shareholder liability does not create partnership, trust, or other pass-through status under the Income Tax Act. The 'Pass_Through_Eligible' classification in the composite key refers solely to U.S. federal check-the-box eligibility under IRC s. 7701 / Form 8832, which is not recognized for Canadian domestic tax purposes.)

Available election forms

No Canadian 'check-the-box' or hybrid party-election regime exists; the ULC is irrevocably a corporation for Canadian tax. Applicable statutory elections and mechanisms include: (i) U.S. check-the-box election under IRC s. 7701 / Treasury Regulation s. 301.7701-3 (Form 8832) to treat the ULC as a disregarded entity or partnership for U.S. federal tax purposes, subject to hybrid-mismatch limitations in ITA ss. 18.6-18.7 and Articles 4(7) and XXIX A of the Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty; (ii) GST/HST section 156 election to treat intercompany taxable supplies between closely related corporations as nil-rated; (iii) ETA section 211 election for cross-border services between related registrants where zero-rating is uncertain; (iv) ITA section 83(2) election to pay a tax-free capital dividend from the capital dividend account (CDA); (v) ITA section 85 rollover elections when incorporating freight-forwarding and GDP logistics assets; and (vi) treaty-based elections to reduce Part XIII withholding rates applicable to dividends or royalties paid to U.S. shareholders.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000 - the small-supplier threshold under ETA s. 148, measured by the total value of taxable supplies made in Canada in the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters or in a single calendar quarter. Once exceeded, mandatory GST/HST registration applies. Freight-arranging, freight-forwarding, and GDP-pharma transportation services are taxable supplies for GST purposes and count toward this threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to: (i) supplies of tangible personal property exported from Canada, under ETA s. 23 and Schedule VI, Part I, where the supplier retains contemporaneous proof of export; (ii) supplies of freight transportation services for goods transported from a place in Canada to a place outside Canada, under Schedule VI, Part V; (iii) supplies of freight-arranging and forwarding services in respect of those exported goods to a non-resident recipient, under Schedule VI, Part V; and (iv) services performed wholly outside Canada and supplied to a non-resident, provided they are not in connection with real property or goods situated in Canada at the time of performance. GDP-pharma freight validation services qualify for 0% GST only where the validation is performed in respect of goods being exported from Canada or where the entire service is rendered to a non-resident for wholly offshore movement; otherwise, the supply is standard-rated at 5%. The ULC must be registered at the time of supply, report the zero-rated supply on the GST/HST return, and claim corresponding input tax credits in the same return; any net GST refund is recovered through the periodic filer mechanism, not a separate refund application.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A / Canadian Export Declaration System or CBSA Form A6) evidencing physical export from Canada
  • Bill of Lading (master and house, or air waybill) showing Canadian origin and foreign destination, endorsed by the carrier
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank statement confirming settlement of the foreign-currency freight invoice within 180 days
  • Commercial invoice and fully executed freight-arranging contract naming the non-resident principal
  • Cargo manifest and GDP temperature-monitoring dataloggers evidencing pharmaceutical chain-of-custody
  • Destination-country import authorizations or medical-device export permits where required for GDP pharma freight

No mandatory continuous transaction control (CTC), real-time fiscal clearance, API-based invoice clearance, or digital stamping regime exists in Canada. Registered suppliers must issue tax invoices containing prescribed particulars under ETA s. 223 and retain all paper or electronic records for a minimum of six years under ETA s. 286. The CRA operates a voluntary Pan-Canadian e-invoicing framework aligned with the Peppol PINT specification (e.g., PBC e-invoicing), which is optional. Mandatory electronic filing of GST/HST returns applies to larger registrants (gross annual revenue above C$1.5M) via GST/HST NETFILE or EDI, with remittances by direct deposit.

CRA GST/HST auditors test export substantiation against the ETA s. 286 six-year record-retention obligation; missing bills of lading, customs export declarations, or treasury settlement records cause reassessment of the gross freight invoices at the standard 5% rate, with compound daily interest under ETA s. 280 and clawback of all input tax credits associated with the invalidated zero-rating. A gross negligence penalty under ETA s. 285 is imposed at 25% of the overstated rebate or credit; false statements or omissions attract additional penalties of C$250 to C$25,000 and extend the default four-year reassessment window under ETA s. 298. Intentional evasion is an indictable offence under ETA s. 327(1)(a), punishable by fines of 50%-200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years. In the GDP-pharma freight context, falsified temperature-chain logbooks or export manifests additionally trigger Health Canada enforcement under the Food and Drugs Act, including suspension or revocation of the Drug Establishment Licence and loss of GDP certification.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible under both current and capital account transactions. The Foreign Exchange Control Act has been repealed; no administrative approval is required for dividend remittances, repayment of intercompany loans, or repatriation of invested capital. Profit remittance is subject to Part XIII Non-Resident Withholding Tax and the corporate solvency tests under Alberta Business Corporations Act (respectively the ULC's governing legislation).

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory requirement to surrender export proceeds to the government or any prescribed timeline for conversion into CAD. Canadian entities may retain indefinitely earnings in foreign currency, either onshore in a foreign currency bank account or offshore, subject to FINTRAC reporting for international transfers of C$10,000 or more and to Form T1135 filing for specified foreign property exceeding C$100,000.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (central bank and monetary authority); Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre (FINTRAC) executes cross-border transaction surveillance; no dedicated foreign exchange control administration exists.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low feasibility as a primary treasury solution. Canada has no electronic money institution licensure; foreign EMIs cannot open deposit accounts or provide Canadian clearing rail access directly. They may operate as money services businesses registered with FINTRAC to facilitate FX conversion and international remittance, but all CAD settlement must ultimately route through a Canadian chartered bank or a bank-sponsored payment service provider. Legal constraints: no deposit-taking, mandatory FINTRAC registration, and OSFI-regulated sponsorship required.

Use SWIFT MT103 from the foreign parent/investor to the ULC's CAD-denominated operating account at the designated Canadian Schedule I bank. Domestic Canadian disbursements are executed using Payment Canada EFT/ACH with direct deposit capabilities. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined federal (15%) and Alberta provincial (8%) general corporate income tax rate for active business income; lower rates apply to Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) on the first C$500,000 of active business income, but foreign-controlled and non-resident-owned corporations generally do not qualify for the CCPC small business deduction.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder. Dividend payments from the ULC to its non-resident parent are subject to 25% unless reduced by an applicable tax treaty; under the Canada-U.S. tax treaty, portfolio dividends are 15%, and dividends to a U.S. corporation that owns at least 10% of the voting stock of the Canadian payer are 5%. For other treaty partners, rates generally range from 5% (10% substantial shareholding) to 15%.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 (CAD) for gross revenue in the taxation year triggering the requirement to prepare a complete transfer pricing documentation file (Local File / comparable analysis) if the corporation enters into transactions with non-residents with whom it does not deal at arm's length. Under paragraph 247(4), the documentation must be prepared by the taxpayer's filing-due date but only needs to be provided to CRA within three months of a formal demand. Master File and Local File per the OECD-aligned CRA guidance (Information Circular IC 87-2R) are required for transactions exceeding the 'specified transaction' threshold of C$1,000,000; the Canadian TP documentation standard also extends to a Country-by-Country Report (CbCR) for multinational groups with consolidated group revenue over C$1.18 billion (2022 threshold, indexed annually to C$1.2 billion in 2025). For a ULC in scope ISIC 5229 with clinical pharma freight, the practical functional profile will be a low-risk logistics service provider (routine distributor/freight forwarder) earning a routine service margin under the Transactional Net Margin Method (TNMM).

Economic substance scope: Alberta does not maintain a separate Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime like UAE/Cayman; however, Canadian tax law applies the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section 245, the transfer pricing rules under section 247, and the 'objective economic substance' doctrine to disallow tax benefits from arrangements lacking real business purpose. For an Alberta ULC, CRA will assess whether the ULC has actual substance: physical premises, employees, bank accounts, board decisions in Canada, maintenance of books/records in Alberta, and income-producing activities. A ULC created predominantly to access beneficial tax characterization under foreign law (e.g., US check-the-box hybrid) while lacking economic substance in Alberta will attract GAAR, sham argument, and potential recharacterization of dividends as royalties or management fees. Additionally, Canada's anti-hybrid rules in sections 18.2/18.3 and the December 2022 final hybrid mismatch legislation may deny deductions for payments made by a ULC that are not included in income of the U.S. shareholder by reason of its disregarded entity status, if the arrangement results in double non-taxation. The Canada Revenue Agency's transfer pricing adjustment powers permit full recharacterization of transactions that lack commercial rationality, including disregarding the entity for tax purposes in egregious cases.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under subsections 239(1) and 239(1.1) of the Income Tax Act: on summary conviction, a fine of 50% to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to 2 years; on indictment, a fine of 100% to 250% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to 5 years. Civil penalties include gross negligence penalty of the greater of C$100 and 50% of the understated tax, plus arrears interest compounded daily. CRA may also levy third-party civil penalties under section 163.2 for promoter/planner penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted withholding taxes and GST/HST under section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act and Alberta provincial equivalents. For a ULC, unlimited liability attaches to shareholders as a matter of corporate law; both directors and shareholders may face personal civil restitution orders in a CRA collection action. Criminal tax fraud can result in personal fines, restraining orders, travel bans via court order, and imprisonment. CRA may obtain a jeopardy order to freeze personal assets of directors. Extradition may apply for cross-border tax fraud between Canada and the shareholder's treaty partner home country.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation / Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing adjustment under section 247(2) with recharacterization and computing the arm's length price. Consequential penalties under subsection 247(3): if the net transfer pricing adjustment exceeds the lesser of 10% of gross revenue and C$5,000,000, the taxpayer is liable for a penalty of 10% of the adjustment amount. Interest and gross negligence penalties may stack. GAAR under section 245 may alternatively deny the tax benefit entirely with a 25% avoidance penalty under subsection 245(4).

Director personal liability: Directors can be held personally liable if they signed false tax filings or knowingly participated in the transfer pricing scheme. While section 247 penalties are assessed against the corporate ULC, the unlimited liability feature permits a judgment creditor (CRA) to recover from each shareholder personally without any cap, piercing the corporate veil. Directors who approve or implement sham intercompany contracts may face civil liability for breach of fiduciary duty and a bankruptcy order. In egregious cross-border schemes, criminal tax evasion charges can be laid against the controlling officer/shareholder, attracting imprisonment up to 5 years.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply GAAR (section 245) to reattribute the income to the non-resident, deny treaty benefits under the Limitation of Benefits (LOB) provisions in the Canada-U.S. treaty Article XXIX A, and reassess the ULC on the basis it is a sham or a fiscal nullity. The CRA may also audit the ULC under the OECD Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) Action 6 test and issue a notice of assessment with penalties. Alberta Corporations Canada may administratively dissolve the ULC for failure to file annual returns; dissolution results in forfeiture of assets to the Crown and personal liability of former directors for taxes during the wind-up.

Director personal liability: Because a ULC is an unlimited liability company, shareholders (usually a U.S. parent LLC) face unlimited personal liability for the corporation's debts, including CRA assessments and any third-party logistics liabilities. Directors similarly owe fiduciary duties; if they failed to ensure economic substance in Canada, they are liable to the estate for misrepresentation. A court may impose a personal compliance order requiring the shareholder to file Canadian tax returns. Repeat or egregious avoidance can support a finding of fraudulent conveyance under provincial fraudulent preferences legislation, leading to civil asset recovery, freezing orders, and a travel ban if the individual attempts to leave Canada with a compliance debt exceeding thresholds.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Non-Resident Tax (Part XIII)

Statutory sanction: The ULC is liable for the full gross amount of Part XIII tax under subsection 215(1), even if not withheld, plus interest and penalties (late filing/non-filing penalty 5% to 25%). Directors are personally liable under section 227.1 for failure to withhold and remit, and CRA has priority in bankruptcy. There is no limitation period for collecting withheld source deductions. Failure to file NR4 information returns incurs penalties under section 162(7).

Director personal liability: Directors and each unlimited shareholder of the ULC are jointly and severally liable for the unremitted withholding tax. CRA can freeze personal bank accounts, garnish wages, and institute civil suits against the ULC owners without corporate shield. The Canada Revenue Agency has a statutory deemed trust over any property held by the corporation and can trace into shareholder assets. Personal liability may extend to the full amount of the dividend or payment, plus interest, for each director/shareholder; imprisonment is possible only for criminal tax evasion, but civil travel bans can be issued where tax debts exceed C$50,000 and the individual attempts to depart Canada without settlement.
AML / Money Laundering / Sanctions Violations

Statutory sanction: As a non-financial business, a freight forwarder may be a reporting entity under PCMLTFA if it engages in money services business; otherwise the entity is required to comply with FINTRAC reporting only if it falls under specified categories. Nonetheless, failing to have an AML program, making false statements to FINTRAC, or knowingly laundering proceeds attracts criminal penalties: up to C$500,000 fine and/or 5 years imprisonment for directors/officers; corporate fines up to C$1,000,000 or more. Canada's sanctions laws (the Special Economic Measures Act and the United Nations Act) impose absolute liability penalties for conducting prohibited transactions with designated states, with fines up to C$25,000 per violation for corporations and imprisonment of directors for up to 10 years, plus asset freezing under RCMP/CBSA enforcement. The Canada Border Services Agency can suspend or revoke the business import/export license and seize cargo.

Director personal liability: Directors and unlimited shareholders of a ULC cannot hide behind the corporate veil for violations of the Criminal Code under section 462.31 (laundering proceeds). Each person who directs, authorizes, or assents to the offence is a party to the offence and liable to imprisonment. FINTRAC can issue administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) against individuals and corporations, ranging from C$500 to C$100,000+ per violation. Courts may issue restraint/forfeiture orders against personal and corporate property, and a conviction for laundering triggers mandatory travel restrictions for persons over C$1,000,000 judgments. For a ULC, the unlimited liability design makes all shareholders fully exposed to AML penalties, restitution, and legal fees.
M&A / Dividend Stripping / Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Recharacterization of the share redemption proceeds as a dividend subject to Part XIII withholding tax; subsection 55(2) applies the gross-up and refundable tax rules to deem capital gains as dividends; section 84.1 converts the capital gain to a deemed dividend where the payment is to a non-resident parent. The anti-avoidance rule under section 245 can disallow all treaty benefits and may impose a 25% penalty. Provincial and federal tax reassessments with interest can extend to all prior open years (3-year normal reassessment period, extended to unlimited in cases of misrepresentation).

Director personal liability: For a ULC, the unlimited liability of shareholders permits CRA to recover the full reassessed tax plus interest from every shareholder. Directors who executed the dividend-stripping transaction are personally liable for the amount under section 227.1 if it was a withholding obligation; otherwise, they face civil liability for breach of fiduciary duty to the corporation and other shareholders. If the transaction is criminal tax evasion (e.g., false valuations, backdated documents), directors are subject to imprisonment up to 5 years, large fines, and prohibition orders against acting as a director of any Canadian corporation. The court may also issue an international travel ban to secure attendance at sentencing.
