Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); registration portal: CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) and My Business Account (MyBA); Alberta corporate income tax is administered and collected by the CRA on behalf of Alberta under the federal-provincial Tax Collection Agreement; provincial incorporation and corporate registry filings are processed by Alberta Registries (Corporate Registry).

E-government filing portal

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible (domestic Income Tax Act treatment nonetheless imposes entity-level corporate taxation: federal 15% general corporate rate plus Alberta 8% provincial corporate rate, filed on the T2 corporate return; pass-through eligibility is recognized for foreign members under the Canada-US tax treaty, the MLI fiscally-transparent entity rule, and US check-the-box flow-through classification of an unlimited liability corporation)

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a US-style check-the-box election for domestic purposes; a ULC remains an entity-level taxpayer under the Income Tax Act. Available statutory and treaty elections include: (i) a US federal check-the-box election treating the CA/AB ULC as a disregarded entity or partnership for US tax purposes, with corresponding Canada-US treaty flow-through protection for ULC members; (ii) joint election under Excise Tax Act s. 156 for GST/HST treatment of closely related corporations and partnerships; (iii) Income Tax Act s. 216 election enabling a non-resident corporation to be taxed on net Canadian-source rental or timber income; (iv) s. 219 election for non-resident corporations and s. 115.1 elective taxable-income filings; and (v) documented hybrid-mismatch-neutralization for cross-border royalty and master-franchise flows. Alberta has no separate provincial check-the-box or additional unlimited-liability election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD $30,000 in total taxable supplies (including zero-rated exports) in the current calendar quarter or across the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters, per the Excise Tax Act small-supplier threshold. A foreign-owned ULC that carries on business in Canada or solicits orders in Canada must register regardless of the threshold; registration is also strategically necessary for the Chinese pastry chain to recover input tax credits on central-kitchen equipment, leased premises, and franchise-system intangible property.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods — zero-rated under Excise Tax Act Schedule VI, Part V (ss. 20–21) when pastry and bakery products are exported from Canada by the ULC, by the purchaser, or by an agent, with physical export completed within 60 days and contemporaneous proof of export retained by the exporter. Services — zero-rated under Schedule VII, Part V when supplied to a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST and is not present in Canada when the service is performed; this covers offshore master-franchise consulting, international IP licensing, and royalty services rendered to foreign sub-franchisees, unless the service relates to real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada. Supplies to GST-registered Canadian franchisees remain taxable at 5% with full input tax credit flow; exported promotional materials, packaging, and international freight are separately zero-rated.

  • Customs export declaration — CBSA eManifest/ACI carrier filing, exporter Form B13 where mandated, or foreign customs import certificate evidencing export date and consignee
  • Bill of lading or airway bill naming the ULC as shipper and the non-resident franchisee or end customer as consignee
  • Inward remittance certificate / SWIFT MT103 issued by a Canadian Schedule I bank evidencing the foreign-currency transfer and FX repatriation
  • Zero-rated commercial invoice and export packing list with HS codes, weight, and value details
  • For services: CRA non-resident status evidence (GST/HST registration search printout) and the executed master-franchise or IP agreement proving offshore performance

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or CRA pre-approval of invoices. Electronic invoices (PDF, EDI, XML, or Peppol BIS 3.0) are legally valid if they contain the ETA s. 3 disclosure elements: vendor Business Number, invoice date, vendor name, recipient name, consideration, GST rate, and tax amount. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically through CRA-authorized channels (NETFILE, EDI, or MyBA). The Pan-Canadian e-Invoice network (Business Interoperability System) supports voluntary Peppol-based e-invoicing between buyers and suppliers; the CBSA eManifest/ACI regime governs electronic export reporting by carriers and forwarders but is not a tax-invoice clearance or stamping mechanism.

CRA international audit teams require the complete export substantiation file; absent contemporaneous export proof, the zero-rating is reassessed as taxable at 5% GST, with retroactive clawback of the zero-rating entitlement, disallowance of associated input tax credits, and repayment of any refunded amounts with prescribed-rate interest. Penalties: Excise Tax Act s. 280(1) failure-to-remit penalty of 5% of the unremitted amount plus interest; s. 285(1) false-statement penalty of the greater of $100 and 25% of the understated tax; s. 285.1 gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the net misstated tax; and criminal prosecution under ETA ss. 327–328 for wilful evasion, with fines of up to 200% of the evaded tax and possible imprisonment. The bakery franchise chain must retain per-shipment export dossiers for at least six years, with the reassessment period extended indefinitely in cases of misrepresentation or fraud.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is generally fully convertible for current-account and capital-account transactions. Canada has no general exchange-control regime, no general prohibition on non-resident ownership of Canadian companies, and no routine central-bank approval requirement for capital injections, dividends, royalties, loan repayments, or ordinary commercial foreign-exchange conversions. Canadian banks may require documentary evidence of transaction purpose, beneficial ownership, source of funds, tax status, sanctions screening, and corporate authority. Dividend, royalty, interest, and service-fee remittances remain subject to corporate-law solvency requirements, withholding-tax rules, transfer-pricing requirements, treaty eligibility, thin-capitalization rules, Part XIII tax where applicable, and anti-avoidance provisions. An Alberta unlimited liability corporation may create additional shareholder-level and cross-border tax considerations, particularly where a foreign parent seeks flow-through or hybrid treatment; tax classification must be confirmed in the relevant shareholder jurisdictions before implementation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no general statutory deadline for repatriating export proceeds and no mandatory conversion ratio or surrender requirement for foreign-currency export receipts. Export proceeds may generally be retained in foreign-currency accounts or converted at commercial rates. The company must maintain commercially reasonable books and records, support customs and sales documentation, report taxable income in Canadian dollars under applicable tax rules, and comply with sanctions, proceeds-of-crime, anti-terrorist-financing, customs, GST/HST, and transfer-pricing requirements. Banks may impose contractual settlement, reporting, or documentary requirements even though no general statutory surrender rule applies.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Foreign-exchange, payments, anti-money-laundering, deposit-taking, and prudential matters are administered additionally by the Department of Finance Canada, the Canada Revenue Agency, the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions, and applicable Alberta authorities.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for payment processing and foreign-exchange execution; Low for primary Canadian operating-bank replacement. Canada does not use an EU-style electronic-money-institution passport. An offshore digital EMI cannot automatically accept deposits, conduct Canadian banking business, or provide payment services to Canadian users merely because it is licensed abroad. Depending on its activities, a payment service provider may be required to register under the Retail Payment Activities Act and comply with Bank of Canada operational-risk, safeguarding, incident-reporting, and governance requirements. Money-service-business activities may require FINTRAC registration and AML compliance. Canadian bank account access, foreign-currency conversion, stored-value issuance, merchant acquiring, and payment initiation must be analyzed separately. Customer funds should not be represented as Canadian deposit-insured funds unless held through an eligible deposit-taking institution and within applicable insurance limits.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal general corporate income tax rate: 15%; Alberta general corporate income tax rate: 8%; combined general rate: 23%. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) may generally access the federal small-business rate of 9% and Alberta small-business rate of 2%, for a combined rate of 11% on qualifying active business income within applicable business limits. Non-resident control generally prevents CCPC status. A Canadian ULC is a corporation and is not a pass-through entity for Canadian income tax purposes.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Part XIII withholding tax is generally 25% on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident, unless reduced by an applicable tax treaty. Under the Canada–United States treaty, the rate is generally 5% for a qualifying corporate beneficial owner meeting the ownership threshold and 15% in other ordinary cases. Treaty eligibility depends on beneficial ownership, residence, limitation-on-benefits and other treaty conditions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general statutory CAD threshold that automatically triggers OECD-style Local File or Master File preparation. Under Income Tax Act section 247, contemporaneous documentation must be prepared by the documentation deadline and provided to CRA within three months of a written request. T106 reporting commonly applies where reportable non-arm’s-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1 million in aggregate. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least CAD 1 billion, subject to the detailed statutory conditions.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a general Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Canadian anti-avoidance, transfer-pricing, beneficial-ownership, sham-transaction and treaty anti-abuse rules apply. The corporation must maintain genuine commercial records and substantiate the functions performed, assets used, risks assumed and business purpose of related-party arrangements.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Transfer pricing and inadequate contemporaneous documentation

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust income or deductions under Income Tax Act section 247. A penalty generally equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment may apply where the statutory threshold and documentation conditions are met. Interest, reassessments and penalties for inaccurate returns may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for the corporation’s transfer-pricing tax, but may face director-liability assessment for specified unremitted source deductions and other statutory amounts, and personal exposure for their own knowing participation in offences.
Failure to withhold or remit non-resident tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be liable for the unremitted withholding tax, interest and applicable penalties. A penalty may apply for failure to deduct or remit, and information-return penalties may apply for deficient or late NR4 reporting.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable under applicable provisions for specified unremitted source deductions and related amounts, subject to statutory defences and limitation periods.
Failure to file or pay corporate income tax

Statutory sanction: Late-filing penalties, interest at prescribed rates, reassessment, collection enforcement and potential gross-negligence penalties may apply. Repeated late filing can increase the federal late-filing penalty.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for specified unpaid source deductions, GST/HST and certain other statutory amounts; ordinary corporate income tax is not automatically a director debt. Personal liability may arise from statutory liability, fraud or the director’s own offence.
Fraudulent evasion, false statements or tax obstruction

Statutory sanction: Criminal Code or Income Tax Act prosecution may result in fines, imprisonment, or both; tax reassessments, interest, civil penalties and asset-recovery measures may apply. Criminal fines and imprisonment depend on the offence and court findings.

Director personal liability: Individuals who authorize, participate in, or acquiesce in an offence may be prosecuted personally. Corporate status does not shield an individual from liability for their own conduct.
Misclassification of a Canadian ULC as tax-transparent

Statutory sanction: The corporation may face unfiled-return penalties, tax, interest and reassessment. Cross-border hybrid mismatch and treaty-benefit rules may deny deductions or relief where statutory conditions are met.

Director personal liability: No automatic veil piercing follows from classification error. Directors may face statutory personal liability for specified remittances and personal exposure for knowing false filings or participation in an offence.
Unreported foreign property or international information returns

Statutory sanction: Information-return penalties may accrue daily or be assessed at fixed or gross-negligence rates under the applicable provision; late or incomplete reporting may extend reassessment periods in specified circumstances.

Director personal liability: Corporate information-return liability is not automatically personal. Directors may be exposed for their own knowing participation in false reporting or for applicable statutory remittance liabilities.
Unlicensed food and bakery operations

Statutory sanction: Regulatory orders, permit suspension or cancellation, administrative penalties, prosecution, fines or business closure may apply under applicable Alberta and municipal legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors or officers may face personal prosecution where legislation or evidence establishes their authorization, participation, consent or acquiescence. This is a regulatory liability issue, not automatic corporate veil piercing.
Unlawful distribution or shareholder-loan extraction

Statutory sanction: The distribution may be recoverable; shareholder benefits, deemed dividends, interest and penalties may be assessed under tax law. Alberta corporate-law remedies may apply to unlawful distributions.

Director personal liability: Directors may be subject to statutory liability or restitution claims for authorizing unlawful distributions, depending on the governing corporate statute and facts. An Alberta ULC’s unlimited shareholder liability is a separate structural exposure and does not make it a pass-through for Canadian income tax.
