Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC). Online portals: Business Registration Online (BRO), My Business Account (MyBA), and GST/HST NETFILE for electronic return filing. The CRA administers federal corporate income tax, GST/HST, and collects Alberta corporate income tax on behalf of the Province of Alberta under the federal-provincial tax collection agreement.

E-government filing portal

Step 1 - Incorporate the ULC under the Business Corporations Act (Alberta) and obtain the Certificate of Incorporation from the Alberta Corporate Registry (the ULC is a separate statutory corporation available to non-resident owners). Step 2 - Register with the CRA for the 9-digit Business Number (BN), which is the universal Canadian tax identifier; apply online via BRO for same-day issuance or file Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts) with the CRA tax centre, processing in 2 to 6 weeks. Step 3 - Activate program accounts: (i) RC account for T2 corporate income tax; the account is normally auto-created upon incorporation and the T2 return is due within 6 months of each taxation year-end; (ii) RT account for GST/HST, mandatory once the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold is exceeded, with non-resident applicants using Form GST494-1; (iii) RP account for payroll where the ghost-kitchen operator employs Canadian staff, triggering source deduction remittances. Step 4 - The CRA issues a BN confirmation letter; registration is effective on the date the BN is assigned or as specified in the account application. Step 5 - If the ULC is controlled by non-residents, dividends paid to non-resident shareholders are subject to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (statutory 25%, often treaty-reduced) and the ULC must file NR4 information returns; no separate Alberta tax identification number is needed because Alberta corporate tax is collected by the CRA under the collection agreement.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible (U.S. federal check-the-box via Form 8832 and transitional Alberta ULC flow-through election under the Revenue Amendment Act, 2021) / Entity_Level_Taxation (Canadian federal and provincial statutory default). Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible (U.S. federal check-the-box via Form 8832 and transitional Alberta ULC flow-through election under the Revenue Amendment Act, 2021) / Entity_Level_Taxation (Canadian federal and provincial statutory default). For Canadian income tax purposes the Alberta ULC is a corporation under subsection 248(1) of the Income Tax Act and a 'taxable corporation' under the Alberta Corporate Tax Act. It files as a separate taxpayer on its worldwide income if central management and control is situated in Canada. Combined general corporate rate in Alberta: 15% federal plus 8% provincial = 23%; the reduced 11% small-business rate (9% federal plus 2% provincial) is unavailable where more than 50% of the shares are owned by non-residents because the ULC would not qualify as a Canadian-controlled private corporation.

Available election forms

(i) U.S. Form 8832 check-the-box election: permits the ULC to be classified as a disregarded entity or partnership for U.S. federal income tax purposes only; it has no effect on Canadian entity classification. (ii) Alberta transitional ULC election: pre-existing ULCs may elect under the Revenue Amendment Act 2021 to retain member-level flow-through treatment for Alberta income tax for a transitional period, while newly incorporated ULCs are taxed at the entity level. (iii) GST/HST closely related corporation election under section 156 of the Excise Tax Act: the ULC and its 90%-or-more owned subsidiaries may elect to be treated as a single registrant, simplifying intercompany GST/HST accounting. (iv) Voluntary GST/HST registration election under subsection 240(3) of the Excise Tax Act: a small supplier may elect early registration to recover input tax credits.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) made by the entity in the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters or in any single calendar quarter; the threshold is aggregated with taxable supplies of associated persons under section 148 of the Excise Tax Act. A multi-brand ghost kitchen must aggregate revenue from all virtual brands. Registration becomes mandatory once the CAD 30,000 threshold is exceeded; failure to register attracts a penalty equal to the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the net tax not collected.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating at 0% GST/HST is available for supplies of goods exported directly by the supplier under section 6 of Part V, Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act, including prepared foods and co-packed virtual-brand products shipped by the operator or its carrier to a destination outside Canada. Supplies of services to a non-resident purchaser are zero-rated under section 7 of Part V, Schedule VI where the services are performed entirely outside Canada, or are performed partly in Canada but relate to real property situated outside Canada or to goods outside Canada and are for consumption outside Canada, and the purchaser is not registered under Part IX and carries on no business in Canada; this covers virtual-brand licensing fees and management or technology fees charged to a foreign parent. Domestic ghost-kitchen sales to Canadian consumers through DoorDash or UberEats are taxable at 5% GST. Export eligibility requires direct export with no intermediate Canadian processing or sale, contemporaneous proof of physical export movement, and retention of supporting records for six years.

  • Customs Export Declaration - Form B13A Goods to be Exported filed through the Canadian Automated Export Declaration (CAED) system or the carrier's cargo report for shipments above CAD 2,000, including the assigned Export Report Number
  • Transport evidence - Bill of Lading (ocean), Air Waybill, truck manifest, or courier shipment scan history showing delivery to an address outside Canada
  • Commercial Invoice and Purchase Order or Sales Contract describing the exported goods, quantity, price, and foreign destination
  • Inward Remittance Certificate - bank-issued proof of foreign currency receipt, SWIFT MT103 credit advice, or bank statement evidencing settlement of the export consideration
  • Cargo control or customs broker documentation demonstrating border exit and foreign customs entry

Canada has no statutory real-time fiscal clearance, mandatory API stamping, or digital stamping regime. GST/HST registrants must issue invoices, in paper or electronic form, containing the particulars set out in section 3 of Schedule V to the GST/HST Regulations: supplier name, Business Number, date of invoice, description of goods or services, and tax amount. Electronic records and invoices must be maintained in a CRA-readable format for six years under section 286 of the Excise Tax Act. Registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 1 million must file GST/HST returns electronically via GST/HST NETFILE. The CRA is developing a voluntary Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing framework based on the PEPPOL standard; deployment remains optional and there is no clearance mandate. Digital platform operators such as DoorDash and UberEats are separately subject to the Information Return for Digital Platform Operators (effective 1 January 2023), which imposes due-diligence and seller-reporting duties unrelated to e-invoicing.

The CRA's normal reassessment period for a corporation is four years from the date of the original notice of assessment under section 298 of the Excise Tax Act; the limitation period is unlimited where misrepresentation, neglect, or wilful default is proven. Where export zero-rating is claimed without eligible documentation, the supply is reassessed as taxable at 5% GST, any related input tax credits are clawed back, and compound daily interest accrues at prescribed CRA rates. Gross-negligence penalties equal the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the net tax sought to be avoided under subsection 280(1) of the Excise Tax Act. Criminal evasion under section 327 is punishable on summary conviction by a fine of up to 200% of the evaded tax and/or 2 years imprisonment, and on indictment by a fine of up to 250% and/or 5 years imprisonment. Directors of the ULC are jointly and severally liable for unremitted GST/HST under subsection 323(1) unless they exercised the standard of care of a reasonably prudent person. The Excise Tax Act prescribes no statutory F/X repatriation deadline; the 180-day benchmark is a documentary best-practice standard, and prolonged non-receipt of export proceeds will trigger audit scrutiny and retrospective clawback of zero-rating benefits.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada observes full Article VIII convertibility for both current-account and capital-account transactions. The Canadian dollar is freely floating and fully convertible. There are no prior-exchange-control licenses, no non-resident remittance permits, and no restriction on inbound equity investment into an Alberta unlimited liability corporation. Practical hurdles to profit remittance are corporate solvency under the Alberta Business Corporations Act and Canadian withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act. Dividends paid by the ULC to a non-resident shareholder are subject to the statutory 25% withholding rate unless reduced under an applicable tax treaty; the Canada-U.S. treaty may reduce this rate to 5% or 15%, depending on shareholding and treaty eligibility. A ULC may distribute funds only if the solvency test is satisfied and directors have approved the remittance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory surrender rule, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no timeline for converting export receipts in Canada. Exporters may retain foreign currency balances in Canadian or offshore accounts, may convert freely, and may use such balances for foreign operating expenses. For regulatory reporting, cross-border electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more are reportable to FINTRAC by the transmitting financial institution. If the ULC holds specified foreign property, including foreign bank or investment accounts, with an aggregate cost amount exceeding CAD 100,000, Form T1135 must be filed with the Canada Revenue Agency. Export proceeds recognized in Canadian taxable income are converted into Canadian dollars using the Bank of Canada annual average exchange rate or the CRA exchange rate for the relevant taxation year.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada, with prudential banking oversight by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), anti-money-laundering supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), and no dedicated foreign-exchange-control authority; Canada does not maintain a centralized exchange-approval regime.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low-Medium. Offshore electronic money institutions are not recognized as Schedule I banks under the Bank Act and cannot individually provide Canadian clearing account numbering or Payment Canada settlement. An offshore EMI may offer ancillary payment processing only if it registers as a money services business with FINTRAC and complies with Alberta money-services licensing requirements. However, for DoorDash and UberEats payout rails, a Canadian-chartered-bank or ATB Financial account is operationally required because merchant settlement schedules and clearing codes in Canada are bank-account based. An EMI can serve as a supplementary disbursement/float instrument but not as the primary treasury rail for the ULC.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity or shareholder-loan capital from non-resident investors should be sent via SWIFT MT103 or a direct payment instruction in CAD, USD, or other freely convertible currency. The beneficiary must be the Alberta ULC bank account held at the chosen Canadian clearing bank. The remittance must include full originator name, address, and account number to satisfy FINTRAC electronic-funds-transfer reporting when the amount is CAD 10,000 or more. There is no Canadian exchange-control permit or central bank declaration. SWIFT field 70 should state 'ULC EQUITY INJECTION' or 'SHAREHOLDER LOAN WORKING CAPITAL'; no single statutory purpose code is prescribed for Canada. The receiving bank should be provided with the properly executed equity subscription, loan agreement, source-of-funds evidence, and board resolutions. To avoid operational flags, do not split a capital injection into multiple wires below CAD 10,000; instead evidence the entire lawful transaction and accept the bank's large-wire review process.Outbound trade disbursements for kitchen supplies, food ingredients, packaging, franchise-style royalties, or SaaS subscriptions should be cleared through SWIFT MT103 for cross-border vendors and Payments Canada's LVTS/ACSS domestic clearing rails for Canadian suppliers. For high-value imports, a commercial documentary letter of credit may be opened through a Schedule I bank trade-services desk under UCP600. USD disbursements can be sent via the correspondent banking network using a Canadian branch's U.S. domestic clearing access. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal plus 8% Alberta. The combined small-business rate may be 11% for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) on eligible active business income within the applicable business limit, comprising 9% federal and 2% Alberta. A non-resident-controlled ULC ordinarily cannot access the CCPC small-business rate.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. A Canada-U.S. treaty rate is generally 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying company holding the required voting interest and 15% in other qualifying cases. Other treaties commonly reduce dividends to 5%, 10%, or 15%, depending on ownership and treaty conditions. A dividend paid by a ULC may create additional treaty-classification and hybrid-entity risk because Canada may treat the ULC as a corporation while a foreign jurisdiction treats it as fiscally transparent.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is automatically unnecessary. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires arm's-length pricing and contemporaneous documentation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. Form T106 generally applies where the statutory aggregate non-arm's-length non-resident transaction threshold is exceeded; the commonly applicable threshold is CAD 1,000,000, subject to the detailed statutory rules and exclusions. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year. Canada does not impose a universal OECD-style Local File/Master File filing threshold for every Canadian corporation, but CRA may require supporting documentation and may impose penalties for inadequate documentation.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a standalone regime formally called Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Economic substance remains material under the Income Tax Act's general anti-avoidance rule, sham and simulation principles, treaty anti-abuse provisions, principal-purpose tests, beneficial-ownership analysis, transfer-pricing rules, residence and central-management analysis, and permanent-establishment rules. A ULC should maintain real Canadian decision-making, commercial functions, personnel or outsourced operational capability, Alberta premises or operational access, books and records, banking controls, contracts, pricing authority, food-safety compliance, platform relationships, and evidence that the entity independently bears and controls relevant risks. A conduit ULC receiving funds and paying them immediately to a foreign owner without commercial functions is a high-risk arrangement.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, conviction may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to two years, or both, in addition to reassessment, interest, civil penalties, and collection costs. Conduct involving fraud may also be prosecuted under the Criminal Code, with substantially more severe imprisonment exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal prosecution where they directed, authorized, acquiesced in, or participated in the conduct. Directors may be assessed personally for unremitted source deductions and certain GST/HST liabilities under statutory director-liability provisions. Corporate separateness generally protects directors from ordinary corporate tax debt, but does not protect against statutory assessments, personal participation, fraud, breach of trust, or criminal conduct. Civil restitution and asset recovery may follow; travel restrictions may arise through criminal process or bail conditions, but are not an automatic tax penalty.
Unreported Related-Party Transactions and Transfer-Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: The CRA may reassess under section 247, impose transfer-pricing adjustments, deny deductions, assess Part XIII withholding, and apply a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the applicable adjustment where the statutory conditions are met. Interest and other information-return penalties may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for knowingly approving false records, source-deduction failures, or fraudulent arrangements. Ordinary transfer-pricing reassessment is generally assessed against the corporation, but director liability can arise under statutory derivative-liability provisions or through civil and criminal participation.
Improper Treaty Relief and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the unwithheld Part XIII tax, interest, failure-to-withhold penalties, failure-to-remit penalties, and information-return penalties. Treaty benefits may be denied under anti-conduit, principal-purpose, beneficial-ownership, or other anti-abuse provisions.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors and officers may incur personal exposure for unremitted source deductions where the statutory due-diligence and director-liability tests are met. Intentional misuse of treaty documentation may support civil penalties or criminal tax allegations.
Shell Company, Artificial Residency, or Conduit Arrangement

Statutory sanction: The CRA may deny deductions, recharacterize transactions, apply the general anti-avoidance rule, deny treaty benefits, assess withholding tax, and reassess tax for applicable periods with interest and penalties. Sham transactions and fraudulent records may lead to prosecution under the Income Tax Act or Criminal Code.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil claims, personal assessments for statutory remittances, director-and-officer liability for knowing participation, asset seizure or forfeiture in criminal proceedings, and imprisonment where fraud or evasion is proven. A travel ban is not an automatic corporate-tax sanction but may be imposed by a court as a criminal-process condition.
GST/HST, Payroll, and Platform-Settlement Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: The corporation may face GST/HST reassessments, net-tax and source-deduction interest, late-filing and late-remittance penalties, gross-negligence penalties, and denial of input tax credits where invoices or records are deficient.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for unremitted payroll source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts. Personal exposure can continue after dissolution or insolvency, subject to statutory limitation periods and due-diligence defenses.
Corporate Records, Beneficial Ownership, and Annual Return Failure

Statutory sanction: The Alberta corporate registry may impose late fees, administrative dissolution, restoration costs, and compliance orders. Tax authorities may impose record-keeping and information penalties and draw adverse inferences from missing records.

Director personal liability: Directors may face statutory offences, fines, disqualification consequences, civil claims, and personal exposure where inaccurate records conceal tax evasion, creditor prejudice, or unlawful distributions. Dissolution does not eliminate tax debts or director-liability assessments.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Proceeds-of-Crime Risk

Statutory sanction: Proceeds-of-crime and money-laundering offences may result in substantial fines, forfeiture, and imprisonment under the Criminal Code and Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Regulated reporting entities may face administrative monetary penalties for registration, suspicious-transaction, record-keeping, or compliance-program failures.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, beneficial owners, and employees who knowingly participate may face criminal charges, imprisonment, forfeiture, restitution, asset restraint, and court-imposed travel restrictions. Corporate liability does not shield individuals who personally commit or authorize laundering conduct.
Unlawful Dividend or Capital Distribution

Statutory sanction: The distribution may be voidable under applicable Alberta corporate law, recharacterized for tax purposes, treated as a shareholder benefit or dividend, and subject to withholding tax, interest, penalties, creditor remedies, and insolvency clawback.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorize prohibited distributions may face restitution claims, contribution claims, creditor actions, disqualification, and personal liability under corporate statutes. Personal liability is distinct from ordinary shareholder liability and may arise where directors breached statutory solvency duties or acted fraudulently.
Food, Employment, and Licensing Non-Compliance with Tax Consequences

Statutory sanction: Authorities may issue inspection orders, administrative penalties, licence suspension, closure orders, employment assessments, payroll reassessments, and prosecution for serious violations. Unlawful payments may be denied as deductions or treated as unreported employment income.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally prosecuted for offences involving unsafe food, workplace violations, or knowing payroll and tax evasion. Civil restitution, compensation orders, and travel restrictions may follow criminal proceedings; imprisonment depends on the governing statute and facts.
