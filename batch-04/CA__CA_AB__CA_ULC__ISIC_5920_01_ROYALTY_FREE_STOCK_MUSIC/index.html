Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

None. The ABCA imposes no minimum paid-up capital, no escrow, and no capital deposit for Alberta ULCs; shares may be issued for cash, property, or past services at a consideration fixed by the directors (s. 25). The ULC's distinguishing feature is that shareholders remain jointly and severally liable for the corporation's debts, which is a statutory liability substitution, not a capital funding requirement. US owners may file IRS Form 8832 (check-the-box) to treat the ULC as a disregarded/partnership entity.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html; GST/HST online portal: https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-individuals/gst-hst-netfile.html

E-government filing portal

A non-resident entity carrying on business in Canada must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) via the CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO) system. For GST/HST purposes, the ULC must register for a GST/HST account using the BN 'RT' program account. Registration can be completed online through BRO, by mail using Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts), or by telephone. The ULC must provide its legal name, Alberta corporate access number, registered office address, and description of commercial activity (NAICS 512240). If the entity is a non-resident without permanent establishment in Canada, it must still register for GST/HST if it makes taxable supplies in Canada or solicits orders in Canada, subject to the 'non-resident registrant' rules. A simplified non-resident GST/HST registration is available under Form RC1 and may require a Canadian resident representative for service of process if the non-resident has no place of business in Canada. The CRA normally issues the BN immediately in the case of online registration and by mail within 5-10 business days for paper applications. The entity must also register with the Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration for provincial corporate income tax administration (Alberta levies a flat provincial corporate tax administered by the CRA under a federal-provincial tax collection agreement, so a separate Alberta corporate tax registration is not required; however, if the ULC carries on a commercial activity or has a physical presence in Alberta, it should complete the Alberta Corporate Tax Registration via the CRA's BRO system). For payroll, the ULC would need a CRA payroll deductions program account (RP) if it has Canadian employees; for import/export, an importer/exporter account (RM) must be added to the BN. The customs account is obtained through the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) via the CRA Business Registration Online interface or the CBSA Portal (Account Registration and Security Management portal). If the ULC is resident in Alberta for corporate tax purposes, it must file an annual T2 Corporation Income Tax Return; if classified as a partnership or disregarded entity for Canadian tax purposes, the ULC may be treated as a corporation under the Income Tax Act (Canada) unless it validly elects otherwise under section 216 or relies on an applicable tax treaty; in all cases a T2 return is required for corporations, while a Canadian-resident ULC that is fiscally transparent under a treaty may need to file a T2 as a deemed resident corporation. The ULC should procure a BN (9-digit) and confirm the applicable CRA program accounts: RT (GST/HST), RC (corporate income tax), RP (payroll), and RM (customs).

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (the 'small supplier' threshold) in global taxable supplies other than financial services in the preceding four calendar quarters; once exceeded, the ULC must register for GST/HST. A non-resident that is a 'non-registrant' may also be deemed to be carrying on business in Canada and required to register if it solicits orders in Canada or has a Canadian agent. For digital supplies of audio licensing subscriptions to Canadian recipients, a non-resident supplier may be required to register under the mandatory 'registration of non-resident persons carrying on business in Canada' rule if it makes taxable supplies in Canada; however, the special 'specified supplies' regime for non-resident digital service suppliers uses a separate threshold: for supplies of 'specified digital services' (including music downloads and streaming) made to Canadian recipients, a non-resident supplier must register if it is not a small supplier (CAD 30,000) or if it sells more than CAD 30,000 in a calendar quarter to Canadian recipients and does not meet the small supplier exception. New GST/HST rules effective July 1, 2021: non-resident vendors of digital goods and services are required to register, collect, and remit GST/HST on supplies to Canadian consumers unless their worldwide taxable supplies do not exceed CAD 30,000 in any rolling four-quarter period. Since the audio licensing marketplace targets Canadian customers via Stripe/PayPal, the ULC should assume the threshold is CAD 30,000. There is no separate Alberta provincial registration threshold for GST because Alberta has no provincial sales tax; but if the ULC is a 'large business' (generally over CAD 10 million in taxable supplies), it must file GST/HST electronically monthly and may be subject to a penalty for not registering. For zero-rated exports, there is no threshold exception.

Canada does not currently impose a mandatory electronic invoicing (e-invoicing) or real-time fiscal clearance regime akin to Latin American models. The CRA accepts electronic invoices if they are legible and contain prescribed information: supplier name, recipient name, GST/HST registration number, invoice date, description, amount, and tax charged. The CRA does not require pre-clearance or digital stamping. Since May 2022, the CRA has published the 'Electronic Payment Standard' and is progressively implementing a 'Digital Compliance' framework using data analytics; business must retain source books and records in an electronically readable format. GST/HST registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 5 million must file GST/HST returns electronically; all registrants must use electronic filing if the CRA directs. The CRA mandates a specific GST/HST NETFILE XML format for the GST/HST return itself, but there is no government-validated e-invoicing clearance. However, for any imports into Canada, the CBSA requires electronic customs declarations via the Canadian Single Window and a B3 form or CADEX; commercial goods must be reported through the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) portal. For digital services, no fiscal clearance stamp is required. The ULC should implement secure electronic invoicing that complies with Canada's books and records retention rules (Income Tax Act section 230; Excise Tax Act section 286) and must be prepared to produce invoices in English or French. There is no requirement to obtain a CRA-issued 'authorization code' for each invoice. The ULC should use its BN/RT account on invoices.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully liberalized capital account with no general exchange controls, no restriction on current or capital account transactions, and a freely floating Canadian dollar (CAD). Profits, royalties, service fees, dividends, and capital repatriation from an Alberta-Based Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) can be remitted without central bank authorisation. The only mandatory hurdles are PCMLTFA record keeping, large cash transaction reporting (LCTR), and electronic funds transfer reporting (EFTR) for entries/remittances exceeding CAD 10,000 in a single transaction or consecutive transactions intended to evade thresholds. The CAD is fully convertible; there is no differentiated capital vs current account treatment.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export proceeds surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion ratio to CAD. Exporters and cross-border licensors may retain all foreign currency proceeds in foreign-denominated accounts maintained in Canada or abroad. The only obligations relate to transfer pricing contemporaneous documentation under the Income Tax Act, Section 247, and submission of Canadian Balance of Payments transaction reports through Statistics Canada for inbound/outbound payments exceeding CAD 1,000,000 annually, where applicable. There is no specific timeline for converting export receipts; banks may establish internal policy requiring declaration of foreign currency receipts for reporting under the Foreign Exchange Accounting Framework (managed by OSFI).

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) in conjunction with the Canadian Department of Finance, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for cross-border financial intelligence; foreign exchange and capital account administration is principally handled by the Exchange Fund Account managed by the Bank of Canada and the cross-border data reporting framework under the Customs Act and the Currency Declaration Regulations.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: HIGH for ancillary collection rails on royalty-free stock music subscriptions and Soundscape licensing fees; LOW for primary treasury account holding. Canadian-regulated Financial Technology Entities (e.g., Wise, Payoneer, Stripe) and provincial Money Services Businesses may provide payment facilitation, but they are not Canadian Schedule I banks and cannot serve as the primary bank account for a ULC requiring statutory capital escrow, dividend distributions, or CRA direct debit relationships. Offshore EMIs without registration under the Retail Payment Activities Act or while required licenses are pending, are not viable as principal corporate bank accounts. They may be used as receiving-node only, with full sweep to a domestic Tier-1 account.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant routing uses SWIFT MT103 or SWIFT MT202 for the transfer to an Alberta-based Tier-1 bank account. The payment must be clearly labelled with purpose code ‘CA200 – Equity Injection’ or use ISO 20022 purpose code INVE/IVGT (Investment). FINTRAC requires an EFT report for a single transfer of CAD 10,000 or more. The following documentation must accompany the wire instruction: certified copy of the ULC articles, board resolution approving the capital contribution, share subscription agreement, source-of-funds declaration by the remitting member, and a covered funds or CIP attestation from the remitting bank. No central bank permission is required, but the receiving bank will require a Bank of Canada cheque or electronic clearance via Lynx for same-day CAD settlement; if the capital is in USD, the Canadian bank will convert to CAD using the floating exchange rate at delivery, unless a hedging forward is pre-arranged.For royalty-free stock music licensing royalty payouts to foreign copyright owners, use SWIFT MT103 with purpose code ‘Royalties’ (Category S–General Services) and populate Field 70 to include the licensing contract number. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined (federal 15% + Alberta 8%). For Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) claiming the Small Business Deduction, the combined effective rate on active business income up to C$500,000 is 17% (federal 9% + Alberta 8%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder. Reduced treaty rates apply generally: 5% for U.S. corporate shareholders holding at least 10% voting shares under Article 10, 15% for portfolio shareholders; 5%/15% under most other Canadian DTAs.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T2 must be filed within 6 months after the end of the corporation's taxation year. For a December 31 year-end, the deadline is June 30 of the following calendar year. Instalment payments are due on the 15th day of the 2nd month after the year-end and the 15th day of the 3rd, 6th, 9th, and 12th months of the taxation year for qualifying corporations.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000. A Canadian corporation must file Form T106 and prepare contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation when its reportable transactions with any non-resident related party exceed C$1,000,000 in the taxation year. CRA generally follows OECD transfer pricing documentation standards, including Master File and Local File expectations.

Economic substance scope: Applicable through Canadian GAAR (s.245 of the Income Tax Act) and the OECD-aligned transfer pricing rules. Transactions must have real economic substance, purposeful business rationale, and arm's-length economic returns. Alberta does not operate offshore Economic Substance Regulations, but hybrid entity structures (ULC check-the-box) require substantive commercial activity, proper staffing, Canadian decision-making, and actual risk assumption to withstand CRA anti-avoidance review and treaty LOB scrutiny.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Fine under s.239 of the Income Tax Act of up to 200% of the tax evaded plus imprisonment up to 5 years. Civil penalty under s.163(2) of 50% of understated tax. Accrued interest and revocation of eligibility for voluntary disclosure.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in or authorize evasion are liable to criminal prosecution, personal fines and imprisonment. Personal remittance liability under s.227.1 applies for unremitted withholdings. Civil fraudulent conveyance judgments may pierce the corporate veil. Travel bans or departure orders may be issued under s.226 of the ITA for unpaid assessed tax.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA reassessment under s.247 to arm's-length prices; 10% transfer pricing penalty on the adjustment amount if documentation is inadequate; interest on deficiencies; potential criminal prosecution for grossly negligent misrepresentation.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve or sign false transfer pricing documentation face personal civil liability; in insolvency scenarios the CRA may seek director assessments for unremitted amounts; criminal liability for aiding in tax evasion.
Hybrid Mismatch / Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Anti-hybrid rules under s.18.4/18.5 disallow deductions; GAAR under s.245 denies the tax benefit; treaty refusal under LOB clauses; Part XIII assessments on deemed dividends; 50% gross negligence penalty; potential FAPI inclusion for shareholders.

Director personal liability: Directors who structure sham hybrid transactions with knowledge face personal assessments and criminal exposure; courts may disregard the ULC's separate legal identity if it is a facade; travel bans and asset freezing orders are available in serious cases.
Money Laundering / Unlicensed Cross-Border Payment Processing

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to C$500,000 per violation; criminal penalties under the Criminal Code (up to 10 years imprisonment) for laundering proceeds; forfeiture orders; prohibition from serving as corporate director.

Director personal liability: Directors who direct or approve money laundering are criminally liable as principals; personal fines and imprisonment; travel bans imposed as bail conditions or part of restraining orders; assets subject to restraint and forfeiture.
Unreported Foreign Affiliate / Foreign Reporting Failures

Statutory sanction: T1135: C$25/day up to C$2,500; for knowing failure C$500 to C$12,000. T1134: C$100/day up to C$8,000. Additional penalties for failure to respond to demand up to C$24,000. Reassessment plus interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly fail to file may be personally assessed for penalties; if foreign reporting was used to conceal taxable income, directors face criminal tax evasion liability and personal fines; CRA departure orders may apply for ongoing delinquent accounts.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR reassessment under s.245; denial of treaty benefits; corporate veil-piercing under provincial and federal corporate law; penalties for failure to maintain corporate registration (annual return defaults); potential revocation of incorporation status and director disqualification.

Director personal liability: Under Alberta and British Columbia ULC statutes, shareholders have unlimited liability; directors may be personally liable for corporate debts where the ULC was treated as an agent or sham. Directors can be assessed without benefit of corporate shield for unpaid taxes, GST/HST, and employee source deductions; travel bans under s.226 for leaving Canada while debts to the CRA exceed C$10,000.
