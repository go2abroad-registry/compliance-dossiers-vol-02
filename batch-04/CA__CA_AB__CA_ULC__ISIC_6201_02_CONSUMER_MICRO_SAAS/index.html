Tax ID Application Infrastructure

      

        
Competent tax authority
Canada Revenue Agency (Agence du revenu du Canada) — Business Registration Online (BRO) portal, My Business Account portal, and Represent a Client portal

        
E-government filing portal
A foreign-owned Alberta ULC must first be registered with the Alberta Corporate Registry to obtain a corporate Access Number. The entity then applies to the Canada Revenue Agency for a 9-digit Business Number (BN) and a GST/HST account using Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number'. The application requires the corporate registry particulars, the designated financial institution, the date the first taxable supply was made or is expected to be made, and a declaration of annual worldwide taxable supplies. Registration is effective on the date of the first taxable supply if applied within 30 days thereafter; late filers lose the ability to claim input tax credits on pre-registration expenses. Non-resident directors or agents may complete the application provided a Canadian mailing address or an authorized representative with a Canadian address is designated.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

      

        
Default tax classification
Pass_Through_Eligible

        
Available election forms
For Canadian income tax purposes, the Alberta ULC is an entity-level taxable corporation; Canada has no domestic check-the-box regime. However, under Article IV(7) of the Canada-US Income Tax Convention (Fifth Protocol) and the associated competent authority mutual agreement, a ULC whose members are all residents of the United States for US tax purposes may be treated as a fiscally transparent/disregarded entity for US federal tax purposes via US Form 8832 election. The same entity remains a Canadian taxable corporation, creating hybrid entity exposure; Canada's hybrid mismatch rules (subsections 18.2 to 18.4 of the Income Tax Act) and the OECD BEPS Action 2 recommendations require contemporaneous documentation of any deduction/non-inclusion mismatches arising from this dual classification.

        Export 0% zero-rating conditions
        
A supply of SaaS / cloud-based productivity software delivered electronically to a non-resident recipient who is not registered for GST/HST and who is not in Canada at the time the service is performed qualifies for zero-rating under Section 7 of Part V, Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act (service supplied to a non-resident), supplemented by Section 21 for intangible personal property supplied to a non-resident. Supplies of software by download are zero-rated under Section 23 where the recipient is a non-resident. The supplier must obtain documentary evidence of the recipient's non-residency within a reasonable period and must retain subscription records, IP geolocation data, payment gateway settlement statements from Paddle or Lemon Squeezy, and any relevant tax exemption certificates. Supplies to Canadian-resident consumers remain subject to 5% GST and are not zero-rated.

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or continuous transaction control regime is in force. The CRA operates a voluntary B2B e-invoicing initiative aligned with the Pan-Canadian Trust Framework and the PEPPOL network, with a federal government roadmap toward mandatory e-invoicing for business-to-government supplies over the 2024–2029 horizon. Taxpayers may issue invoices in electronic form (including XML or PEPPOL formats) via certified service providers; there is no pre-clearance API, no sequential invoice stamping by the CRA, and no fiscal repository requirement.

      
The CRA may reassess GST/HST refunds and zero-rated net tax claims within the normal four-year limitation period, and without time limit where misrepresentation, negligence, or fraud is established. A false statement or omission made knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence attracts a penalty of 50% of the overclaimed refund or input tax credit under section 280 of the Excise Tax Act; unremitted GST/HST bears interest at the prescribed rate compounded daily. Failure to remit collected amounts may also trigger a 10% penalty. Criminal prosecution for deliberate evasion may result, on summary conviction, in fines up to CAD 50,000 and imprisonment up to 2 years, or on indictment, fines up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years. Retroactive clawback of input tax credits and the revocation of non-resident GST/HST registration are additional consequences for non-compliant zero-rating claims.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

      

        
Current-account convertibility
The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current and capital account, with no mandatory conversion or surrender requirements. Profit repatriation from an Alberta ULC is unrestricted, subject to federal withholding tax considerations under Income Tax Act section 212 where applicable, while the unlimited liability pass-through structure permits flexible cross-border capital flow planning.

        
Export proceeds surrender rules
Canada does not impose statutory surrender, conversion timelines, or mandatory ratio requirements for export receipts. No export proceeds surrender regime exists under federal FX law; filings are limited to Trade in Services and statistical reporting administered by Statistics Canada and the Canada Border Services Agency as applicable.

      

      
Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) has monetary and systemically important clearing supervision authority; the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) issues primary bank prudential licensing; FINTRAC administers AML/CFT supervision; there is no dedicated foreign exchange control administrative body for Canada.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

      
Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 in aggregate reportable transactions with each non-resident related party per taxation year; above this threshold the taxpayer is expected to maintain contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation (Local File / Master File) and to file Form T106. CRA may impose transfer-pricing penalties of 10% of the understated taxable income (minimum CAD 5,000) when documentation is absent and the pricing is outside arm's-length range.

      
Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply the offshore Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) as seen in the UAE; however, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under subsection 245(2) of the Income Tax Act denies tax benefits from transactions lacking economic substance. For a ULC, CRA will scrutinize whether the entity has substantive board meetings, Canadian operational staff, a Canadian bank account, and a real Canadian marketing presence. A shell ULC whose activities are only nominally Canadian may be deemed managed in the United States and lose Canadian residency, causing unrelieved withholdings and challenging treaty benefits under the Canada–US DTA.

      

    

    

      
Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

      
      

        Tax Evasion (willful omission)
        
Statutory sanction: Section 239(1) of the Income Tax Act: fine of 100% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years, and 50% gross-negligence penalties under section 163.2 in addition to reassessment with compound interest.

        
Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for failed withholding remittances (section 227.1), and can be criminally prosecuted if they directed or acquiesced in evasive conduct. Personal travel bans, U.S. extradition under Canada–U.S. mutual legal assistance, and seizure of personal assets can follow a conviction.

      

      
      

        Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure
        
Statutory sanction: CRA can make transfer-pricing adjustments, levy a penalty of 10% of the net understatement (minimum CAD 5,000) under subsection 247(3), and add interest on the reassessed amount. A recurrence may trigger criminal prosecution for false statements.

        
Director personal liability: Directors who authorized intercompany payments without TP review may be personally liable for failure to severence? In practice, CRA will recover against corporate assets; directors can be held personally for withholding and for fraudulent preferences in insolvency.

      

      
      

        Shell Company / Artificial Economic Substance
        
Statutory sanction: GAAR (subsection 245(2)) permits CRA to ignore the avoidance transaction and reassess tax as if the substance existed. Canada can also reject treaty benefits where a foreign shareholder fails the Limitation-on-Benefits test, and the CRA may reallocate income to a permanent establishment in the foreign shareholder's residence.

        
Director personal liability: Directors knowingly maintaining a sham corporation can be liable for civil fraud and deceit, and may be ordered to unwind distributions under provincial laws, with criminal liability if the shell facilitates money laundering.

      

      
      

        Profit Repatriation without Proper Withholding
        
Statutory sanction: Under section 215, the payer is liable for the full unpaid withheld amount, plus penalties and interest; reinstatement may include a fine of up to CAD 10,000 or imprisonment if the non-remittance is willful.

        
Director personal liability: Directors are explicitly liable for unremitted withholding taxes under subsection 227.1. The CRA may assess the director personally immediately after the corporation defaults, commuting to personal bank seizures, property liens, and restraint on travel under the Income Tax Act.

      

      
      

        AML / Sanctions Violation
        
Statutory sanction: Canada's United Nations Act and Special Economic Measures Act impose offshore fines of up to CAD 25,000 per violation and imprisonment up to one year. Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), failing to maintain beneficial ownership and transaction records can result in administrative penalties up to CAD 2,000,000 for a financial institution and referral to FINTRAC.

        
Director personal liability: Directors and officers knowingly failing to prevent sanctions violations can be prosecuted as parties to the offence, fined personally, and barred from serving as directors under the Canada Business Corporations Act. In egregious cases, personal travel restrictions and extradition to foreign jurisdiction may be imposed for money laundering.
