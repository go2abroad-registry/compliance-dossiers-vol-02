Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC). National registration and filing portals: Business Registration Online (BRO) and My Business Account (MyBA) at canada.ca. The Alberta provincial corporate income tax is administered and collected by the CRA under the Canada-Alberta Tax Collection Agreement, so no separate provincial tax registration is required; incorporation is effected separately through the Alberta Corporate Registry (OneStep).

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate the Alberta Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) under the Alberta Business Corporations Act (ABCA) through the Alberta Corporate Registry; no minimum capital contribution is required and non-resident shareholders are permitted. Step 2: Register the corporation with the CRA via Business Registration Online (BRO) using the legal name and corporate registry number; the CRA issues the nine-digit Business Number (BN), which is the statutory tax identifier (functional equivalent of a TIN/VAT ID) for all federal and Alberta tax obligations. Step 3: Open the required CRA program accounts under the BN: corporate income tax (T2), GST/HST (program account suffix RT), payroll (suffix RP) if employees are engaged, and import/export accounts if goods are physically moved. Step 4: Because Alberta corporate income tax is collected by the CRA, the provincial tax is computed and reported as part of the single federal T2 corporate income tax return; no separate Alberta account is opened. Step 5: Register for GST/HST before making the first taxable supply if worldwide taxable revenues exceed the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold; voluntary registration below the threshold is available and is commercially advisable for a LegalTech SaaS exporter because it permits input tax credit recovery and zero-rated export refund claims. The BN must be printed on every GST/HST invoice.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

The Alberta ULC is Pass_Through_Eligible solely through the U.S. check-the-box regime: a U.S. shareholder files IRS Form 8832 (Entity Classification Election) within 75 days of formation, with late-election relief available, to classify the ULC as a disregarded entity or partnership for U.S. federal income tax purposes. The resulting structure is a hybrid entity: Canadian domestic law contains no analogue election, and the CRA treats the ULC as a taxable Canadian corporation subject to entity-level federal Part I tax and Alberta corporate income tax, so the ULC files its own T2 return. Ancillary statutory elections include: (i) the closely-related registrants election under Excise Tax Act (ETA) s. 156, which can deem taxable intercorporate supplies between the ULC and qualifying affiliates to be for nil consideration, eliminating GST/HST on intra-group SaaS and IT cost-recharge invoices; and (ii) alignment with Canada's hybrid-mismatch rules (Income Tax Act ss. 18.4-18.5) and Canada-United States tax treaty tie-breaker and limitation-on-benefits provisions, which CRA may invoke to deny deductions or recharacterize payments where the check-the-box outcome creates deduction/non-inclusion mismatches.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 worldwide taxable supplies made in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (the ETA s. 148 small-supplier threshold); registration is mandatory once exceeded, and a non-resident supplier of digital services to Canadian consumers is deemed to carry on business in Canada and must register at the same CAD 30,000 threshold. Voluntary registration is available below the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rated export treatment is available under Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act as follows: (i) tangible goods delivered to a destination outside Canada (Part V, s. 1, with the goods required to be exported as soon as reasonably possible); (ii) services supplied to a non-resident person (Part V, s. 7) where the non-resident is not a GST/HST registrant and the service is not in respect of real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time the service is performed; and (iii) intangible personal property, including software licences and cloud/digital document-automation IP, supplied to a non-resident (Part V, s. 10) where the entitlement is not for use in Canada. For the ULC's LegalTech contract-automation and e-signature SaaS, the principal claim is zero-rating under Part V, s. 7 as a service supplied to a non-resident customer; the supply to Canadian-resident customers remains taxable at 5% GST. The supplier must hold contemporaneous records proving the non-resident status of the customer, the delivery of the digital service outside Canada, and the absence of use or enjoyment in Canada.

  • B13A Export Declaration (electronic export declaration filed through the Canadian Export Reporting System, CERS) for goods exported from Canada when the value exceeds CAD 2,000
  • Commercial invoice and packing list identifying the supplier, buyer, description of goods, quantities, and export value
  • Transport evidence: bill of lading (ocean), air waybill (air), or courier/CERS e-manifest (land, rail, and express) proving that the goods physically departed Canada
  • Banks and inward remittance records: Inward Remittance Certificate, foreign exchange settlement, SWIFT/credit-card settlement statements evidencing receipt of consideration from the non-resident buyer
  • Executed SaaS subscription agreement, e-signature service order, and digital delivery logs (API records, download/IP geolocation data) demonstrating that the service was delivered to and consumed outside Canada

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, no digital stamping, and no continuous transaction control (CTC) API filing regime applies in Canada. The Pan-Canadian E-Invoice network (voluntary Peppol/UBL-BIS standard) is being piloted by the federal government for B2B interoperability. CRA accepts paper or common electronic invoice formats. Registrants whose total annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 1,500,000 must file GST/HST returns electronically (GST/HST NETFILE or a certified service provider), and registrants with annual net tax remittances above CAD 50,000 must remit electronically. A valid GST/HST invoice must show the supplier name, the 9-digit Business Number, the date of supply, a description, the consideration, the GST amount charged or a 'tax included' statement, and, where the recipient is a registrant, the recipient's BN and GST/HST registration status.

Although Canada does not impose a statutory foreign-currency repatriation deadline, the 180-day benchmark is applied as an internal documentary-control and audit-readiness standard for zero-rated export claims; CRA requires export and payment evidence to be retained for six years. If CRA denies a zero-rating claim, the full 5% GST/HST becomes payable on the consideration, and CRA may reassess beyond the normal four-year reassessment period (extended to eight years where a return contains a misrepresentation, neglect, or fraud attributable to the taxpayer) and recover previously refunded net tax with prescribed daily compounded interest. Statutory penalties include: (i) a false-statement or omission penalty of the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the net tax understated (ETA s. 280(1)) for GST/HST; (ii) a parallel gross-negligence penalty of the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the tax sought to be avoided (Income Tax Act s. 163(2)) where the T2 return is affected; (iii) criminal prosecution for willful evasion (ETA s. 327 and ITA s. 239) with fines of up to 200% of the tax evaded and terms of imprisonment; and (iv) joint and several director liability for unremitted GST/HST, interest, and penalties under ETA s. 323. CRA audit teams scrutinize export claims for round-tripping, missing CERS declarations, absence of outward shipping evidence, FX receipt mismatches, and same-day invoice/payment patterns, and the registrant bears the burden of proof.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full IMF Article VIII convertibility. Both current-account and capital-account transactions are freely convertible for CAD and foreign currency; there are no surrender, conversion-margin, licensing, or destination restrictions. The CAD has floated since 1970; the Bank of Canada intervenes only in exceptional disorderly-market conditions. Profit-remittance hurdles are tax-driven, not FX-driven: (i) Part XIII withholding tax on ULC dividends to the US parent — 25% statutory, reduced to 5% under US-Canada Income Tax Convention Article X where the US parent is a resident of the US and owns at least 10% of the ULC's voting shares; (ii) monthly CRA remittance of withheld amounts and NR4 information returns; (iii) FINTRAC/EDD reporting on cross-border electronic transfers of CAD 10,000 or more; (iv) Investment Canada Act notification once the ULC commences a new Canadian business — non-discretionary filing, with substantive net-benefit review applying only for non-WTO investors (CAD 5M direct acquisitions) or large WTO acquisitions (2024 enterprise-value threshold CAD 1.365B), both far above a LegalTech SaaS operation; (v) no government approval required for recapitalization, upstream dividends, or intercompany loan repayments.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Zero surrender requirement — Canada imposes no statutory timeline and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. SaaS subscription revenue, software-licence royalties, and other service export proceeds may be retained indefinitely in USD, EUR, or any other currency in any jurisdiction. The only compliance triggers are: (i) physical cross-border currency movements of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported to the CBSA; (ii) electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more are reportable by banks/fintechs to FINTRAC (not by the company); (iii) for Canadian income tax, amounts are computed in CAD using Bank of Canada daily exchange rates under ITA s. 261, with an optional functional-currency election available to qualifying corporations. The ULC should adopt a written FX conversion policy for monthly CRA/GST filings, intercompany settlement, and Part XIII remittance calculations.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). Functional FX administration: Bank of Canada under the Currency Act and Bank of Canada Act; prudential banking supervision: Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/CFT supervisor: FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); inbound investment review: Department of Finance / Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada under the Investment Canada Act. Canada has no separate 'foreign exchange administration' agency or exchange-control licensing body.

Stripe Fit: High

Restricted — Low-to-Medium feasibility. Canada does not recognize the EU/UK 'EMI' licence. Feasibility as a supplementary cross-border payout/collection conduit (e.g., Wise Business, Payoneer, Stripe Treasury-type products): MEDIUM, strictly limited to sub-CAD 10,000 per transaction flows and never as the principal account. Stripe itself must be configured as a merchant processor settling into a Schedule I bank account in the ULC's legal name.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1 million for total transactions with non-residents (services) or CAD 5 million for other transactions; large corporations with gross revenue > CAD 50 million must prepare Master File and Local File.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have specific ESR, but General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and substance-over-form principles apply; CRA may challenge transactions lacking economic substance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Penalty of 50% of tax evaded, plus interest; criminal prosecution with fines up to 200% of tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unpaid corporate tax under Section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act; travel bans and asset freezes possible.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR reassessment, denial of treaty benefits, penalties up to 50% of tax avoided.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for negligence if they knowingly participate in sham arrangements.
AML/CFT Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 500,000 for corporations; criminal penalties up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for penalties if they knowingly fail to comply.
