Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada — federal tax authority; Business Registration Online (BRO) and My Business Account portal. GST/HST is administered exclusively by CRA; Alberta corporate income tax is collected by CRA under a single-administration agreement with the Alberta Ministry of Finance and Service Alberta Corporate Registry.

E-government filing portal

(1) Incorporate the ULC in Alberta through Service Alberta Corporate Registry to obtain the provincial incorporation/registry number; (2) Register with CRA for a nine-digit Business Number (BN) via Business Registration Online or Form RC1, activating T2 (corporate income tax), RT (GST/HST) and RP (payroll) program accounts; (3) For a foreign-owned ULC, provide certified constitutional documents, a Canadian business address, and a duly appointed representative for service of process; (4) Register for GST/HST under the non-resident digital economy rules where taxable digital supplies to Canadian consumers exceed the CAD 30,000 threshold (mandatory as of 1 July 2021); (5) File annual T2 returns with federal and Alberta (provincial) allocation, remitting final balances and installments in CAD.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — (1) Incorporate the ULC in Alberta through Service Alberta Corporate Registry to obtain the provincial incorporation/registry number; (2) Register with CRA for a nine-digit Business Number (BN) via Business Registration Online or Form RC1, activating T2 (corporate income tax), RT (GST/HST) and RP (payroll) program accounts; (3) For a foreign-owned ULC, provide certified constitutional documents, a Canadian business address, and a duly appointed representative for service of process; (4) Register for GST/HST under the non-resident digital economy rules where taxable digital supplies to Canadian consumers exceed the CAD 30,000 threshold (mandatory as of 1 July 2021); (5) File annual T2 returns with federal and Alberta (provincial) allocation, remitting final balances and installments in CAD.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

No domestic check-the-box: an Alberta ULC is automatically a taxable Canadian corporation (entity-level Part I tax; federal 15% non-CCPC general rate plus Alberta 8% equals 23% combined; Part XIII dividend withholding at 25% statutory, treaty-reduced). Pass-through eligibility is a foreign-shareholder concept: a U.S. shareholder can file Form 8832 check-the-box to treat the ULC as a disregarded entity for U.S. tax (hybrid entity), but Canada ignores that election. Available elections include: ETA s. 156 (GST/HST grouping of closely related corporations), ETA s. 211.5 (selected listed financial institution election for financial-service revenue), and ITA s. 216 (non-resident election for Canadian rental income). Canada-U.S. Treaty Article XXIX A restricts ULC treaty benefits where the ULC is fiscally transparent in the U.S.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — small-supplier threshold over the current and four preceding calendar quarters for worldwide taxable supplies made in Canada (zero-rated export supplies count toward the threshold; a ULC exporting digital video-matching services above CAD 30,000 must register for GST/HST); non-resident digital-platform suppliers have the same CAD 30,000 mandatory registration trigger for Canadian consumer taxable supplies, effective 1 July 2021

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods: zero-rated under Schedule VI Part V of the Excise Tax Act where exported by the supplier, delivered to a common carrier for export, or sold to a non-resident for export (normally leaving Canada within 60 days) with documentary evidence per GST/HST Memorandum 17.5.2. Services: zero-rated under Schedule VI Part V s. 7 where supplied to a non-resident and not performed in relation to Canadian real property or unexported goods — real-time video-matching app access, platform subscriptions, and related digital telephony services supplied from Canada to non-residents qualify for 0% GST/HST. The supplier must evidence the customer's non-resident status, offshore delivery/consumption of the digital service, and payment terms.

  • CBSA Customs Export Declaration (Canadian Export Reporting System / CAED) or B13A for controlled or regulated goods
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical export and date of departure
  • Commercial invoice and zero-rating certificate (CRA export evidence per GST/HST Memorandum 17.5.2)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / SWIFT wire record evidencing collection of export proceeds in freely convertible currency

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or mandatory government-authorised continuous transaction control (CTC) e-invoicing API in Canada. CRA permits electronic invoices where the recipient consents; the Pan-Canadian e-Invoice standard (PEPPOL BIS 3.0) is voluntary, with a CRA phased adoption roadmap but no current statutory mandate. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically (GST34 via NETFILE or My Business Account) and payments remitted in CAD to CRA.

CRA denies zero-rating and claws back output GST plus previously refunded input tax credits where the documentation bundle is incomplete. Reassessments within four years (or unlimited for misrepresentation) plus interest; ETA s. 280 penalty of the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of net tax for false statements or omissions; gross-negligence penalties; criminal exposure under ETA s. 331. CBSA Administrative Monetary Penalties up to CAD 25,000 per export-reporting violation. CRA reviews export proceeds received beyond the 180-day benchmark as an indicator of undisclosed foreign receivables or non-export transactions, which can trigger transfer-pricing, deemed-dividend, and foreign-affiliate recharacterization adjustments.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is generally fully convertible for current-account and capital-account transactions. A Canadian Alberta unlimited liability corporation may generally receive foreign capital, pay foreign suppliers, remit dividends, repay shareholder loans, and purchase foreign currency, subject to corporate law, solvency requirements, tax withholding, sanctions, anti-money-laundering controls, beneficial-ownership verification, transfer-pricing rules, and bank-specific transaction monitoring. Dividends must be lawfully declared and paid from available corporate resources under applicable Alberta corporate law. Dividends, interest, royalties, and certain other payments to non-residents may require Canadian withholding tax, subject to applicable treaty relief and prescribed documentation. Cross-border related-party financing must be supported by arm's-length terms, transfer-pricing records, and appropriate thin-capitalization analysis. Foreign ownership is generally permitted, but federally regulated payment activities, sanctions exposure, national-security considerations, and regulated-sector rules may create additional requirements.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no general statutory deadline for repatriating export proceeds and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. Export proceeds may generally remain in Canadian-dollar or foreign-currency accounts, subject to contractual terms, accounting records, tax reporting, sanctions compliance, and ordinary bank monitoring. Payments involving sanctioned jurisdictions or persons may be prohibited or require authorization under Canadian sanctions legislation. Customs, GST/HST, income-tax, transfer-pricing, and export-control obligations remain separate from foreign-exchange convertibility.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada; Canada has no separate foreign exchange administration imposing general capital controls or mandatory currency surrender requirements.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for payment collection and payouts; low as a substitute for a Canadian corporate operating bank account. An offshore electronic-money institution does not automatically obtain the right to provide payment services in Canada. Activities within the federal Retail Payment Activities Act framework may require registration with the Bank of Canada, operational-risk and safeguarding compliance, incident reporting, and regulatory disclosures. The institution must also comply with applicable anti-money-laundering, sanctions, consumer-protection, data, and foreign-jurisdiction requirements. Canadian banks and payment processors may restrict third-party collection, money transmission, marketplace settlement, stored value, or commingling of customer funds. The entity should use a Canadian bank account for corporate capital, payroll, taxes, refunds, chargeback reserves, and settlement reconciliation, and should obtain written confirmation that Apple IAP, Paymentwall, and any other processor permit the proposed merchant-of-record and beneficiary structure.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route equity through a Canadian commercial bank operating account by SWIFT or correspondent-bank wire in the corporation's legal name. The payment narrative should accurately identify the transaction as paid-up capital, share subscription proceeds, or shareholder loan principal, as applicable; Canada does not use a universal statutory FX purpose-code system for ordinary corporate wires. The corporation should preserve wire confirmations, maintain a capital and intercompany-loan ledger, reconcile the receipt to the corporate books, and apply Canadian withholding, transfer-pricing, thin-capitalization, sanctions, and tax-reporting rules where relevant. Do not route capital through personal accounts, unrelated payment processors, or omnibus wallets. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined general federal and Alberta corporate income tax rate (15% federal Part I on grossed-up taxable income plus 8% Alberta general corporate rate). For Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) claiming the small business deduction, the combined rate on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income is 11% (9% federal small business rate plus 2% Alberta small business rate).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under subsection 212(2) of the Income Tax Act, payable by the Canadian corporation on any dividend paid to a non-resident shareholder. An Alberta ULC, despite its unlimited liability structure, remains a resident corporation for Canadian tax purposes, so its distributions are dividends subject to Part XIII withholding.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The federal T2 return must be filed on or before the later of six months after the end of the taxation year and, for a corporation with a non-calendar registered future without specific exception, the six-month anniversary of the fiscal year-end. Payment of balance due is required within two months after fiscal year-end for non-CCPCs and three months for CCPCs eligible for the small business deduction.

Deadline: · Frequency:

The AT1 must be filed within six months after the end of the federal taxation year. Alberta corporate income tax instalments and final balance payments generally follow the federal payment schedule: two months after year-end, or three months for CCPCs entitled to the small business deduction.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no statutory monetary threshold for preparing Local File or Master File documents in advance, but CRA's transfer pricing administration (Transfer Pricing Memorandum TPM-08) expressly expects contemporaneous, complete and accurate documentation to be prepared where the aggregate controlled transactions with a particular non-resident exceed CAD 1,000,000 in a taxation year. The filing of Form T106 (Information Return for Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents) is mandatory whenever the aggregate amount of per year transactions with a non-resident exceeds CAD 1,000,000. For a digital video-matching app operating through an Alberta ULC, all intercompany fees for video content licenses, technology support, user acquisition, and payment processing services must be benchmarked at arm's length and supported with OECD-aligned documentation. CAD 1,000,000 is the operative threshold triggering T106 disclosure and CRA's enhanced documentation expectation.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not administer a stand-alone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime like offshore financial centres; however, the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section 245 of the Income Tax Act defeats transactions that are considered artificial, lack any business purpose, or are designed to obtain treaty benefits without commensurate economic activity. For an Alberta ULC used in a video-chat monetization structure, CRA will scrutinize the entity's payroll, office premises, decision-making, and risk absorption. The Canada-US Treaty includes a Limitation on Benefits article that requires substantive business presence. The unlimited liability feature of the ULC is not itself economic substance; the ULC must be actively managed, pay arm's-length consideration for any Canadian user data and intellectual property inflows, and maintain full corporate governance records.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Under section 239 of the Income Tax Act, tax evasion is punishable by a fine of up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years on indictment, plus civil reassessment and a gross negligence penalty under subsection 163(2) equal to 50% of the understated tax. The CRA may also apply the collection of unpaid taxes against the ULC's assets and directors.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence assisted in the evasion are jointly and severally liable for corporate tax debts under subsection 227.1(1) of the Income Tax Act. In an Alberta ULC, shareholders' unlimited liability for corporate obligations enables the CRA to pursue personal assets for tax liabilities where improper distributions or fraudulent conveyances have occurred. Travel bans can be issued upon indictment, and imprisonment applies to natural persons convicted of evasion.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 247(3) of the Income Tax Act, a transfer pricing penalty of 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment may be imposed when the adjustment exceeds CAD 1,000,000. The CRA will also reassess the ULC's taxable income upwards, disallow excessive deductions, apply interest on the reassessed balance, and impose secondary documentation penalties up to CAD 24,000 per failure to provide a complete and accurate response to a demand for TP documentation.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for TP penalties under section 247, but if the TP adjustment gives rise to unpaid source withholding tax or Part XIII tax, directors can be held personally liable under the withholding-tax director liability regime. For an Alberta ULC, shareholders and directors are liable to creditors for the full debts and liabilities of the corporation; CRA can exercise civil tracing remedies against personal assets where the transfer pricing transactions were structured to strip the ULC of funds.
Shell Company / Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: The CRA may invoke GAAR under section 245 to prevent the treaty benefit and recharacterize distributions as dividends subject to 25% Part XIII withholding. The Canada-US Treaty's Limitation on Benefits clause automatically denies treaty benefits to a shell corporation that is not a recognized resident with substantial business purpose. Application of GAAR can result in a denial of treaty reduction, a reassessment of amounts paid as royalty into deemed dividends, and a gross negligence penalty of 50% of the federal tax payable.

Director personal liability: Because an Alberta ULC is an unlimited liability entity, any shareholder who participates in treaty-shopping using a shell ULC can be personally liable for the corporate obligations to CRA. CRA may pierce the corporate veil under common-law fraudulent conveyance and agency doctrines. Directors face civil restitution for disgorged treaty benefits, and where a financial crime has been committed, travel restrictions and criminal sanctions under the Criminal Code can be imposed.
Anti-Money Laundering and Payment Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, a corporation faces administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 250,000 per violation and criminal fines up to CAD 2,000,000 on conviction. Penalties are also available under Alberta securities and consumer protection statutes if the payment processing is used for unlawful gambling, fraud, or unlicensed money transmission.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are personally liable if they knowingly direct, authorize, or acquiesce in AML non-compliance; the court can order them to pay restitution to the Crown and impose imprisonment up to 5 years. FINTRAC may also issue a statement of objections and the Minister may seek a court order freezing corporate and personal assets. The unlimited liability structure of the ULC means that AML penalties and disgorgement orders can be enforced directly against shareholders' personal estates.
