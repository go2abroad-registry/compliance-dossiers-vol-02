Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — Under the Canadian Income Tax Act, an Alberta ULC is a taxable Canadian corporation resident in Canada. It is subject to federal Part I corporate income tax on worldwide income and, if non-resident controlled, may be subject to Part XIII withholding on dividends or Part XIV branch tax. No domestic passthrough treatment is recognized; the ULC files a T2 corporate return and is assessed at the corporate level.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a 'check-the-box' or elective entity-classification regime under the Income Tax Act. Entity classification is statutory, based on the incorporating act and residency. Available elections include: (a) a fiscal-period election under subsection 256(6) of the Income Tax Act to align the fiscal year of a subsidiary with its parent; (b) an election under subsection 219(1) to treat the ULC as a foreign-owned corporation for branch tax purposes; and (c) for GST/HST, the related-corporation election under section 239 of the Excise Tax Act. In cross-border structures, the owners may make a foreign classification election (e.g., US check-the-box election under IRC 301.7701-3), but that election has no effect on Canadian taxation; Canada will continue to tax the ULC as an entity-level corporation. Hybrid mismatch rules under sections 18.2 and 18.6 of the Income Tax Act may apply to deny deductions arising from hybrid mismatch arrangements.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the 'small supplier threshold' under subsection 123(1) and section 148 of the Excise Tax Act. A ULC carrying on business in Canada must register for GST/HST when its total taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) exceed CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter, or CAD 30,000 over four consecutive calendar quarters. Once registered, the threshold is disregarded.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

The supply of retail securities and CFD brokerage services to a non-resident is a zero-rated supply of a service under section 7 of Part V of Schedule V to the Excise Tax Act, provided (i) the recipient is a non-resident, (ii) the service is not rendered to an individual present in Canada at any time the service is supplied, and (iii) the service does not relate to real property situated in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada, or intangible property that may not be used outside Canada. Because the offshore brokerage model targets non-resident clients and the services are executed outside Canada, the zero-rating applies. The ULC should not treat such supplies as exempt under Part I (financial services) because zero-rating takes priority for exported financial services and allows the recovery of input tax credits (ITCs).

  • Signed brokerage contract (International Client Agreement) identifying the non-resident client and the offshore nature of the securities/CFD services
  • Commercial invoice issued to the non-resident client showing the client's foreign address, stock exchange settlement details, and a specific statement that the supply is zero-rated under Section 7, Part V, Schedule V
  • Evidence of non-resident tax status (tax residency certificate, foreign certificate of incorporation, or valid government-issued identification with a non-Canadian address)
  • Bank wire remittance documentation from the non-resident client (SWIFT MT103, inward remittance certificate, or bank statement showing the foreign originating account)
  • Order execution and trade confirmation records demonstrating that services are rendered and completed outside Canada (server logs, execution venue in foreign exempt market, and client onboarding KYC files)
  • Internal board or compliance memorandum confirming that the service does not relate to Canadian situs property or Canadian-resident individuals

None — Canada does not require real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or transactional tax pre-approval. Taxpayers may issue paper or electronic invoices (PDF, EDI, XML) as long as the mandatory information under subsection 169(1) and regulation 238 of the Excise Tax Act Regulations is included. GST/HST returns are filed electronically via GST/HST NETFILE; the CRA performs compliance audits on a post-filing basis. No API-based continuous transaction control (CTC) or government-monitoring platform is applicable in Alberta during the current filing period.

CRA may reassess the ULC within four years of a notice of assessment (or without time limit if misrepresentation, negligence, or fraud is found) and restore GST at 5% on the brokerage revenues, disallow associated ITCs, and impose interest and penalties. Negligent or fraudulent zero-rating claims attract a gross-negligence penalty of the greater of CAD 250 and 50% of the net GST/HST reduction under subsection 285.1(1). Failure to remit knowingly can lead to criminal charges under section 286. The ULC must maintain complete, verifiable documentation for every zero-rated supply; missing foreign payment proof or a CRA determination that the client was a Canadian resident may trigger clawback of GST and loss of all related ITCs, with retrospective assessment.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current and capital account convertibility under the standalone floating-exchange regime administered by the Bank of Canada and Department of Finance. No exchange control approvals are required for capital injections, dividends, or repatriation of funds. Profit remittance is hindered only by standard non-resident withholding tax obligations (Part XIII Income Tax Act), e.g., 25% on dividends paid to non-residents, subject to treaty reduction. No remittance authorization or Canadian Investment Regulatory Organization (CIRO) approval is required for wire transfers of operational or client-segregated funds.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export-proceeds surrender or mandatory conversion ratio exists under the Currency Act, the Bank of Canada Act, or the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Exporters and licensed securities dealers may freely retain, convert, or repatriate foreign-currency receipts. The only compliance requirement is transaction reporting to FINTRAC for cross-border currency movements exceeding CAD 10,000, supported by a completed Cross-Border Currency and Monetary Instruments Report (E677, CBSA). For offshore brokerage activities, securities transfers involving client funds are classified as financial transactions requiring suspicious transaction reporting under PCMLTFA if structured to avoid the CAD 10,000 threshold.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low-to-Medium feasibility. Canada has no dedicated EMI licence; non-bank cross-border payment providers operate as Money Service Businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC and, in Alberta, must also register as money lenders under the Alberta Consumer Protection Act if offering loans. Offshore digital EMIs cannot operate with full banking powers without being an authorized Canadian Schedule I or Schedule II bank. For segregated client funds and CFB (customer clearing brokerage) purposes, an MSB is viable only as a conduit not as the primary custodian or settlement bank. Client funds must be maintained as a trust account at a regulated deposit-taking institution eligible under CIRO's client identity and segregation rules.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

High-risk foreign-domiciled client funding and wire transfers involving OFAC-listed jurisdictions or high-risk money-laundering zones

Root cause: High-risk foreign-domiciled client funding and wire transfers involving OFAC-listed jurisdictions or high-risk money-laundering zones

Defense evidence: A transactional schema accompanied by (1) all client onboarding AML/KYC documentation, including source-of-funds declarations and proof of corresponding bank references; (2) FINTRAC suspicious transaction reporting protocol; (3) sanction screening log (OFAC, UN, EU, Canadian SEMA) evidencing zero hits; (4) a client jurisdictional restriction policy that excludes prohibited countries and categories; (5) proof of independent AML compliance officer appointment registered with FINTRAC under Form MSB-1.
Commingling of the firm's FX dealing proceeds with client segregated funds in a single account

Root cause: Commingling of the firm's FX dealing proceeds with client segregated funds in a single account

Defense evidence: The bank requires (1) a separate trust account agreement citing the CIRO Client Relationship and Trading accounts framework; (2) monthly internal segregation compliance certificate signed by two directors; (3) in-depth third-party audit report issued by a licensed Canadian public accounting firm covering Receivership and Bankruptcy Act trust obligations; (4) the complete set of client fund ledgers showing a 1:1 ratio and daily reconciliation.
Cross-border securities settlement transactions triggering the Canadian 'international financial business' classification

Root cause: Cross-border securities settlement transactions triggering the Canadian 'international financial business' classification

Defense evidence: Provide (1) legal opinion from Ontario or Alberta counsel confirming that transactions settle in an offshore trading platform and are excluded from 'resident Canadian' dealing; (2) CIRO membership confirmation or a tailored exemptive relief order; (3) details of the segregated custodial banking arrangement at an eligible Canadian trust company; (4) periodic financial statements prepared under IFRS and reconciled with foreign-custodian reports.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% (federal general 15% + Alberta provincial 8%; no CCPC small business deduction is available to a foreign-controlled ULC)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII (ITA s.212(1)) on dividends paid by the Canadian ULC to non-resident shareholders. Canada-US treaty (Article X): 15% for portfolio shareholders; 5% for a US resident corporation owning 10% or more of the voting power, subject to Limitation on Benefits.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 for loan and financing transactions with a non-resident non-arm's-length party per taxation year; CAD 5,000,000 for all other non-arm's-length transactions in aggregate with each non-resident related party; Master File must be prepared when the multinational group reports gross revenue above CAD 50,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a discrete Economic Substance Regulation code, but the CRA applies the general anti-avoidance rule (ITA s.245), the 'mind and management' residence test, and the artificial transaction doctrine. For an offshore securities brokerage operating through an Alberta ULC, economic substance must be demonstrated by locally resident directors, active trading decision-making, segregated client accounts with Canadian banks, physical office premises and a Canadian AML/PCMLTFA compliance team; absence of these indicia risks the structure being disregarded as a sham and the income re-attributed to the beneficial owners.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion - Income Tax Act s.239

Statutory sanction: On indictment: fine up to 200% of the tax evaded plus imprisonment up to 5 years; on summary conviction: fine up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 2 years less a day; civil gross negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax may be added

Director personal liability: Directors are joint and severally liable under ITA s.227.1 for unremitted source deductions and GST; culpable officers may face civil restitution, asset freezes, travel bans and imprisonment upon criminal conviction.
Late or Omitted Tax Filing - ITA s.162 / s.163

Statutory sanction: Late filing penalty of 5% plus 1% per month up to 12 months; gross negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax under s.163(2); daily penalties of CAD 100 for continued information-return defaults up to CAD 24,000; prescribed compound interest on all arrears

Director personal liability: A director authorizing the omission is personally assessed for the unpaid corporate tax and penalties; CRA may garnish personal accounts, impose liens and issue departure prohibitions.
Unregistered Securities Dealing - Alberta Securities Act s.151-153

Statutory sanction: Offence penalties: for an individual, a fine up to CAD 5,000,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years less a day; for a corporation, a fine up to CAD 10,000,000; additional administrative monetary penalties, cease-trade orders and restitution orders by the Alberta Securities Commission

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize unlawful dealing face ASC cease-trade orders, personal prohibition orders, restitution to affected clients and criminal liability on conviction.
AML/CFT and Money Laundering - PCMLTFA / Criminal Code s.462.31

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative penalties up to CAD 2,000,000 per contravention for an organisation and CAD 1,000,000 for an individual; Criminal Code money laundering is indictable with imprisonment up to 10 years

Director personal liability: Directors facilitating laundering are exposed to personal administrative penalties, prohibition orders, asset confiscation and imprisonment for up to 10 years; reckless direction is sufficient for culpability under Canadian money-laundering jurisprudence.
Transfer Pricing Penalties - ITA s.247(3)

Statutory sanction: Penalty equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where reasonable documentation was not prepared; fixed CAD 100 per-day penalties for T106 default up to CAD 24,000; interest and reassessment of the ULC's Part I tax

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly sign fabricated intercompany invoices may be named in personal penalty assessments; the corporate veil may be pierced for restitution of the adjusted tax, interest and penalties.
