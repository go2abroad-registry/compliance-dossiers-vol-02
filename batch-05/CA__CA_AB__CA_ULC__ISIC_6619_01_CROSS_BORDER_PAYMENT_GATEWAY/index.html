Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) is the competent federal tax authority for income tax and GST/HST; Alberta Finance administers Alberta corporate income tax under the Alberta Corporate Tax Act. Primary portals are CRA My Business Account, GST/HST NETFILE, and Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration e-Services.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned Alberta ULC must obtain a nine-digit Business Number (BN) from the CRA by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) either electronically through My Business Account or by mail to the Sudbury Tax Centre. The applicant must provide entity formation documents, foreign registration details, names of directors/officers, Canadian resident agent address, and estimated taxable supplies. The BN is then assigned program accounts: RC for corporate income tax, RT for GST/HST registration, RD if any customs/import/export activity is contemplated. Non-residents may register for GST/HST using the same BN procedure; Form RC1 should be accompanied by a statement of the applicant's worldwide taxable supplies to determine eligibility to register as a small supplier.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box election. An Alberta ULC is always a taxable corporation under the Income Tax Act and must file a T2 corporate return. Its unlimited liability status makes it an eligible entity for U.S. federal tax classification, permitting a Form 8832 election to be treated as a partnership or disregarded entity, but such foreign election does not alter Canadian entity-level status. Domestic statutory elections potentially relevant to the cross-border PSP include the choice of taxation year, functional currency election for qualifying foreign affiliates, and elections under income tax conventions to waive or allocate taxing rights. Canada's hybrid mismatch rules (Income Tax Act ss. 18.4–18.7) apply in respect of instruments or entities that are fiscally transparent in a foreign jurisdiction and operate without an elective disapplication.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

For GST/HST, a non-resident carrying on business in Canada is required to register once worldwide taxable supplies from all jurisdictions exceed CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter or over the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Supplies of financial services that are exempt do not count as taxable supplies for this threshold; however, zero-rated supplies to non-residents are included in the threshold calculation unless an election to treat them as excluded from related-party financial services applies. A person meeting the small supplier threshold may remain unregistered but may voluntarily register for GST/HST if carrying on a commercial activity in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of goods exported from Canada are zero-rated when the supplier obtains and retains documentary evidence that the goods were exported. Services supplied to a non-resident are generally zero-rated when the recipient is a non-resident not registered for GST/HST and the service is not performed in respect of real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada while the service is performed. For the cross-border payment gateway operator, the merchant acquiring, clearing, and settlement services will qualify as zero-rated financial services under Schedule VI, Part IX of the Excise Tax Act only where the service is supplied to a non-resident recipient who is not registered for GST/HST, is not related to a Canadian permanent establishment of the recipient, and is not performed in connection with Canadian-resident cardholders or merchants in a manner that causes the service to be consumed in Canada. The PSP must obtain and retain a declaration of non-residency, a copy of the foreign recipient's registration status, and a contract specifying the place of consumption of the service.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A) for any physical goods exported
  • Bill of Lading or airway bill evidencing export movement
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank SWIFT/payment rail settlement confirmation evidencing receipt of foreign currency
  • Duly executed cross-border service agreement identifying the non-resident recipient and applicable GST/HST treatment
  • GST/HST invoice showing 0% rate, supplier BN, and recipient non-resident status
  • Certificate of residency or equivalent foreign tax authority documentation establishing the recipient's non-resident status

Canada does not operate a real-time tax clearance or continuous transaction control (CTC) system. There is no national mandate for electronic invoicing, digital stamping, or transaction-level fiscal clearance. GST/HST returns are filed periodically (typically annually or quarterly) and must be filed electronically for registrants whose annual taxable supplies exceed established e-filing thresholds. The Pan-Canadian e-invoicing standard built on Peppol is voluntary. The PSP must nonetheless maintain complete electronic records and issue tax invoices in compliance with the Excise Tax Act, including supplier BN, GST/HST registration number, date, description of supply, payable amount, and separate tax amount. CRA may require access to source systems and payment-rail logs during audit.

CRA audits zero-rated export claims with particular scrutiny over payment gateway and financial services because of their digital nature and high volume. Where documentation is insufficient, CRA will reclassify the supply as taxable, disallow input tax credits relating to the export, and claw back any net GST/HST refund with interest calculated from the original filing date. Fraudulent zero-rating claims attract penalties under section 280 of the Excise Tax Act equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the tax that would have been payable or refund claimed, plus interest. Gross negligence or deliberate omission may also lead to criminal prosecution under the Excise Tax Act and income tax offences provisions, and to extended reassessment periods and joint and severable liability for corporate officers. Foreign exchange repatriation outside the prescribed contractual timelines and absence of inward remittance evidence will be treated as an indicator that the export did not occur, triggering retrospective clawback of the zero-rating benefit for the entire audit period.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital account, and Canada accepts IMF Article VIII obligations and the OECD Code of Liberalisation of Capital Movements; therefore no exchange-control authorisation is required for equity injections, intercompany advances, trade settlements, or profit repatriation. The practical repatriation hurdles are taxation-based: Part XIII non-resident withholding tax at a 25% statutory rate (reducible under the Canada-US and Canada-UK treaties), transfer-pricing rules under section 247 of the Income Tax Act (Canada), and thin-capitalisation limits under section 18(4). For a CA_AB ULC, the unlimited-liability share structure and the ability of US shareholders to make a check-the-box disregarded-entity election may create deemed-dividend characterisation, but Canada will impose treaty-based withholding on any remittance characterised as a dividend, and interest payments remain subject to the participating-financing and withholding rules.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender rule and no mandatory foreign-currency conversion ratio; exporters and PSPs may lawfully retain foreign-exchange balances indefinitely in Canadian or offshore accounts. Binding compliance requirements are: (i) FINTRAC cross-border electronic-fund-transfer reporting for each transaction of C$10,000 or more, with records retained for five years; (ii) income-tax recognition of foreign-currency gains and losses on revenue-account receivables under section 39(1.1) and functional-currency elections under section 261 of the Income Tax Act; and (iii) Bank of Canada Lynx participation rules requiring settlement balances in central-bank money, which in practice obliges CAD liquidity buffers for high-value clearing.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central monetary and payments authority, operating the Lynx high-value settlement system, setting the policy interest rate, and acting as lender of last resort. Foreign-exchange policy is coordinated with the Department of Finance Canada; Canada maintains no centralised exchange-control authority or prior-approval regime for capital movements. Federally regulated deposit-taking institutions are supervised by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), while anti-money-laundering and terrorist-financing supervision of payment-service providers is conducted by FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low-to-Medium for a non-resident fintech. Canada does not recognise the EU e-money-institution (EMI) licence and provides no passporting mechanism; an offshore EMI must instead register with FINTRAC as a Money Services Business before conducting payment services in or into Canada, obtain any applicable provincial money-transfer licences, and operate with no direct access to the Lynx high-value settlement rail or Canadian automated clearing infrastructure. Non-bank PSPs can only reach clearing and settlement through sponsored or agent accounts held at a Schedule I bank, face heightened AML/ATF governance and capital expectations, and must maintain a substantive Canadian compliance presence; consequently, reliance on an offshore EMI licence alone is not a viable onboarding path.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound capital injections are executed by SWIFT MT103 customer credit transfers (or MT202 COVER for interbank cover legs) from the shareholder's bank to the ULC's designated Capital Escrow Account at a Schedule I bank in Canada. The wire must state the OECD Balance of Payments purpose code for direct investment and equity contribution in the remittance-information field, be supported by a board-approved capital call and source-of-funds documentation, and be pre-notified to the receiving bank where the entity is a licensed PSP. CAD liquidity is settled over the Bank of Canada Lynx RTGS; USD funds may be cleared through the Canadian bank's US correspondent Fedwire or CHIPS network. FINTRAC EFTR record-keeping applies to any cross-border transfer of C$10,000 or more, and the capital injection must be recorded in the ULC's Canadian tax and accounting ledgers for thin-capitalisation and paid-up-capital tracking.Outbound trade disbursements are executed through: (i) SWIFT MT103 customer credit transfers for open-account supplier payments; (ii) commercial letters of credit and documentary collections governed by UCP600 and eUCP, normally confirmed by a corresponding overseas bank; (iii) CAD high-value settlements over the Bank of Canada Lynx network; and (iv) USD vendor payments over Fedwire or CHIPS through the Canadian clearing bank's US-dollar bulk-exchange programme. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal small business rate on active business income up to CAD 500,000; 38% on the balance

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% on dividends paid to non-resident shareholders

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 50,000,000 for related-party transactions triggering mandatory local file documentation

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Failure to file corporate tax return

Statutory sanction: Penalty of $50 per day with potential prosecution; interest on unpaid taxes

Director personal liability: Directors personally liable for penalties and restitution
Transfer pricing non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Penalty up to $10,000 per transaction or reassessment of income

Director personal liability: Directors personally liable for penalties and restitution
Improper profit repatriation or withholding

Statutory sanction: Penalty of 10% of unpaid tax plus interest; criminal prosecution for evasion

Director personal liability: Directors personally liable for unpaid taxes, fines, and potential imprisonment for willful evasion
Economic substance violation

Statutory sanction: Penalty up to $10,000 or denial of deductions

Director personal liability: Directors personally liable for compliance failures and restitution
