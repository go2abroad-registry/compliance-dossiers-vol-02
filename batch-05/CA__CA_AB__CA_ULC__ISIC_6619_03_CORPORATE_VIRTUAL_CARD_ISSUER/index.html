Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including Business Registration Online, My Business Account, and Represent a Client; Alberta corporate income tax is generally administered through the CRA, while Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration administers specified provincial tax programs. Alberta has no general provincial sales tax.

E-government filing portal

An Alberta ULC obtains a federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA through Business Registration Online or Form RC1, generally after incorporation or registration. The entity must provide its legal name, Alberta registration details, registered office, directors, ownership information, responsible officer, fiscal year-end, and business activities. The BN is used to establish relevant program accounts, including GST/HST (RT), payroll deductions (RP), import-export (RM), and corporate income tax administration where applicable. A foreign corporation carrying on business in Canada must obtain a BN and register for applicable CRA program accounts, generally using Business Registration Online, Form RC1, or CRA non-resident procedures. A GST/HST registration is required when the entity is not a small supplier or when voluntary registration is elected. A separate provincial tax identification is not generally required for Alberta sales tax because Alberta has no general provincial sales tax. Financial-service activities may require additional CRA classification and reporting analysis because qualifying financial services are generally GST-exempt rather than zero-rated.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no general check-the-box election comparable to the United States. An Alberta ULC is generally treated as a corporation and taxed at the corporate level under Canadian law, notwithstanding that its unlimited liability form may be eligible for pass-through or disregarded treatment under the tax law of another country. A ULC may potentially elect for United States federal tax classification under Internal Revenue Code Section 301.7701-3 by filing IRS Form 8832, subject to United States eligibility, ownership, timing, and hybrid-entity mismatch considerations. Canadian anti-hybrid mismatch rules can deny deductions or otherwise neutralize tax benefits arising from specified hybrid arrangements. Canadian corporate tax integration, partnership, trust, amalgamation, rollover, and other statutory elections are transaction-specific and do not convert an Alberta ULC into a generally transparent entity for Canadian income tax purposes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding supplies of capital property and goodwill, measured under the applicable small-supplier rules over the relevant single-quarter and preceding four-calendar-quarter periods. The threshold generally applies to taxable supplies, including zero-rated supplies, but not exempt supplies. A non-resident may be required to register based on carrying on business in Canada and its GST nexus even where the ordinary small-supplier analysis differs. Voluntary registration is generally available where statutory conditions are satisfied.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada may generally be zero-rated where the statutory export conditions under the Excise Tax Act are satisfied, including delivery or shipment outside Canada and retention of evidence establishing export. Services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated only where the applicable Schedule VI conditions are met; the analysis depends on the service type, recipient status, place of performance, use or enjoyment, and exclusions for services relating to Canadian real property, tangible personal property, litigation, or other specified matters. For a corporate virtual-card issuer and spend-management platform, card issuance, account, credit, payment-processing, and related financial services must first be classified under the financial-services rules. Qualifying financial services are generally GST-exempt, not zero-rated, and therefore do not ordinarily generate input tax credits. Taxable ancillary technology, platform, data, support, or administrative services may be taxable or zero-rated only if the specific statutory requirements are met. Export zero-rating requires contemporaneous commercial, contractual, customer-residency, delivery, usage, and payment evidence; non-resident status alone is insufficient.

  • Canada Border Services Agency export declaration or applicable Canadian export reporting record, including Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) filing where required
  • Commercial invoice identifying the supplier, purchaser or consignee, description, value, currency, country of origin, and export terms
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, courier record, or other carrier documentation showing shipment from Canada to a destination outside Canada
  • Packing list and transport or delivery records, where applicable
  • Proof of delivery or importation in the destination jurisdiction, where available
  • Contract, purchase order, customer confirmation, and evidence that the recipient is outside Canada or that the statutory export condition is satisfied
  • Accounting records reconciling the exported supply to the GST return and zero-rated revenue ledger
  • Banking and payment records supporting the commercial transaction; an inward remittance certificate is not a general Canadian statutory requirement
  • For exported services, service agreements, invoices, recipient-location evidence, usage or enjoyment analysis, work logs, access records, and proof that statutory exclusions do not apply

Canada has no general real-time GST invoice-clearance, mandatory digital-stamping, or universal business-to-business e-invoicing mandate. GST registrants must issue documentary evidence containing prescribed information, retain books and records in an acceptable electronic or paper form, and support input tax credit claims with compliant invoices, receipts, agreements, or other prescribed evidence. CRA electronic filing and online account systems are mandatory for specified categories and transactions, but they do not constitute continuous transaction controls or pre-clearance of invoices. Electronic records must remain reliable, accessible, readable, and auditable for the applicable retention period. Large corporations and specified financial institutions may have additional electronic filing, information-return, and GST reporting obligations.

Canada does not generally impose a statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds or require an inward remittance certificate as a condition of GST zero-rating. CRA may reassess GST where zero-rating is unsupported, the supply is exempt or taxable under a different rule, the recipient or place of supply is mischaracterized, or required evidence is absent. The reassessment may include GST payable, denial or repayment of input tax credits and refunds, arrears interest, and applicable administrative penalties. Gross-negligence penalties can generally equal 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit or refund attributable to the conduct; other failure-to-file, failure-to-remit, false-statement, or document-related penalties may also apply. Knowingly false or deceptive statements may expose the entity and responsible persons to prosecution, fines, and imprisonment under applicable Canadian tax legislation. Export-reporting violations may trigger Canada Border Services Agency Administrative Monetary Penalty System sanctions, cargo or release consequences, and other customs enforcement. Where a refund was improperly obtained, CRA may recover the refund, interest, and penalties through reassessment and collection proceedings.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible. Canada accepts IMF Article VIII obligations and maintains a free-floating CAD with no approval, licensing, or quantitative restriction on current-account or capital-account transactions, including equity injections, intercompany loans, dividends, interest, royalties, and service fees. Profit remittance from the Alberta ULC carries no FX-control hurdle; the controlling constraint is tax: Part XIII Canadian withholding tax applies to dividends paid to the US parent at a statutory rate of 25%, treaty-reduced to 5% where the US parent company owns at least 10% of the ULC's voting shares, with eligibility evidenced via Canada Revenue Agency Form NR301 and FATCA/CRS self-certifications. For US tax purposes the ULC is typically treated as a disregarded entity under a check-the-box election (Form 8832), but Canada continues to tax the ULC as a resident Canadian corporation; remittance is therefore structured as dividends, and the Part XIV branch tax applicable to non-resident corporations operating Canadian branches does not attach to a Canadian-incorporated ULC. The practical remittance hurdle is bank counterparty due diligence and FINTRAC reporting, not exchange-control permission.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender obligation, no mandatory conversion ratio, no foreign-currency retention limit, and no statutory repatriation deadline. The virtual card issuer may freely retain USD and multi-currency settlement balances, convert at spot or forward rates, or net export receivables against payables. Surveillance-only obligations apply: FINTRAC International EFT Reports for every incoming and outgoing cross-border electronic funds transfer of CAD 10,000 or more, Large Cash Transaction Reports for cash equivalents of CAD 10,000 or more, and the issuer's card-network settlement reconciliation. There is no requirement to convert export receipts into CAD within any statutory timeframe.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (central bank and monetary authority); the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) acts as the prudential regulator of federally incorporated banks; Payments Canada operates the national clearing and settlement infrastructure (Lynx for high-value payments, ACSS for retail clearing, and the forthcoming Real-Time Rail), with final settlement in Bank of Canada accounts; FINTRAC administers the anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing regime under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); and the Bank of Canada supervises payment service providers under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA). Canada maintains no dedicated Foreign Exchange Administration and no transaction-level exchange-control approval body.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 for mandatory T106 Information Return reporting; comprehensive OECD-aligned Master File / Local File documentation is recommended by the CRA when aggregate cross-border intercompany transactions exceed CAD 10,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain standalone Economic Substance Regulations. The CRA applies the General Anti-Avoidance Rule under s.245, common law substance-over-form, and treaty Limitation-of-Benefits or Principal Purpose Test provisions to deny tax benefits where an Alberta ULC lacks real commercial activity, employees, office, or risk-bearing functions in Canada.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Misreporting

Statutory sanction: Gross negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax, plus late-filing penalties and compounded daily interest. Criminal prosecution under s.239(1) of the Income Tax Act exposes the corporation to fines up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment of controlling officers for up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST under s.227.1; civil restitution may be ordered for diverted corporate funds; travel bans may be imposed by a court as a bail condition or sentencing measure.
Artificial Conduit / Shell Company

Statutory sanction: CRA reassessment applying GAAR to deny tax benefits; transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment under s.247(4); denial of treaty benefits; reassessments may extend beyond the normal limitation period where misrepresentation is established.

Director personal liability: Courts may pierce the corporate veil if the ULC is a facade; directors can be held personally liable for the corporation's unpaid taxes and required to restore diverted assets; travel bans and asset freezes are available in international tax fraud investigations.
Unlicensed MSB / AML Compliance Failure

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 1 million per violation for corporations under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act; criminal prosecution can result in fines up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years; provincial licensing or payment-network approvals may be revoked.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who knowingly participate in an unlicensed money services business are personally liable for AML penalties; courts may issue travel bans pending investigation; assets traceable to money laundering are subject to seizure, and fines are not dischargeable in bankruptcy.
