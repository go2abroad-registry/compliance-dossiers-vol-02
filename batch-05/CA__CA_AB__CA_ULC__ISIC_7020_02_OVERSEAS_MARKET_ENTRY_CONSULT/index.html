Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; official portal: CRA My Business Account and Business Registration Online (BRO) at canada.ca; Alberta provincial corporate income tax is administered by CRA under the Alberta Corporate Tax Collection Agreement.

E-government filing portal

For a foreign-controlled or foreign-incorporated party establishing an Alberta ULC: (1) obtain an Alberta Corporate Registry file number through incorporation of the ULC or extra-provincial registration; (2) apply to CRA for a federal nine-digit Business Number (BN) via Business Registration Online or Form RC1; select the RP (corporate income tax) and RT (GST/HST) program accounts; (3) attach the Alberta incorporation certificate and, if non-resident directors or shareholders are involved, any CRA-prescribed identity and residence evidence; (4) CRA issues the BN and confirms the corporate income tax account; (5) register for GST/HST using Form GST1 or through the BRO questionnaire if the entity is not a small supplier; (6) activate My Business Account to complete registration, set up direct deposit for refunds, and file returns. The BN is the sole Canadian taxpayer identifier and is also used for Alberta corporate income tax reporting; no separate provincial TIN is issued.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — the Alberta ULC is a taxable Canadian corporation for domestic Income Tax Act purposes, subject to federal and Alberta corporate income tax, and is not a pass-through entity in Canada; however, it is eligible to be treated as a pass-through or disregarded entity for U.S. federal income tax purposes under check-the-box, giving the ULC hybrid characteristics.

Available election forms

Canada has no domestic check-the-box regime. The ULC hybrid status is managed through: (a) an IRS Form 8832 entity-classification election electing partnership or disregarded-entity status for U.S. tax owners; (b) a treaty-based election under Article IV(7) of the Canada-U.S. Tax Convention (Fifth Protocol) that fixes the ULC as a resident of Canada and not fiscally transparent for treaty benefits; (c) hybrid-mismatch disclosure and relief rules under Income Tax Act sections 12.6 to 12.8, including the specified-entity notice mechanism that neutralizes duplicate foreign-tax credits, deduction-without-inclusion, and double-deduction outcomes; and (d) where applicable, CRA administrative elections governing provincial allocation of taxable income, for example the Alberta share determination election on Schedule 4.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000. A person, including an Alberta ULC supplying consultancy services, must register for GST/HST once taxable supplies made in Canada (including zero-rated supplies) exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or, if not already registered, over the preceding four calendar quarters (ETA section 240; small-supplier threshold defined in ETA section 148(1)). The threshold applies to taxable supplies made in Canada, not worldwide supplies; once exceeded, registration is mandatory.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of foreign market-entry consultancy services (ISIC 7020 / NAICS 541611) is zero-rated under section 23, Part V, Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act when supplied to a non-resident person who is not a GST/HST registrant, provided the supply is not in respect of real property or goods situated in Canada and is not otherwise excluded (for example, a personal performance service). CRA administrative policy classifies management, advisory, and consultancy services as zero-rated when performed for and delivered to non-residents. The ULC must obtain and retain documentary evidence of the recipient's non-residence and non-registration status, such as a contract showing a foreign address, a foreign tax identification number, bank data, or a certificate of incorporation, and must evidence offshore delivery of the consulting output; partial work performed in Canada does not deny zero-rating if the supply is made to the non-resident and is not for use in Canada by a Canadian resident portion of the recipient's business.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A / CBSA Automated Export Declaration) where the zero-rated supply includes physical goods leaving Canada
  • Bill of Lading or airway waybill evidencing physical export of goods from Canada for goods transactions; for consultancy services, a signed project-delivery report, work log, or offshore-client meeting record demonstrating that the supply is performed for and delivered to the foreign market
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank wire (SWIFT MT103) or credit advice evidencing receipt of payment from the non-resident and the foreign-currency denominated settlement of the export invoice
  • Non-resident tax status evidence: foreign tax identification number, certificate of incorporation in the recipient jurisdiction, and a signed declaration that the recipient is not registered for GST/HST and is not resident in Canada

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, tax attachment, digital stamping, or CRA pre-clearance of invoices exists in Canada. GST/HST invoices must satisfy the prescribed content requirements of Excise Tax Act section 221, including registrant name, Business Number, date, taxable amount, and GST paid or payable, and may be issued electronically. CRA supports voluntary e-invoicing through the Pan-Canadian Trust Framework and PEPPOL interoperability, but continuous transaction controls are not implemented. Registrants must retain all books and records in an electronic or machine-readable format and provide them to CRA upon demand.

CRA audits export zero-rating claims by cross-checking customs records, service delivery documentation, and banking data against the GST/HST return. A claim lacking timely export evidence is disallowed; the supplier is reassessed for 5% GST on the invoice with compound interest, plus an Excise Tax Act section 280 penalty of up to 25% of the understated net tax (minimum CAD 250) where false statements or gross negligence are established. Deliberate fabrication of export documents or use of shell non-resident entities can trigger unlimited reassessment, clawback of previously paid GST refunds, denial of the foreign supplier rebate, criminal liability under ETA section 327 with fines up to 25% of the tax evaded or up to CAD 100,000 and imprisonment of up to five years, and director/executive liability assessments under ETA section 323 where the corporate entity is insolvent.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible. Canada has maintained no exchange controls since repeal of the Foreign Exchange Control Act in 1951 and is an IMF Article VIII member. No licensing or prior approval distinguishes current-account versus capital-account transactions; profit repatriation via ULC dividends is free from exchange-control hurdles. The only fiscal frictions are Part XIII non-resident dividend withholding tax (25% statutory, treaty-reducible, with ULC dividend-recharacterization risk under hybrid-entity rules) and bank-level FINTRAC electronic funds transfer (EFT) reporting for cross-border wires of C$10,000 or more.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No surrender or mandatory-conversion regime exists in Canada. Export and consulting-fee proceeds may be retained indefinitely in foreign currency, whether onshore or offshore; no statutory timeline or conversion ratio applies. The sole compliance burden is FINTRAC EFT reporting for each cross-border transfer of C$10,000 or more within a 24-hour period, plus Statistics Canada balance-of-payments statistical reporting. These requirements are informational only and do not restrict holding or deployment of export receipts.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada), Payments Canada, and the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC). No separate foreign-exchange administration body exists; prudential and AML oversight is allocated across these institutions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Wise Business (Wise Canada Inc.) is the designated B2B rail and is technically viable but legally constrained: Wise holds no OSFI banking licence and operates as a FINTRAC-registered money services business (MSB), safeguarding client funds at Schedule I banks without CDIC deposit insurance. Feasibility is High for low-value, high-volume cross-border consulting disbursements and fee collection in 40+ currencies via local clearing, but Medium for high-value capital flows because Wise lacks Canadian escrow or trust account capabilities and cannot host a capital escrow account. The ULC must complete Wise business validation, including UBO disclosure, registry extract, and linkage to a verified Schedule I operating account.

Funds intended for staged deployment should first clear through a dedicated Capital Escrow Account at ATB Financial, released only upon Alberta Corporate Registry verification and a board resolution. Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

0% at the ULC entity level due to pass-through treatment for Alberta Unlimited Liability Corporations

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Not applicable; distributions from ULC treated as partnership income with no withholding tax

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 500,000 for related-party transactions requiring local file documentation

Economic substance scope: No dedicated Economic Substance Regulations; compliance enforced via GAAR, transfer pricing adjustments, and CFC rules

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Fines up to CAD 5 million or imprisonment up to 2 years for individuals; corporate penalties up to CAD 25 million

Director personal liability: Directors personally liable for willful misrepresentation; potential criminal charges, restitution orders, and director disqualifications
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR recharacterization; tax reassessment with penalties of 10-50% of tax benefit

Director personal liability: Personal liability for tax underpayments, possible imprisonment for fraudulent schemes, and travel restrictions under certain acts
AML Violations

Statutory sanction: Fines up to CAD 500,000 or imprisonment up to 5 years under Proceeds of Crime Act

Director personal liability: Directors can face personal criminal liability, fines, and disqualification from corporate roles
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Penalties of 10-50% of the tax shortfall; interest on underpayments

Director personal liability: Directors personally liable for authorizing non-compliant positions; potential civil and criminal penalties
