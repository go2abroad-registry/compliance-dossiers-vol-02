Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA); primary online services include CRA My Business Account and Business Registration Online (BRO). Alberta corporate-law registration is administered separately by Alberta Registry agents and is not a tax registration.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned Alberta ULC generally obtains a CRA Business Number (BN), a nine-digit identifier, and applicable program accounts. Incorporation or registration may generate a BN; otherwise apply through BRO or submit CRA Form RC1. Register for a corporation income tax program account (RC) and, when required, a GST/HST program account (RT). A non-resident carrying on business in Canada should apply for the required BN and accounts through BRO or the CRA non-resident registration process. Provide the entity's legal name, Alberta incorporation or registration details, registered address, activities, fiscal year-end, directors, and authorized representative information. A BN does not itself establish tax residence or satisfy all filing obligations.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

An Alberta ULC is a Canadian corporation and is generally taxed as a corporation under the Income Tax Act (Canada); it is not a Canadian pass-through entity and has no Canadian check-the-box election to become a partnership or disregarded entity. A qualifying ULC may be treated as disregarded or as a partnership for United States federal income tax purposes by an eligible entity election under US Treasury Regulations, but that election does not alter its Canadian classification and may produce cross-border hybrid-mismatch consequences. Canadian corporate residence, provincial allocation, and any treaty relief require separate analysis.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters for a small supplier, subject to statutory aggregation and related-person rules. A non-resident carrying on business in Canada may be required to register under the regular regime without relying on the small-supplier threshold. Separate simplified-regime rules generally use a CAD 30,000 threshold over a 12-month period for specified cross-border digital products, services, and goods supplied to Canadian consumers.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods may be zero-rated when the statutory export conditions are met and the supplier retains evidence that the goods were exported. Services supplied to a non-resident may be zero-rated under the applicable Excise Tax Act provisions, subject to exclusions, including specified services relating to real property situated in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada, and certain services performed in Canada in respect of goods. Carbon-accounting or ESG advisory services are not automatically zero-rated: determine the recipient's residence, the actual recipient and use of the service, and whether an exclusion applies. Zero-rated supplies remain taxable supplies for registration and input tax credit purposes. Refunds are generally claimed through input tax credits on required GST/HST returns, subject to documentation and statutory limitation periods.

  • Export declaration or other applicable Canada Border Services Agency export reporting evidence, where required
  • Commercial invoice, purchase order, and shipping or transport records identifying the goods and foreign destination
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, courier record, or other carrier proof of export, as applicable
  • Evidence of foreign recipient and delivery outside Canada
  • For exported services, contract, invoices, proof of recipient non-residence, service records, and evidence supporting the applicable zero-rating provision
  • Bank or payment records supporting the transaction; an Inward Remittance Certificate is not a generally prescribed Canadian GST export document

Canada has no general federal real-time e-invoice clearance, tax API submission, or digital-stamping mandate for ordinary GST supplies. GST registrants must provide prescribed invoice or receipt information when required and retain books and records in an accessible, readable form; electronic records must be available to the CRA on request. Certain public-sector procurement or contractual arrangements may impose separate electronic invoicing requirements.

Canada imposes no general foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds. The CRA may deny zero-rating or input tax credits where statutory conditions or supporting records are not established, assess tax, interest, and applicable penalties, and reassess within the applicable limitation period or later where statutory exceptions apply, including misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, wilful default, or fraud. False statements or omissions may attract penalties under the Income Tax Act or Excise Tax Act, including gross-negligence penalties where applicable; fraud may also result in prosecution. No fixed export-zero-rating penalty or automatic clawback applies solely because an export claim is challenged.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital account, consistent with Canada's long-standing IMF Article VIII obligations. There are no exchange-control permits, surrender rules, or repatriation restrictions on capital injections, dividends, interest, royalties, or trade proceeds. A non-resident shareholder of an Alberta ULC may remit profits freely, subject only to Part XIII withholding tax under the federal Income Tax Act (statutory 25% on dividends, reduced by applicable tax treaty — e.g., 5% under the Canada–US Treaty for a 90%-owned subsidiary, 15% otherwise) and to standard commercial banking documentation. The ULC's partnership-style pass-through eligibility does not alter the Part XIII analysis, and no Bank of Canada or CRA advance approval is required for any remittance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline, no minimum conversion ratio, and no mandatory surrender of export proceeds or any other foreign-currency receipts. An exporter may retain foreign currency (USD, EUR, etc.) indefinitely in a Schedule I bank foreign-currency account, convert to CAD at a commercially negotiated spot rate, or apply the proceeds directly to foreign trade payables. FINTRAC EFT reporting applies only to record-keeping thresholds (CAD 10,000 or more per transaction), not to exchange-control conversion.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) — monetary authority and lender of last resort; banking prudential supervision is delegated to OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions) under the Bank Act; AML/CFT administration is vested in FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Canada operates a free-floating exchange-rate regime and maintains no foreign-exchange control authority; the Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Rating: Low for primary treasury; Medium only for ancillary cross-border payout corridors. Canada does not issue EU/UK-style Electronic Money Institution (EMI) licences. Offshore digital EMIs cannot obtain a Bank Act Schedule I licence, cannot hold demand deposits at the Bank of Canada, and lack direct access to Canadian clearing systems (Lynx, ACSS, Real-Time Rail). A foreign EMI may at best sponsor a Canadian bank account or register as a FINTRAC money services business (MSB) and, where applicable, as a Retail Payment Service Provider (RPSP) under the Bank of Canada-administered Retail Payment Activities Act. For an Alberta ULC, an unlicensed offshore EMI is not viable for payroll, CRA tax remittances, or LC operations; Canadian tax obligations must settle through a Canadian bank or an MSA/RPSP-approved PSP.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Equity injections from a non-resident parent or shareholder are executed by SWIFT MT103 (or MT202/MT202COV where an intermediary bank is used) to the ULC's CAD account at a Schedule I bank. The beneficiary account uses the Canadian institution number (3 digits), transit/branch number (5 digits), and account number (e.g., RBC institution '003', Calgary transit '000XX'). The payment narrative must state 'Shareholder capital contribution / equity injection into [ULC legal name]' to prevent misclassification. No Canadian exchange-control filing or CRA clearance is required; the receiving bank records the transaction for FINTRAC EFT reporting when the value is CAD 10,000 or more. The ULC must book the receipt as stated capital or contributed surplus, supported by a board resolution, an updated share register, and, where shares are issued, a certificate of allotment filed with the Alberta Corporate Registry. USD-denominated injections may be converted at the bank's spot rate or credited to a USD settlement account and cleared through CLS/CHIPS; no statutory conversion into CAD is required.Outbound settlements use four principal rails: (1) SWIFT MT103 for cross-border supplier, client, and carbon-credit settlement payments, with full beneficiary account details and descriptive remittance information; (2) Lynx, Canada's high-value payment system (successor to LVTS), for CAD payments of CAD 500,000 or more, providing real-time finality; (3) the Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS) for lower-value CAD ACH-style credits and pre-authorized debits; and (4) commercial letters of credit opened or confirmed by a Schedule I bank in Calgary or Edmonton under UCP 600 and ISBP 745, together with documentary collections under URC 522 when appropriate. For USD settlements, Canadian Schedule I banks clear through their US branch networks and CHIPS/Fedwire membership. Audit is compulsory if shares are distributed to the public, if shareholders holding 5% or more of voting shares demand an audit, or if the Directors cannot establish the unanimous-consent waiver. The ULC's unlimited liability structure does not eliminate this statutory audit requirement unless the unanimous shareholder waiver is validly obtained each year.
