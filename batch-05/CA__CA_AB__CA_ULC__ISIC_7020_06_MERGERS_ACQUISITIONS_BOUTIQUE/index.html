Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), acting through the Business Registration Online portal, with provincial incorporation and corporate registry administration by Alberta Corporate Registry (Service Alberta) and Corporations Canada for federal matters.

E-government filing portal

For an Alberta Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC), the foreign parent must first complete incorporation under the Alberta Business Corporations Act through the Alberta Corporate Registry. The resulting entity receives an Alberta Corporation Access Number and Certificate of Incorporation. The ULC must then obtain a federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA and register for the appropriate program accounts: Corporation Income Tax (RC0001), GST/HST (RT0001), Payroll (RP0001), and Import/Export (RM0001), as applicable. Non-resident corporations may register by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Program Accounts) through the CRA's Business Registration Online service, submitting the certificate of incorporation, constitutional documents, director information, and, where required, a Canadian representative for service of documents. Registration is required immediately upon commencement of Canadian commercial activity, GST/HST registration obligations, or payroll remittance obligations.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation - Under the Canadian Income Tax Act, an Alberta ULC is a corporation and is taxed at the entity level. Although a wholly owned ULC may be treated as a disregarded entity for U.S. federal income tax purposes under the check-the-box regime, Canadian tax law does not permit any pass-through or transparency election for an incorporated ULC; the ULC must file a Canadian corporation income tax return and pay corporate income tax on its taxable income.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a check-the-box election under the Income Tax Act. Statutory elections relevant to an Alberta ULC include: subsection 85(1) rollover on transfers of property to a corporation, section 86 share-for-share exchange reorganization, section 87 or 88 amalgamation/wind-up elections, and the GST/HST election for closely related corporations under subsection 156(1) of the Excise Tax Act to treat qualifying supplies between group members as not being a taxable supply. Hybrid mismatch rules under section 18.2 and 18.3 of the Income Tax Act impose special deductions or inclusions where a hybrid entity is used for cross-border arbitrage; ULCs used in U.S.-Canada structures require careful evaluation of the Canada-U.S. Treaty and the hybrid mismatch rules.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 - A person supplying taxable supplies (other than zero-rated supplies) in Canada in the course of a commercial activity must register for GST/HST once the total worldwide gross revenue from taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated supplies) from the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters exceeds CAD 30,000. A non-resident carrying on commercial activity in Canada is subject to the same threshold, with special rules for supplies of real property, incorporeal property, and electronically supplied services.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of goods is zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act when the supplier exports the goods in accordance with prescribed conditions, including delivery by common carrier or post to a destination outside Canada. Services supplied to a non-resident person are generally zero-rated where the service is supplied to a non-resident who is not registered under the GST/HST and the service is not performed in Canada, or if performed in Canada is in direct relation to real property or tangible personal property located outside Canada or to intangible personal property the use of which is wholly outside Canada. The M&A advisory boutique must maintain contemporaneous evidence that the recipient is a non-resident and that the services are not consumed within Canada. 'Offshore services' may qualify as zero-rated if the service is rendered to a non-resident and is not taxable in Canada under the place-of-supply rules.

  • Commercial Invoice and Foreign Purchase Order identifying the buyer and the exported goods or services
  • Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) Export Declaration (B13 form) or electronic export reporting through Canada's Export Reporting System for goods exceeding applicable value thresholds
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical movement of goods outside Canada
  • Common carrier delivery record or customs stamp confirming export completion
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank settlement confirmation evidencing foreign consideration received in Canadian currency

Canada does not operate a real-time e-invoice clearance mandate, API-based fiscal stamping regime, or digital continuity obligation comparable to Latin American or certain EU systems. Registered GST/HST suppliers must issue standard tax invoices or simplified invoices containing prescribed information (supplier name, BN, GST/HST registration number, transaction date, consideration, and tax payable). GST/HST returns must be filed electronically through CRA's GST/HST NETFILE, Telefile, or My Business Account when net tax exceeds specified thresholds or for certain large registrants; however, there is no requirement for pre-clearance of invoices by the tax authority.

CRA audits export zero-rating claims by requiring complete documentary evidence that the goods left Canada and that services were provided to a non-resident for use wholly outside Canada. Failure to substantiate an export claim results in reassessment of the 5% GST/HST as uncollected tax payable, plus interest and penalties. Knowingly making a false statement in a GST/HST return is an offence under section 327-328 of the Excise Tax Act, carrying penalties of up to CAD 25,000 or, on indictment, CAD 100,000, and liability to restore the tax. Additionally, under section 280 the CRA may impose a penalty of CAD 100 to CAD 1,000, and the Income Tax Act's false-statement penalties under subsection 163(2) can apply where an incorrect tax return is filed. Fraduent export schemes may also be prosecuted under the Criminal Code of Canada as tax fraud, subject to imprisonment and substantial monetary sanctions. The clawback period for a reassessment is generally four years from the original assessment, but the limitation period is extended indefinitely where misrepresentation, fraud, or gross negligence is established.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible and freely floating under Canada's Article VIII obligations in the IMF Articles of Agreement. No exchange controls, no capital account restrictions, no approval requirements for non-resident investment, and no limits on the amount or timing of profit remittance by an Alberta ULC. Dividends are remittable in CAD or foreign currency without central bank consent; the only structural hurdle is statutory dividend withholding (25%, treaty-reduced, and potentially eliminated via the ULC dividend-as-interest election under subsection 112(2.2) of the Income Tax Act).

Export proceeds surrender rules

None applicable. Canada imposes no statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no surrender requirement for export proceeds, advisory success fees, or any foreign-currency receivables. The ULC may indefinitely retain USD, GBP, EUR, or other proceeds in domestic or offshore accounts; repatriation strategy is solely a treasury policy matter, subject to arm's-length transfer pricing documentation for related-party inflows.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as central monetary authority; prudential banking regulation by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/ATF supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); securities licensing for the M&A advisory boutique by the Alberta Securities Commission (ASC) under the Securities Act (Alberta).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium Restricted. Canada has no EU/UK-style electronic money institution (EMI) license; the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) requires payment service providers to register with the Bank of Canada for supervision. Offshore EMIs are not passportable into Canada and may hold ULC funds only through a Canadian licensed bank correspondent. The ULC's Medium-Risk-Enhanced profile makes an EMI unsuitable as a primary treasury rail due to lack of CDIC deposit insurance, FINTRAC wire-traceability expectations, and constrained success-fee acceptance. Acceptable use: contingent expense cards or minor disbursements, never for closing or success-fee settlement.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined federal and Alberta general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal plus 8% Alberta. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access an 11% combined small-business rate on eligible active business income within the applicable business limit, comprising 9% federal and 2% Alberta.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to a treaty reduction. Common treaty outcomes include 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting the ownership threshold, 15% for other qualifying corporate or individual portfolio holdings, and 25% where treaty relief is unavailable. The Canada-United States treaty commonly provides 5% for a qualifying 10% corporate shareholder and 15% otherwise.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 and AT1 are generally due within six months after the end of every taxation year. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end. A Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally obtain a three-month balance-due date if it meets the statutory conditions, including the applicable taxable-income limit and association rules. Instalments are due monthly unless the corporation qualifies for quarterly instalments.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold that eliminates the contemporaneous-documentation requirement under Income Tax Act section 247(4). The T106 reporting threshold is generally CAD 1,000,000 of total reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents. A Canadian corporation may be required to prepare a Local File-style analysis and, where applicable under the OECD country-by-country framework, a Master File or equivalent group information. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year. Documentation must generally be provided to CRA within three months of a written request.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a single general economic-substance regulation equivalent to the UAE ESR regime. Substance is nevertheless relevant under the Income Tax Act, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership analysis, transfer pricing, debt-versus-equity characterization, foreign-affiliate rules and the principal-purpose test in applicable treaties. CRA may challenge an Alberta ULC that lacks personnel, decision-making, risk control, premises, records or commercial functions in Canada, particularly where it invoices foreign group entities for M&A services. Transfer-pricing analysis must reflect actual functions, assets and risks, including origination, deal execution, regulatory work, personnel compensation, client ownership, financing and success-fee risk.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act offences may result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years. False or deceptive statements may attract separate penalties, reassessment, interest and prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize, assent to or participate in the conduct may face prosecution, fines and imprisonment. Corporate status does not protect deliberate criminal conduct. Directors may also face civil claims, restitution orders and asset recovery proceedings.
Failure to Remit Payroll or Part XIII Withholding

Statutory sanction: Withholding tax, arrears interest, failure-to-remit penalties, late-information-return penalties and, in serious cases, prosecution. Unremitted source deductions are generally deemed trusts in favour of the Crown.

Director personal liability: Directors can be assessed personally for unremitted deemed-trust amounts if statutory due-diligence and timing requirements are satisfied against them. Resignation does not automatically eliminate liability for amounts arising during the directorship.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Reassessment under section 247, denial or recharacterization of deductions, withholding-tax exposure, a potential 10% transfer-pricing penalty, interest and general penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil claims for breach of fiduciary or statutory duties where transfer-pricing manipulation harms the corporation or creditors. Criminal exposure arises where false statements or intentional evasion is proven.
Shell Company and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Treaty relief may be denied under beneficial-ownership, limitation-on-benefits, principal-purpose or anti-avoidance rules. CRA may reassess tax, impose withholding obligations, penalties and interest, and apply the general anti-avoidance rule.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally exposed to civil claims and, where they knowingly participate in fraudulent tax conduct, criminal sanctions. Unlimited-liability characteristics of the ULC must be reviewed under its articles and applicable Alberta corporate law; shareholder exposure should not be assumed to be limited.
Unlimited Liability Corporation Insolvency Risk

Statutory sanction: Potential repayment of unlawful distributions, oppression or creditor remedies, bankruptcy or insolvency proceedings, tax reassessment and director-liability claims under applicable corporate and insolvency legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability for statutory breaches, improper distributions, unpaid deemed-trust amounts, employee-source deductions and knowing participation in fraudulent conveyances. Shareholder liability must be analyzed against the ULC's articles, governing statute and insolvency circumstances.
AML, Sanctions and Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, reporting failures, production orders, asset seizure and criminal prosecution under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and Criminal Code. Sanctions violations may result in substantial fines and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers may be prosecuted for knowing participation, wilful blindness or obstruction. Corporate indemnification may not protect against criminal fines or deliberate misconduct.
GST/HST Misclassification

Statutory sanction: Assessment of uncollected tax, denial of input tax credits, interest, gross-negligence or false-statement penalties and late-filing penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for fraud, knowing participation or unremitted deemed-trust amounts, subject to statutory limitations and due-diligence defences.
Corporate Residency and Permanent Establishment Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Canadian corporate income tax, provincial tax, payroll and GST/HST assessments; treaty denial; penalties and interest. Foreign jurisdictions may also assert permanent-establishment or corporate-residence tax.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil and criminal consequences for knowingly filing false residency or permanent-establishment positions and may be subject to cross-border information requests and asset-preservation orders.
Unlawful Profit Repatriation

Statutory sanction: Recovery of improper distributions, withholding-tax assessments, denial of deductions, transfer-pricing adjustments, penalties, interest and possible shareholder or recipient assessments.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for unlawful distributions, breach of fiduciary duties, unpaid deemed-trust amounts and knowing participation in fraudulent transfers.
