Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA); full administrative authority for federal corporate income tax and federal GST through My Business Account and Business Registration Online (BRO) portal; Alberta corporate income tax is administered by the CRA on behalf of the Province of Alberta under the Tax Collection Agreement, with Alberta Tax and Revenue Administration retaining audit and assessment oversight for provincial taxpayer services

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned Alberta ULC obtains a federal Business Number (BN) with a separate GST/HST program account by submitting Form RC1 through CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) or by paper filing; the non-resident shareholder or ULC must appoint a representative for service of process and returns using Form RC59 and must maintain books and records in Canada; issuance of the BN and GST/HST account is generally completed within one to two business days, and the BN constitutes the single statutory TIN for CRA and Alberta corporate tax filings; no separate provincial tax registration identification is required

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — A foreign-owned Alberta ULC obtains a federal Business Number (BN) with a separate GST/HST program account by submitting Form RC1 through CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) or by paper filing; the non-resident shareholder or ULC must appoint a representative for service of process and returns using Form RC59 and must maintain books and records in Canada; issuance of the BN and GST/HST account is generally completed within one to two business days, and the BN constitutes the single statutory TIN for CRA and Alberta corporate tax filings; no separate provincial tax registration identification is required

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Under US Treasury Regulation Section 301.7701-2(b)(8), an Alberta unlimited liability corporation is not a per se corporation and is an eligible entity; a US parent may file an IRS Form 8832 check-the-box election to treat the ULC as a disregarded entity for US federal tax purposes; CRA administrative policy permits a qualifying single-shareholder ULC owned by a US corporation to be recognized on a look-through basis with the US shareholder treated as carrying on business in Canada, subject to the ULC filing Canadian Part I returns and maintaining arm's length accounts; draft hybrid mismatch proposals (Income Tax Act sections 17.1 et seq., issued as draft legislation but not yet enacted) would disallow double-deduction or deduction-without-inclusion outcomes arising from hybrid entity classification, and no elective safe harbor for such hybrid structures is currently available

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide gross revenues from taxable supplies in Canada (the small-supplier threshold); a person must register for GST once this threshold is exceeded in a calendar quarter; special deemed-registration rules under Excise Tax Act subsections 240(1.1) to 240(1.4) apply to non-resident suppliers soliciting orders for goods to be delivered in Canada, which can remove the small-supplier exemption even below the CAD 30,000 threshold

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Programmatic advertising, ad exchange, DSP and SSP services supplied to non-resident recipients are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act (ETA): the general rule for services supplied to non-resident persons (ETA Schedule VI, Part V, section 7) applies where the recipient is a non-resident at the time the tax becomes payable and the supply is not in respect of Canadian real property, tangible personal property situated in Canada, or intangibles to be used or enjoyed in Canada; the advertising-specific provision (ETA Schedule VI, Part V, section 10) supports zero-rating of advertising services directed to persons outside Canada; tangible goods exported from Canada are zero-rated under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, sections 20 and 21 provided export shipment and customs documentation are retained; the supplier must substantiate the non-resident status of the recipient and the foreign destination in contemporaneous books and records to support input tax credit claims and refunds; although Canadian legislation imposes no statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline, a 180-day documentary collection benchmark is applied in CRA audit practice to evidence that the export was a genuine cross-border commercial transaction

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A or approved electronic EDI/ACI transmission) evidencing physical exportation of goods from Canada
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / proof of delivery together with commercial invoice and the programmatic or ad-server contract evidencing the non-resident recipient for zero-rated services
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank wire confirmation and foreign exchange documentation evidencing receipt of export proceeds from the non-resident counterparty

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, API clearance, or digital stamping regime is currently in force in Canada; the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Framework, which is Peppol-compliant, is voluntary and is being phased in for federal procurement only; GST/HST registrants are required to file returns electronically via GST/HST NETFILE and CRA requires taxpayers to preserve books and records in a readable, machine-accessible electronic format for audit purposes

CRA may deny zero-rating where the exporter fails to retain the complete statutory documentation bundle, reassessing the full GST as if the supply were standard-rated and collecting the under-remitted tax with compound/arrears interest; the four-year normal reassessment period is reopened without time limitation where a misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, wilful default, or fraud is established; civil penalties apply under ETA section 285 for false statements in returns (up to 25% of net tax claimed in the false return), and gross negligence penalties may be imposed under income tax provisions where the same transactions affect Part I liability; wilful evasion constitutes a criminal offence under ETA section 327 punishable by fines of up to twice the amount of tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current and capital account convertibility under IMF Article VIII. There are no general foreign exchange controls, no exchange allocation system, and no administrative pre-clearance for inward or outward remittances. Profit repatriation is legally unrestricted, but dividends must be declared out of distributable profits under corporate law, and non-resident dividend/remittance payments are subject to Part XIII withholding tax (default 25%, reduced by tax treaty if applicable). Capital inflows remain subject to the Investment Canada Act net-benefit review only when statutory thresholds are exceeded.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline or mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. Export proceeds may be retained freely in Canadian dollars or foreign currency. Only normal reporting duties apply, such as customs export declarations, balance-of-payments reporting to Statistics Canada, and FINTRAC cross-border electronic funds transfer reporting for transactions at or above CAD 10,000.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); foreign exchange regulatory coordination is distributed among the Department of Finance Canada, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), and the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Rating: Low for primary treasury and escrow use. An offshore electronic money institution (EMI) cannot obtain a Canadian bank license, cannot become a direct participant in Payments Canada settlement systems, and cannot offer insured deposits. It may be engaged only as a supplementary low-value payment facilitator, subject to the Retail Payment Activities Act registration and restrictions on holding Canadian end-customer funds. A fully regulated Canadian bank remains mandatory for the ULC’s core liquidity and compliance infrastructure.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate gross fair market value of controlled non-arm's-length cross-border transactions in a taxation year triggers mandatory contemporaneous Local File documentation. Country-by-Country Report and Master File requirements apply to MNE groups with consolidated global revenue exceeding CAD 750 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a standalone Economic Substance Regulation regime comparable to the UAE. The CRA applies section 245 GAAR, section 247 transfer-pricing re-characterization, and substance-over-form jurisprudence to disregard arrangements lacking genuine commercial or economic substance. A ULC operating as a programmatic AdTech exchange must demonstrate actual personnel, decision-making, allocation of risk, and revenue-generating activity in Canada to preserve treaty benefits and avoid treaty-shopping challenges under the OECD MLI.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Willful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Civil penalty of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded under section 163(2) ITA; criminal prosecution under section 239(1) ITA carries fines up to CAD 100,000 and imprisonment for up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign, authorize, or acquiesce in false returns are jointly and severally liable for the penalty. Courts may impose travel bans pending investigation and asset seizure orders; personal imprisonment is available for willful evasion.
Late Filing / Non-Filing of Corporate Returns

Statutory sanction: 5% late-filing penalty plus 1% per month for up to 12 months, along with daily prescribed interest; persistent non-filing permits a CRA jeopardy assessment (section 152(4)) and revocation of director clearance certificates.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally under section 227.1 ITA for unremitted source deductions and can be listed for cross-border departure travel prevention orders in flagrant cases of repeated non-compliance.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR re-determination of all tax consequences with gross negligence penalties and interest; treaty relief may be denied under the Principal Purpose Test (MLI Article 7); provincial authorities may dissolve the ULC for cause.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly maintain a sham ULC may be personally assessed on deemed distributions or adjusted income; the corporate veil can be pierced where funds are mingled with personal accounts, and directors face civil restitution orders.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: 10% penalty under section 247(3) on the net transfer-pricing adjustment amount; additional section 247(7) per-day penalties for deficient records; interest accrues on the resulting assessment.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve transfer pricing without an arm's-length review are personally liable if they knew or ought to have known of mispricing and failed to remedy it; severe cases trigger executive travel bans and criminal money-laundering charges.
AML / Anti Money Laundering Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties under the PCMLTFA: first violation up to CAD 500,000, subsequent violations up to CAD 1,000,000; criminal money laundering convictions carry imprisonment up to 10 years and asset forfeiture.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are criminally liable if they consent to or fail to prevent AML violations. Courts may issue personal confiscation orders or travel bans under the Criminal Code, and the corporate veil is waived for fraud and money laundering objectives.
