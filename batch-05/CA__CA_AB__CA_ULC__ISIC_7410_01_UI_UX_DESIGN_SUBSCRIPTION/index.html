E-government filing portal

Step 1 — Incorporate the Alberta ULC: file articles of incorporation in the unlimited liability form under the Business Corporations Act (Alberta) (R.S.A. 2000, c. B-9) with the Alberta Corporate Registry; obtain the Alberta incorporation number and certificate of incorporation; no minimum capital requirement applies; 100% non-resident (including U.S.) shareholding is permitted; a registered office in Alberta must be maintained and an Alberta-resident representative may be required for service of process. Step 2 — Federal Business Number (BN): under the federal-provincial one-stop business registration system, Alberta transmits the incorporation data to the CRA, which automatically issues the 9-digit BN; the ULC activates the program accounts — RC for corporate income tax, RT for GST/HST, RP for payroll (only if employees are engaged); RM/RE customs accounts are inapplicable because digital design exports involve no physical goods. Alternative: file paper form RC1, Request for a Business Number, directly with the CRA. Step 3 — GST/HST registration: the RT account becomes effective from the later of the incorporation date and the date the ULC exceeds the C$30,000 small-supplier threshold; because the ULC will be a zero-rated exporter, voluntary registration is strongly recommended to recover input tax credits (ITCs) on Canadian design-software subscriptions, contractor fees, rent, and marketing costs. Step 4 — Non-resident owner identification: CRA collects director/shareholder identity and jurisdiction of residence for FATCA/CRS exchange; if the U.S. parent has elected check-the-box (Form 8832), the CRA nonetheless requires the ULC to file a T2 return as a taxable Canadian corporation and to identify the U.S. owners on the CRA return schedules. Timeline: BN and program accounts issued within 2 to 5 business days; GST/HST account is effective immediately upon registration. Ongoing compliance: T2 corporate income tax return is due 6 months after fiscal year-end; GST/HST returns are due monthly, quarterly, or annually depending on net tax revenue (quarterly is standard for a small zero-rated exporter); Alberta corporate tax is assessed through the T2 with no separate provincial corporate tax return.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — hybrid entity classification: (i) Canadian domestic tax law treats the Alberta ULC as an entity-level taxpayer, namely a taxable Canadian corporation under Income Tax Act s. 89(1), subject to Canadian tax on worldwide income; the federal basic rate of 38% is reduced by the 10% provincial abatement and the 13% general rate reduction to a 15% federal rate, plus Alberta corporate income tax at 8%, producing a combined headline rate of 23% on active business income (11% combined on the first C$500,000 of active business income where the small business deduction is available). (ii) For U.S. federal tax purposes, the ULC is an eligible entity: solely by reason of its unlimited liability feature, U.S. shareholders may check the box under Treasury Regulation Section 301.7701-3 (and consistent with Rev. Rul. 78-318) to classify the ULC as a disregarded entity for a single 100% U.S. owner or as a partnership for multiple owners, thereby creating a U.S.-Canada hybrid entity. (iii) The CRA does not recognize the U.S. flow-through election; the ULC remains a corporation in Canada, and the U.S. shareholder claims foreign tax credits for the Canadian corporate income tax paid on the ULC's export-design income. Territorial scope: residence-based worldwide taxation, with no territorial exemption and no Alberta provincial tax distinction in the GST/HST system other than the absence of a provincial sales tax component.

Available election forms

(1) U.S. check-the-box election (IRS Form 8832): available to the ULC solely because of its unlimited liability status; default U.S. classification for a wholly owned ULC is disregarded entity; partnership classification applies for multi-owner ULCs; the election is prospective and generally locked for 60 months. (2) Canadian law provides no check-the-box analog; the ULC cannot elect to be a partnership or disregarded entity for Canadian federal or Alberta income tax purposes. (3) GST/HST elections: an election under ETA s. 273 can make taxable supplies between the ULC and a closely related Canadian corporation nil-valued; an election under ETA s. 240 permits GST/HST group registration with related Canadian registrants; an election under ETA s. 156 can fix the time of consideration for related-party supplies. (4) Hybrid mismatch treatment: Canada has not enacted BEPS Action 2 dual-inclusion or non-deduction rules specific to ULCs; CRA may nonetheless challenge aggressive ULC structures under the general anti-avoidance rule (Income Tax Act s. 245) and under the Canada-U.S. Treaty (Article VII permanent-establishment attribution) where the check-the-box election creates double non-taxation; the U.S. parent remains exposed to GILTI/Subpart F inclusions on the Canadian branch income in the United States.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility of the Canadian dollar (CAD) under IMF Article VIII. The Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52) and the Bank of Canada Act impose no foreign-exchange controls; the Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1952 and no reinstatement legislation exists. Profit remittance hurdles: an Alberta ULC may pay dividends, interest, royalties, and management fees to non-resident shareholders without Bank of Canada or OSFI approval; the only imposts are Part XIII Income Tax Act (Canada) withholding taxes (default 25%, treaty-reduced to 10% for US portfolio dividends, 5% for qualifying 10% corporate shareholders actively engaged in trade under Canada-US Treaty Article X; interest generally 0% on arm's-length lending; royalties 10%). The CAD floats freely; there is no dual-rate regime, no surrender obligation, and no prior approval for conversion. Capital-account openness is subject only to Investment Canada Act review for control acquisitions above the applicable WTO-investor enterprise-value threshold (C$1.055 billion in 2024) and to national-security screening. The UI/UX design subscription business qualifies as an exportable 'Design-As-A-Service'; invoicing in USD, EUR, or CAD is unrestricted, and subscription revenues may be accumulated in foreign currency indefinitely. Statutory reporting to the CRA of worldwide income converted at the Bank of Canada annual rate is required, but no treasury approval or exchange licence is needed for any transaction.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes zero surrender or retention requirements on export proceeds. The ULC may retain 100% of gross subscription receipts in foreign currency in domestic or offshore accounts for an unlimited period; no mandatory conversion ratio or repatriation timeline applies. The only compliance obligations are informational: (i) the CRA income tax filing reports all revenue converted to CAD at the applicable annual average exchange rate; (ii) a T106 Related-Party Transaction return must be filed if the ULC transacts with a non-resident controlling shareholder (including a US parent of the ULC); and (iii) FINTRAC imposes no reporting on ordinary commercial subscription credits below the electronic-funds-transfer threshold, while inbound/outbound EFTs of CAD 10,000 or more are automatically reported by the financial institution, not by the ULC. For intercompany settlement of design subcontractor payments abroad, the ULC must retain supporting invoices, contracts, and the banking purpose codes for CRA transfer-pricing audits, but no statutory surrender or licensing mechanism exists.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); prudential regulation delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/CFT enforcement by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); monetary policy and payments settlement infrastructure operated by Payments Canada (Lynx, ACSS) and the Bank of Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium for Wise (Wise Payments Canada Inc.) and Airwallex as supplementary payment-corridor rails; Low for Revolut, N26, and pure offshore digital EMIs as primary bank accounts. Legal constraints: under the Bank Act (Canada), deposit-taking is restricted to chartered banks and authorized credit unions; a foreign EMI cannot accept 'deposits' in Canada and may operate only as a Money Services Business (MSB) registered with FINTRAC, with a Canadian presence or agent, a risk/AML compliance program, and no ability to hold corporate funds as fiduciary deposits. Wise is a registered MSB in Canada and may hold funds under a limited-purpose custodial arrangement, making it suitable for low-balance disbursement corridors, but the ULC must maintain its primary treasury accounts at a Tier-1 Schedule I bank. Stripe Billing is a payment processor (not an EMI) and remains the sanctioned subscription collection rail; its Canadian Merchant of Record (Stripe Payments Canada, Ltd.) requires a Canadian bank settlement account. Offshore EEA EMIs cannot passport into Canada; therefore any non-Canadian EMI holding ULC operating funds is non-compliant and must be avoided as a primary account.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium for Wise (Wise Payments Canada Inc.) and Airwallex as supplementary payment-corridor rails; Low for Revolut, N26, and pure offshore digital EMIs as primary bank accounts. Legal constraints: under the Bank Act (Canada), deposit-taking is restricted to chartered banks and authorized credit unions; a foreign EMI cannot accept 'deposits' in Canada and may operate only as a Money Services Business (MSB) registered with FINTRAC, with a Canadian presence or agent, a risk/AML compliance program, and no ability to hold corporate funds as fiduciary deposits. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23.0% combined headline rate (15% federal Part I general corporate rate after federal abatement and general rate reduction; 8% Alberta general corporate rate). Alberta ULCs are not eligible for the 11% small business rate when controlled by a non-resident or a corporation.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under ITA Part XIII on all dividends paid or deemed paid to a non-resident shareholder; reduced to 5% under the Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty (Art. X(2)(a)) when the beneficial owner is a U.S. company owning at least 10% of the ULC's voting shares and the dividends are paid out of active business income.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate fair market value of specified transactions with non-arm's-length non-residents triggers the mandatory T106 information return. There is no statutory CAD threshold for a contemporaneous TP documentation file; all ULCs must prepare a Local File and simplified Master File documenting the design-service intercompany pricing, functional and risk analysis, and benchmark of subscription design services under OECD and CRA Transfer Pricing Memos.

Economic substance scope: Alberta does not apply UK-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) with annual substance tests, but Canadian jurisprudence and tax administration enforce a substance anti-avoidance doctrine: (i) ITA s.245(GARR) invalidates transactions whose primary purpose is tax avoidance and misrepresents economic substance; (ii) the 'beneficial ownership' doctrine under the Canada-U.S. Treaty denies treaty relief where the ULC lacks real Canadian management activity, real employees, and functional office presence; (iii) CRA may re-characterize a ULC holding only a U.S. 10% membership in a U.S. LLC as a sham under the 'step transaction' doctrine. For an ISIC 7410 digital design subscription business, the ULC must maintain actual designers, UI-UX engineers, sales staff, contracts, invoices, bank accounts, and regulatory substance in Alberta; otherwise, CRA can treat the Canadian ULC as an artificial conduit and impute all revenue to the U.S. parent or assess the parent's deemed dividend under s. 84(2).

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion – Willful Intent / Fraudulent Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: ITA s. 239(1): fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded; imprisonment up to 5 years on indictment, or up to 2 years on summary conviction; additional civil penalty of 50% of the tax benefit plus full gross-up on reassessment. CRA will disallow all deductions connected to the fraudulent T2 and charge added interest from the original filing due date.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in filing a false or deceptive T2 are jointly and severally liable for the corporation's unpaid tax and penalties under ITA s. 227.1 and may face separate criminal indictment; civil restitution orders and a CRA application to prevent a director from leaving Canada can be obtained when the director's unpaid tax liability exceeds CAD 25,000 and a serious flight risk exists. Conviction on indictment leads to imprisonment of up to 5 years plus lifetime prohibition from serving as director of a Canadian corporation.
Shell Company – Absence of Economic Substance / Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: CRA denial of the 5%/0% treaty rates under the limitation-on-benefits and beneficial-ownership principles; reassessment of full Part XIII 25% withholding tax on the dividend stream; application of GAAR under s. 245(2) to re-characterize all shareholder loans and deemed dividends; 100% GAAR penalty under s. 245(4) plus interest and compound penalties; revocation of the corporation's Alberta registration or a compliance order dissolving the ULC.

Director personal liability: Directors of the shell ULC are held personally liable for Part XIII withholding tax not remitted from dividends or royalties (s. 227.1); CRA can assess each director personally and seize personal bank accounts. In aggravated cases involving cross-border U.S. tax abuse, a court may issue a travel ban pending extradition or impose personal fines equal to all tax benefits realized by the shareholder chain.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing / Inadequate Documentation

Statutory sanction: ITA s. 247(2) reassessment adjusting income to the arm's-length stage; penalty under s. 247(3) of 10% of the gross transfer-pricing adjustment; the penalty is waived only if the taxpayer has made 'reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length prices'; late T106 filings attract an additional CAD 2,500 per return; interest accrues on the revised tax, and a repeated gross adjustment may trigger criminal prosecution under the Tax Evasion provisions.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign the T2 while knowing that the TP documentation is incomplete or fraudulent are jointly liable for the 10% TP penalty and the underlying adjusted Part I tax under s. 227.1; in cases of flagrant manipulations, directors face personal criminal liability and a CRA demand for restitution of up to CAD 200,000 plus legal costs.
AML / Beneficial Ownership Reporting Failure

Statutory sanction: Canada's Beneficial Ownership Registry and AML rules impose monetary penalties of up to CAD 500,000 against the corporation; directors can be fined up to CAD 200,000; the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act sets a criminal sanction of up to 10 years imprisonment plus forfeiture of all assets obtained through the concealed structure; banks and payment processors (including Stripe and Wise) report suspicious transactions and can freeze payouts.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for civil penalties in the amount of the undeclared funds, and for imprisonment for up to 10 years if the concealment is linked to money laundering as defined under the Criminal Code (s. 462.31); flight-risk directors may be subject to a court-order travel ban pending criminal proceedings; revocation of the ULC's corporate status exposes the entire share capital and personal assets of the directors to creditor claims, given the unlimited liability feature of the ULC.
