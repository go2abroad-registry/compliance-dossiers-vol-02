Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including its Business Registration Online (BRO) service and My Business Account portal. The CRA administers federal income tax and the federal Goods and Services Tax (GST). Alberta corporate income tax is administered by the CRA under the federal-provincial tax collection agreement. Alberta has no provincial sales tax.

E-government filing portal

An Alberta ULC must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) and register for the applicable CRA program accounts. A non-resident corporation can apply for a BN and GST/HST account using the CRA non-resident registration process or an applicable CRA business registration form; a Canadian resident applicant may use BRO where eligible. The applicant supplies its legal name, incorporation jurisdiction and date, registered and business addresses, directors or authorized representatives, business activities, expected taxable supplies, and effective registration date. GST registration is required when the business is not a small supplier or when a specific statutory rule requires registration; voluntary registration may be available. A GST/HST account is identified by the BN followed by the RT program-account suffix. The ULC is separately subject to Canadian corporate income tax filing and applicable Alberta corporate tax rules; its classification as a corporation must not be inferred from a foreign tax election.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A Canadian ULC is generally a corporation and a separate taxpayer under Canadian income tax law; it is not domestically pass-through by default. Canada does not provide a general check-the-box election to treat a Canadian corporation as a partnership or disregarded entity. An eligible ULC may be classified under United States entity-classification rules by a US check-the-box election, but that election does not change its Canadian classification and may create cross-border hybrid mismatch, treaty, withholding, or foreign tax credit consequences. Canadian elections available to corporations are limited to specific statutory regimes and do not generally alter corporate status.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters for the general small-supplier test, subject to statutory aggregation, timing, and exception rules. Certain non-resident digital-economy suppliers and other specified persons are subject to separate registration rules that may apply without the general threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Qualifying exports of goods may be zero-rated under the Excise Tax Act when the statutory export conditions are met and the supplier retains evidence that the goods were exported. Services supplied to a non-resident may be zero-rated where the applicable statutory conditions are satisfied, subject to exclusions, including specified services relating to Canadian real property, tangible personal property, or litigation. Translation, localization, and dubbing services are not automatically zero-rated; eligibility depends on the recipient's residence, the service's nature, and the statutory exclusions. Zero-rating is not equivalent to exemption: a registrant may generally claim eligible input tax credits.

  • For exported goods, retain the applicable Canadian export declaration and supporting export evidence, such as carrier or freight-forwarder records, bill of lading, airway bill, or proof of delivery, as appropriate.
  • Retain customer contracts, invoices, proof of the recipient's non-resident status and address, service descriptions, work records, and evidence that the service satisfies the relevant zero-rating provision.
  • An inward remittance certificate is not a general Canadian GST zero-rating requirement. Retain payment records where relevant to substantiate the transaction and accounting.

Canada has no general real-time GST invoice-clearance, mandatory tax API, or digital-stamping regime for ordinary domestic business-to-business supplies. GST registrants must issue invoices or receipts containing prescribed information when required, retain adequate books and records, and file returns electronically where required by law or CRA direction. Alberta has no separate provincial sales-tax invoice-clearance system.

Canada imposes no general statutory deadline requiring export proceeds to be repatriated to Canada. A person claiming zero-rating must substantiate the applicable statutory conditions and maintain records. An incorrect claim may result in reassessment of GST, denial or recovery of input tax credits or refunds, arrears interest, and applicable penalties under the Excise Tax Act and Tax Administration Act. Knowing or grossly negligent false statements may attract gross-negligence penalties; tax evasion or related fraud may lead to prosecution and fines or imprisonment. The amount and limitation period depend on the facts and applicable statutory provisions.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current and capital account convertibility under the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act; no exchange control law restricts inward equity injections or outward profit remittances by an Alberta ULC. Practical hurdles are tax-driven, not FX-driven: dividends to non-resident shareholders attract Part XIII withholding tax (25% statutory, reduced to 5%-15% under an applicable tax treaty), and distributions from a ULC may be recharacterized under the subsection 55(2) surplus-stripping rules or section 212.1 deemed-dividend provisions, requiring a clean paid-up-capital and dividend-resolution trail rather than any central bank permit.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory timeline for surrender, repatriation, or conversion of export receipts; an exporter may freely retain foreign currency in Canadian or offshore accounts. Mandatory obligations are limited to (i) FINTRAC reporting of any cross-border physical currency or monetary instrument equal to or above CAD 10,000 under the PCMLTFA, (ii) CRS/FATCA account reporting, and (iii) Income Tax Act section 261 functional-currency rules requiring translation of revenue into Canadian dollars for tax reporting.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); banking prudential supervision is delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), while AML/ATF supervision and cross-border wire reporting are administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

23% combined general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal and 8% Alberta. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) may generally receive the small business deduction on eligible active business income, producing a combined rate of 11% (9% federal and 2% Alberta) within applicable business-limit and eligibility rules. A ULC is not automatically a pass-through entity for Canadian tax purposes.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Part XIII withholding tax is generally 25% on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to treaty relief. Treaty rates commonly reduce the rate to 15% for portfolio dividends and, under qualifying ownership conditions, 5% for substantial corporate shareholders; the applicable treaty and beneficial-ownership requirements control. The Canada–United States treaty contains special hybrid-entity rules relevant to ULC payments, including circumstances in which treaty benefits are denied under Article IV(7).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No general Canadian Local File or Master File threshold applies. Canadian contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is required under the Income Tax Act for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions, and must generally be provided to CRA within three months of a written request. The separate T106 reporting threshold is generally aggregate reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceeding CAD 1 million. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational enterprise groups with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million, subject to Canadian filing rules.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a general Economic Substance Regulations regime comparable to certain offshore jurisdictions. Canadian tax consequences may nevertheless depend on factual substance, residence and central management and control, beneficial ownership, transfer-pricing arm's-length conditions, anti-avoidance provisions, and applicable treaty anti-abuse rules. A Canadian-incorporated ULC is generally resident in Canada for Canadian income tax purposes.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion, false statements, or wilful failure to file

Statutory sanction: Tax, interest, and civil penalties may apply, including a gross-negligence penalty generally equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax or overstated credits. Criminal tax evasion convictions can result in fines of 50% to 200% of tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years; indictable fraud and related offences may carry separate, more severe penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may face criminal prosecution for their own participation, authorization, or legally attributable conduct. Director liability is not automatic merely because of office. Statutory personal liabilities can arise for specified unremitted source deductions and other amounts, subject to statutory defences and limitation periods; civil veil-piercing is exceptional.
Failure to withhold or remit non-resident tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be liable for the unremitted withholding tax, prescribed interest, and applicable penalties, including penalties for failure to deduct or remit and late or inaccurate information returns.

Director personal liability: A director may be assessed personally for specified source-deduction or withholding liabilities under statutory director-liability provisions, subject to due-diligence and other statutory defences. A treaty rate should not be used without adequate evidence of entitlement.
Transfer-pricing non-compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust income or deductions to an arm's-length amount. A transfer-pricing penalty generally may apply where the net adjustment exceeds the statutory threshold, calculated at 10% of the qualifying net adjustment; interest and double-tax exposure may also result. Failure to provide requested documentation may restrict access to the reasonable-efforts defence.

Director personal liability: Corporate tax adjustments do not ordinarily create automatic personal liability. Directors may face personal exposure for their own misconduct or under applicable statutory liability provisions.
Treaty abuse or mischaracterization of a ULC

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty relief, assess withholding tax, interest, and penalties, and apply domestic anti-avoidance rules where their requirements are met.

Director personal liability: Personal liability is not automatic; it may arise under applicable withholding-liability provisions or for directors' own knowing participation in an offence.
Unremitted payroll source deductions or GST/HST

Statutory sanction: Assessments, prescribed interest, and statutory penalties may apply. Directors may be jointly and severally liable for specified unremitted source deductions and, in defined circumstances, certain GST/HST amounts, subject to statutory conditions and defences.

Director personal liability: Personal assessment may be available under the applicable statute; the statutory due-diligence defence, limitation periods, and procedural requirements must be assessed for the specific liability.
Corporate records, beneficial ownership, or AML non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Corporate registry, tax, and criminal penalties may apply according to the specific breach. Canada does not impose a universal corporate AML program obligation on every localization agency; obligations depend on whether the business is a reporting entity under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act or is otherwise subject to applicable rules.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal civil or criminal liability for their own participation, authorization, or statutory breaches. Travel restrictions and imprisonment are not automatic consequences of ordinary corporate tax non-compliance.
