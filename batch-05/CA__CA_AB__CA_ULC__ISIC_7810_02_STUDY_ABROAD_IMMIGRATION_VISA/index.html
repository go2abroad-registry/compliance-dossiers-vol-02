Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); portal: 'My Business Account' accessible at https://www.canada.ca/en/services/taxes/business-taxes.html. Alberta corporate income tax is administered by CRA under a federal-provincial collection agreement with the Alberta Ministry of Finance; the ULC must be registered federally and with the Alberta Corporate Registry.

E-government filing portal

Foreign-owned Alberta ULC must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) via CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) or by submitting Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number). The BN is a 15-character identifier with program-specific reference numbers: 'RM' for corporation income tax, 'RT' for GST/HST, 'RP' for payroll. Registration is mandatory for non-resident-owned ULCs; a non-resident corporation may file using the incorporation certificate and Alberta articles. A Canadian agent for service may be required. The federal T2 corporate return serves as the single return for Alberta; no separate provincial tax trigger is required because Alberta levies a flat 8% corporate rate administered by CRA.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; under the Canadian Income Tax Act an Alberta ULC is a separate legal person and a resident corporation. It is not a partnership or trust and does not qualify for pass-through treatment in Canada; the ULC is taxable at the corporate level, and dividends to shareholders are taxable as foreign income in the shareholder's residence. A Canadian ULC remains fully subject to Canadian taxes on worldwide income if it is resident; if non-resident, it is taxable on Canadian-source income only, and its classification is fixed for Canadian tax purposes.

Available election forms

Canada does not enact a U.S.-style check-the-box election. A ULC may, however, file U.S. Internal Revenue Service Form 8832 to elect 'disregarded entity' status for U.S. federal tax purposes, but such election has no effect on Canadian tax classification. Domestic elections relevant to a ULC include: section 85 rollover for capital assets transferred into the corporation; subsection 150(1.1) five-year non-resident gratuity timing; section 156 (ETA) election for closely-related corporations to inter-charge zero-rated supplies; and a foreign affiliate election under draft 5907. Hybrid mismatch rules under section 18.27 may require recharacterization if a foreign jurisdiction treats the ULC as a fiscal transparency (e.g., the United States) while Canada treats it as a corporation; no unique Canadian hybrid election exists and CRA will assert entity-level taxation.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000; a person must register for GST/HST if its taxable supplies (exclusive of zero-rated supplies) exceed CAD 30,000 worldwide in a single calendar quarter or over the previous four consecutive calendar quarters. For a foreign supply of study-abroad advisory services that are performed in Canada, the supply may still be considered made in Canada and the threshold applies. A ULC making taxable supplies above this threshold must register and charge 5% GST on domestic taxable supplies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A service supplied to a non-resident individual or corporation who is outside Canada at the time and who is not registered for GST/HST generally qualifies for zero-rating under Schedule V, Part V, section 7 of the Excise Tax Act, provided the service is not performed in respect of real property situated in Canada and is not performed in respect of tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance. International student placement and immigration visa advisory services that are performed entirely from Canada and delivered via email or telephone to a non-resident applicant are eligible for zero-rating, but services performed on Canadian real estate (e.g., accommodating a student in Canadian housing) would be taxable at 5%. The supplier must obtain normal commercial documentation proving the recipient's non-resident status.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A / EDI) for any accompanying physical goods; no export customs filing applies to pure service exports, but a CRA auditor will request a service statement
  • Commercial invoice or service contract (with detailed scope, fees, dates, and the non-resident recipient's foreign address) that satisfies the ETA invoice requirements and confirms the supply was made to a non-resident
  • Proof of receipt of payments from the foreign client (bank wire confirmation, SWIFT record, or import/export invoice receipt) plus an Inward Remittance Certificate or FX conversion record evidencing repatriation of proceeds within the statutory period

No statutory real-time fiscal clearance regime, no API-mandated e-invoicing, and no digital stamping/QR watermark in Canada. GST/HST invoices must be issued when the supplier is registered and must contain prescribed particulars under sections 223–224 of the Excise Tax Act, including supplier name, BN, recipient name, description, amount, and tax rate. Electronic invoices are acceptable, including PDF, email, and EDI. CRA has published a voluntary e-invoicing framework but does not require real-time fiscal data submission. Digital pre-clearance may be required for certain excise or provincial liquor products, but not for professional services.

CRA scrutinizes zero-rated service claims. If documentary requirements are not met, the zero-rating may be disallowed and GST at 5% assessed, together with arrears interest and penalties under secton 280 (failure to register) and 285 (false statements or omissions). For exported goods, a zero-rated export may be recaptured if the exporter fails to export within 12 months, but for services CRA applies a general 180-day rule to repatriate foreign currency earnings and to preserve zero-rating. Gross negligence penalties may reach 25% of the re-assessed tax, plus penalties for failure to provide sufficient records. The ULC is jointly and severally liable with its directors in cases of deliberate misstatement. Inability to produce the full documentation bundle creates a presumption that the supply was made in Canada and taxable.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible. Both current-account transactions (e.g., study-abroad program fees, visa-advisory service fees, remittances, and consulting income) and capital-account transactions (e.g., shareholder capital injections, intercompany financing, and repatriation of capital) are generally free from exchange-control authorization requirements. There is no exchange-control permit regime, no central-bank approval for foreign-currency settlement, and no general prohibition on profit remittance. However, a Canadian-resident ULC distributing amounts to a non-resident shareholder is subject to source withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (Section 212) unless reduced by an applicable tax treaty, and all cross-border transfers of funds must comply with FINTRAC large-transaction and electronic-funds-transfer reporting thresholds. The main practical hurdles are not convertibility restrictions but regulatory due diligence, tax-residency considerations, and the requirement that escrowed immigration-program client funds be held in a Canadian-regulated bank or trust account.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory export-proceeds surrender regime exists in Canada. Exporters and service providers, including study-abroad and immigration-consulting entities, are not required to convert foreign-currency receipts into Canadian dollars within any prescribed timeline and may retain receivables in foreign currency. Statutory reporting is limited to FINTRAC obligations: every person or entity dealing with an electronic funds transfer of C$10,000 or more in a single transaction, or aggregate transactions of C$10,000 or more within 24 hours, must report to FINTRAC; banks must also complete settlement and currency-reconciliation documentation. Canadian-dollar and foreign-currency accounts are freely operable for qualifying businesses, subject to ordinary tax accounting under the Income Tax Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (monetary authority only); Canada maintains no separate central exchange-control institution. Foreign-exchange and anti-money-laundering supervision for deposit-taking institutions is performed by OSFI and FINTRAC, with treasury-related regulatory compliance under the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and the Department of Finance Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low as the sole banking solution for mandated client-fund escrow; Medium as an ancillary settlement rail for non-escrow operational disbursements. Canadian immigration and study-abroad consultants regulated by the College of Immigration and Citizenship Consultants (CICC) are required to hold client funds in trust accounts at Canadian financial institutions authorized to accept deposits, which excludes most offshore digital EMI platforms. Offshore EMIs generally are not licensed as banks or trust companies under the Bank Act or Alberta trust laws; their legal status is that of foreign money services businesses, and use of such an EMI for escrow would create a substantive compliance conflict with CICC trust-account rules. An online business account with a Canadian Schedule I bank or a Canadian chartered bank trust subsidiary is required for escrow and is the clearly viable route; select FINTRAC-registered Canadian money services businesses may be used for outbound efficiency, but cannot serve as the statutory trust depository.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital contributions from non-resident shareholders to the Alberta ULC's Canadian operating account must be executed by SWIFT wire transfer (MT103) from the foreign corporate bank, using the bank's beneficiary details and a clear remittance-information field stating 'SHAREHOLDER CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION – ULC' or 'EQUITY INJECTION – ALBERTA ULC' to avoid mischaracterization as taxable income. Where intercompany debt is used, formal loan agreements, payable-on-demand terms, arm's-length interest, and the applicable prepayment or withholding reporting must be considered under the Income Tax Act. For payments received in excess of C$10,000, the Canadian receiving bank is responsible for FINTRAC reporting, and the ULC must provide source-of-wealth and source-of-funds verification. Investment Canada Act notification may be required where a non-Canadian shareholder acquires control of a Canadian business, but a de novo ULC operating a new study-abroad/visa advisory business generally falls outside the notifiable acquisition thresholds unless the enterprise value and asset thresholds are met. The bank's compliance team will also verify that each shareholder's bank is in good standing and that the remittance narrative matches the board resolution and capital-subscription records. All inbound equity proceeds may be held in Canadian-dollar or foreign-currency accounts without exchange-control conversion requirements.Outbound disbursements for study-abroad and immigration operations will primarily use SWIFT MT103 international wire transfers for tuition payments to foreign educational institutions, partner-agency commissions, service vendor settlements, and repatriation of profits or shareholder distributions. Each outbound payment must carry a precise purpose narrative and be supported by an invoice, enrollment or scholarship agreement, agency commission contract, or tax-and-treasury justification. Corporate treasury should use a single designated bank platform with automated reconciliation, HST and GST segregation, and FINTRAC large-EFT reporting. Direct debits, automated clearing settlement in Canada (Payments Canada), and Canada Post/Money Mart rails are not appropriate for cross-border tuition; instead the bank's global transfer service or SWIFT direct clearing is the mandated rail. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Low as the sole banking solution for mandated client-fund escrow; Medium as an ancillary settlement rail for non-escrow operational disbursements. Canadian immigration and study-abroad consultants regulated by the College of Immigration and Citizenship Consultants (CICC) are required to hold client funds in trust accounts at Canadian financial institutions authorized to accept deposits, which excludes most offshore digital EMI platforms. Offshore EMIs generally are not licensed as banks or trust companies under the Bank Act or Alberta trust laws; their legal status is that of foreign money services businesses, and use of such an EMI for escrow would create a substantive compliance conflict with CICC trust-account rules. An online business account with a Canadian Schedule I bank or a Canadian chartered bank trust subsidiary is required for escrow and is the clearly viable route; select FINTRAC-registered Canadian money services businesses may be used for outbound efficiency, but cannot serve as the statutory trust depository.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27.00% federal + 8.00% Alberta general corporate income tax = combined 35.00% general rate. Reduced combined rate of 11.00% (federal 9.00% + Alberta 2.00%) applies to first CAD 500,000 of active business income eligible for small business deduction, subject to federal and provincial association rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends (including deemed dividends) paid by Canadian resident corporation to non-resident shareholders. Alberta imposes no separate dividend withholding tax.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CRA requires contemporaneous Local File documentation where transactions with non-resident non-arm’s length parties exceed CAD 1,000,000 for goods or CAD 50,000 for services per year. Master File required for multinational groups with consolidated revenue ≥ CAD 1,000,000,000. Country-by-Country report required where group consolidated revenue ≥ CAD 1,000,000,000 and applicable threshold conditions met.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a Gulf-style Economic Substance Regulations regime, but the substance doctrine is enforced through GAAR (Income Tax Act s.245), statutory anti-avoidance rules, treaty non-collection and beneficial-ownership tests, and case law substance-over-form principles. The ULC must demonstrate substantive Canadian business activity—actual management, employees, clients, contracts, bank accounts, and decision-making—sufficient to support its income allocations. Absence of substance may cause CRA to disregard the entity, deny treaty benefits, recharacterize transactions, or assess deemed dividends. An unlimited liability corporation formed primarily to flow funds and obtain treaty benefits will be scrutinized as a conduit/shell arrangement.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Income Tax Act s.239, Criminal Code)

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction: fine of up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to 5 years for each count; gross negligence civil penalty (s.163(2)) of 50% of the understated tax; false statements or omissions penalty CAD 1,000 to CAD 25,000; interest accrual from original due date.

Director personal liability: Directors personally liable for corporate unremitted Part XIII and employee-source withholdings; civil fraud restitution; criminal prosecution against directors who authorized evasion; travel bans and seizure of passports when tax evader is investigated; imprisonment up to 5 years; personal assets available for collection.
Offshore/Conduit Shell Company and GAAR

Statutory sanction: GAAR reassessment and cancellation of tax benefits; denied treaty withholding reduction; deemed Part XIII dividend withholding assessed at 25%; transfer-pricing adjustments; gross negligence penalties up to 50%; interest; potential penalty equal to 10% of transaction adjustment under s.247(3); regulatory licensing suspension for Canadian immigration consultancy.

Director personal liability: Directors who created or directed the use of the shell vehicle may be subject to director liability for unremitted withholding, personal GAAR and negligence penalties, professional license revocation, civil liability to defrauded clients or tax authorities, and criminal conspiracy if unlawful avoidance crossed into fraud. Travel bans may be imposed pending enforcement of tax liabilities.
AML/PCMLTFA and Immigration License Violation

Statutory sanction: Under PCMLTFA: administrative monetary penalty for corporations up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation; Criminal Code money laundering penalties up to 10 years imprisonment; CICC disciplinary fines up to CAD 50,000 and/or license revocation; immigration misrepresentation consequences under IRPA include inadmissibility for 5 years and loss of client status.

Director personal liability: Directors can receive personal fines up to CAD 500,000 under PCMLTFA if they directed the violation; criminal money-laundering convictions trigger personal imprisonment, travel bans, extradition, and loss of professional standing; civil suits from defrauded clients; directors’ personal assets may be attached where corporate veil pierced for intentional wrongdoing.
Transfer Pricing and Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA transfer-pricing reassessment to arm’s length amount; s.247(3) penalty of 10% of net upward adjustment if adjustment exceeds CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of gross revenue (lesser); interest on unpaid taxes; Part XIII gross-up on recharacterized dividends; failure to provide documentation also weakens penalty relief.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for penalties if they participated in the transfer-pricing misstatement; in personal liability scenarios involving unpaid tax and bankruptcy, travel bans and asset seizure may occur; civil liability for misrepresentation to lenders, investors, or immigration authorities; potential extradition for cross-border tax fraud conspiracy.
