Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital is imposed for an Alberta ULC; incorporation is permitted with one or more shares without par value and capital subscribed by promissory note or cash. No escrow or capital deposit account is required by the Alberta Corporate Registry.

Funding & unlock path: No civil-defence or special structural clearance is mandated for a standard office tenancy, but the employer must comply with the Alberta Occupational Health and Safety Act and OHS Code, including fire-safety plans, exit signage, two means of egress, first-aid supplies, electrical safety, and ergonomic workstation requirements; the building must hold a valid fire-code occupancy permit, and any leasehold fit-out or change of building use requires a municipal development permit.

Paid-in capital rule: No statutory minimum paid-up capital is imposed for an Alberta ULC; incorporation is permitted with one or more shares without par value and capital subscribed by promissory note or cash. No escrow or capital deposit account is required by the Alberta Corporate Registry.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no U.S.-style check-the-box election for a Canadian-incorporated ULC. A Canadian corporation is generally a separate taxpayer under the Income Tax Act. A foreign jurisdiction may independently characterize a Canadian ULC under its own classification rules; that foreign treatment does not change Canadian classification. Canadian hybrid-mismatch rules may deny or adjust deductions or other tax consequences in specified cross-border arrangements, but they are not an elective entity-classification regime.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, subject to detailed small-supplier rules and exceptions. The threshold is not a blanket exemption for all non-resident suppliers or activities.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Qualifying exported goods are generally zero-rated under the Excise Tax Act where the statutory conditions are met, including applicable export, delivery, purchaser, and use requirements. Many services supplied to a non-resident are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, subject to exclusions and conditions; relevant exclusions can include services relating to real property in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada, certain litigation or other services, and supplies to persons in Canada. Data-labeling, AI-training, and BPO services require fact-specific analysis of the recipient, actual service, place and subject matter of performance, and statutory exclusions. Zero-rating is not established solely by receiving payment from abroad. Maintain evidence of the recipient's non-resident status, contract and scope of work, delivery records, invoices, payment records, and evidence supporting the applicable zero-rating provision. A registrant may generally claim input tax credits for eligible GST/HST incurred in commercial activities, subject to statutory documentation and restrictions.

  • For exported goods, retain the applicable Canadian export declaration and customs records, carrier or freight documentation such as a bill of lading or air waybill, commercial invoice, proof of delivery or export, and records linking the goods to the zero-rated supply.
  • For exported services, retain the executed contract or statement of work, evidence that the recipient is a non-resident, invoices, work and delivery records, correspondence, and payment evidence.
  • An inward remittance certificate is not a general Canadian statutory requirement for GST/HST export zero-rating. Foreign-exchange receipts and bank records may be relevant supporting evidence but do not replace the statutory tests.
  • The precise customs declaration and reporting requirements depend on the goods, export destination, value, exemptions, and applicable Canada Border Services Agency rules; they do not ordinarily apply to services alone.

Canada has no general federal real-time GST/HST invoice-clearance, digital-stamping, or mandatory API e-invoicing regime for ordinary domestic business-to-business transactions. GST/HST invoices and supporting records must meet the applicable statutory information and retention requirements. Electronic records are generally permitted if they remain accessible and reliable. Separate federal or provincial electronic invoicing requirements may apply to particular public procurement or regulated transactions.

Canada imposes no general statutory deadline requiring export proceeds to be repatriated to Canada. CRA may audit the supply classification, recipient residence, contractual facts, export evidence, invoices, and input tax credits. If a supply was incorrectly treated as zero-rated, the supplier may be assessed the GST/HST that should have been collected, applicable interest, and penalties. False-statement or omission penalties may apply under the Excise Tax Act, with enhanced consequences potentially applying to gross negligence or tax evasion. Input tax credits may be denied or recovered where statutory conditions or documentary requirements are not met. Penalty amounts and reassessment periods depend on the facts and applicable statutory provisions.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is an IMF Article VIII jurisdiction: full convertibility exists on both current and capital accounts. The CAD floats freely, with no approval, quota, prior-declaration, or minimum-holding-period requirement for capital inflows, equity injections, or outward remittances. Dividend and profit remittance from the Alberta ULC is unrestricted but subject to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (statutory 25%; treaty-reduced to 15% portfolio or 5% qualifying corporate ownership under the Canada-US treaty, and comparable reduced rates under other applicable tax treaties). The AI-data-labeling BPO is a non-cultural business, so Investment Canada Act review thresholds do not obstruct capitalization; only a new-business notification is required. Practical friction arises from the hybrid nature of a US check-the-box disregarded ULC: banks apply enhanced due diligence, and tax-transparent intercompany debt must comply with ITA thin-capitalization and excessive-interest financing rules.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. No statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no surrender obligation applies to export proceeds or service fees under the Currency Act or the Financial Administration Act. Receipts may be retained in CAD or any foreign currency. Obligations are limited to: (i) FINTRAC electronic funds transfer reporting by the remitting bank for wire transfers at or above CAD 10,000; (ii) CRA Form T1135 reporting if aggregate specified foreign property exceeds CAD 100,000; (iii) Statistics Canada direct-investment and balance-of-payments reporting for non-resident direct investment above CAD 1M (Form 105, quarterly); and (iv) blocking and freezing of transactions involving sanctioned jurisdictions under the Special Economic Measures Act and the United Nations Act.

Central bank supervision: Monetary authority and high-value payment system oversight: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), operator of the Lynx large-value transfer system (successor to LVTS). Prudential regulation of Schedule I banks: Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI). Anti-money laundering supervision and enforcement: Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), with policy ownership at the Department of Finance Canada. Payment service provider supervision: Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) regime. No standalone foreign-exchange control authority exists; statutory exchange-control restrictions in Canada are nil.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

PayPal Fit: High

Feasibility: MEDIUM as a supplementary disbursement rail; NOT viable as primary treasury or for receiving capital injections. Wise Payments Canada Corp. is a FINTRAC-registered money services business using a Schedule I partner bank for settlement; it supports CAD/USD/EUR balances with batch API payouts, matching the target Mass_PayPal_Payout and Wise rails. Legal constraints: (i) an MSB cannot operate a deposit-taking business in Canada, so equity subscription funds must land in a chartered-bank account; (ii) PayPal Mass Pay is treated by Canadian banks as a payfac concentration and should be capped at approximately 35% of weekly outbound volume; (iii) payment service providers active in Canada require registration under the Retail Payment Activities Act, including safeguarding of funds, incident reporting, and operational risk controls; (iv) sub-contractors must settle to a bank account or licensed e-money account with a Canadian intermediate clearing node - neither Wise nor PayPal can serve as the sole receiving point for wages without a regulated buffer.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility: MEDIUM as a supplementary disbursement rail; NOT viable as primary treasury or for receiving capital injections. Wise Payments Canada Corp. is a FINTRAC-registered money services business using a Schedule I partner bank for settlement; it supports CAD/USD/EUR balances with batch API payouts, matching the target Mass_PayPal_Payout and Wise rails. Legal constraints: (i) an MSB cannot operate a deposit-taking business in Canada, so equity subscription funds must land in a chartered-bank account; (ii) PayPal Mass Pay is treated by Canadian banks as a payfac concentration and should be capped at approximately 35% of weekly outbound volume; (iii) payment service providers active in Canada require registration under the Retail Payment Activities Act, including safeguarding of funds, incident reporting, and operational risk controls; (iv) sub-contractors must settle to a bank account or licensed e-money account with a Canadian intermediate clearing node - neither Wise nor PayPal can serve as the sole receiving point for wages without a regulated buffer.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal general corporate rate 15% plus Alberta general corporate rate 8% = 23% combined on active business income; manufacturing/processing profits reduced to 11.5% federal component

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation (such as an AB ULC) to a non-resident shareholder, unless reduced by tax treaty. Under the Canada–US treaty, the rate is 5% for a US company holding at least 10% of the voting shares and 15% in other cases; other treaties may provide 5%-15%.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate annual cross-border transactions with a non-resident related party for Local File and T106 filing; Master File required when the MNE group's consolidated revenue exceeds CAD 50,000,000, though CRA recommends country-by-country reporting for groups above EUR 750 million (approx. CAD 1.1 billion).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply explicit Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) similar to the UAE/Bermuda, but the Income Tax Act's general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section 245 may disallow transactions lacking business purpose and economic substance. Jurisprudence (e.g., Canada v. Alta Energy Luxembourg S.à r.l., 2021) has confirmed that a treaty-treaty double-dip with substance can be valid, but CRA will closely review the following substance attributes for a ULC: (1) business premises in Alberta; (2) actual employees performing data labeling and AI training functions; (3) board governance decisions made in Canada; (4) bank accounts and accounting presence; (5) operational records demonstrating commercial activities. A ULC with nominal substance and offshore management/control risks being recharacterized as a conduit or agent, possibly causing all Canadian-source income to become fully taxable and non-deductible for tax purposes.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Under section 239 ITA, fines up to 200% of the tax evaded (minimum 50% of the avoided tax), plus imprisonment for up to 5 years. CRA may also impose gross negligence penalties of 50% of the understated tax under section 163(2) and disallow all deductions claimed through the false arrangement.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for the corporation's unpaid tax withholdings and GST/HST under section 227.1 and Excise Tax Act section 323. If the evasion involves fraud or breach of fiduciary duty, the corporate veil may be pierced for civil restitution. Criminal conviction for tax evasion can lead to extradition, discretionary travel restrictions, and, in extreme cases, a court-ordered travel ban (e.g., Fugitive Suppression Act).
Shell Company / Sham Arrangement

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply the GAAR to recharacterize the structure, imposing full Part I tax on income attributed to the ULC, denying loss utilization, and levying transfer pricing penalties of 10% on the net adjustment. The entity may be struck from the Alberta Corporate Registry for failing to file annual returns or carry on business.

Director personal liability: Liquidator, receiver, and bankruptcy trustees can sue directors for preferring creditors. Directors may be personally assessed for unreported source deductions (employee income tax, CPP, EI) and for any GST/HST remittances outstanding. If used for tax evasion or money laundering, directors face criminal liability under the Criminal Code and Civil Forfeiture Orders forcing disgorgement of personal assets.
Anti-Money Laundering / Unlicensed Payment Activity

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), failure to register as an MSB/FI with FINTRAC (if required) carries penalties up to CAD 500,000 for individuals and CAD 1 million for corporations, plus administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 2 million. If the operation is a shell company used to move funds offshore, the Minister may invoke Civil Enforcement Measures and forfeit the property.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate MSB that omits to register are personally liable under PCMLTFA section 57.2 for fines/penalties. In serious cases, directors can be individually prosecuted for money laundering if they knew or were reckless about the origin of funds. A travel ban may be issued under the Criminal Code for major financial crimes.
Failure to File / Disregard of Canadian Tax Obligations

Statutory sanction: Penalty per IC 87-2R: CAD 1,000 for each month (max 24 months) for failure to provide documentation; automatic late filing penalty of 5% plus 1% monthly. CRA may also proceed to issue an arbitrary assessment and seize bank accounts, garnish accounts receivable, and enter into section 160 transactions to hold third parties liable.

Director personal liability: Directors who voted for the omission, or did not properly supervise a company officer responsible for filing, are personally liable for the unpaid tax and penalties as assessable under the Income Tax Act subsection 227.1(1). Directors cannot avoid this by resigning if the liability accrued before their resignation; they may also be subject to an injunction and a court-imposed travel restriction to attend asset-purchase examinations.
