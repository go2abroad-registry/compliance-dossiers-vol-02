Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); CRA Business Registration Online and My Business Account portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html)

E-government filing portal

A foreign (non-resident) corporation obtains its statutory tax identifier, the 9-digit Business Number (BN), by filing Form RC1 with CRA or applying through Business Registration Online, attaching certified constitutional documents, a non-resident declaration, and proof of Canadian presence or commercial activity. The GST/HST program account is the BN followed by the 'RT' suffix and a 4-digit program code. Non-residents carrying on business in Canada with Canadian taxable supplies exceeding CAD 30,000 must register for GST/HST; voluntary registration is available. Prior extraprovincial registration under the British Columbia Business Corporations Act with BC Corporate Registry is required before CRA completes the Canadian corporate income tax account (RC) profile.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box election; a BC Business Corporation is mandatorily taxed at entity level as a Canadian corporation under ITA Part I and BC provincial corporate tax. Available statutory elections include voluntary GST/HST registration for non-residents, the closely related corporations joint election (ETA s. 156), the section 216 non-resident rental income election (ITA s. 216), and certain foreign affiliate surplus elections (ITA ss. 93/95). Hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.2/18.21) and EIFEL restrictions apply with limited elective ordering but do not offer a classification menu.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of taxable supplies made in Canada during the preceding four calendar quarters (small supplier threshold); non-residents carrying on business in Canada must register upon exceeding this amount, and may voluntarily register below it

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of tangible personal property, including armored military tactical vehicles, are zero-rated under Excise Tax Act Schedule VI, Part V where the supplier exports the goods, or delivers them to a common carrier for export, as part of the supply to a non-resident recipient. The supplier must retain a complete documentary trail evidencing physical exportation to a destination outside Canada. Armored vehicles transferred to foreign governments or armed forces for export qualify as zero-rated exports. Associated Canadian services supplied in respect of exported goods may also be zero-rated under prescribed provisions. Export Control List permits from Global Affairs Canada and any applicable ITAR / Defence Production Sharing Agreement considerations do not displace GST zero-rating.

  • Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) Export Declaration, including Form B13A filing through the Canadian Automated Export Declaration (CAED) system
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical export movement and destination
  • Inward remittance certificate or bank payment evidence confirming receipt of foreign currency proceeds against the export commercial invoice

Canada imposes no real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or continuous transaction control mandate. CRA requires electronic NETFILE filing for GST/HST registrants and supports API-enabled filing via My Business Account. Federal procurement has adopted the Peppol BIS 3.0 e-invoicing standard on a phased basis, but commercial B2B e-invoicing remains voluntary without government pre-clearance.

CRA may reverse zero-rating and reassess unremitted GST where export evidence is incomplete. The ordinary reassessment period is four years, extended indefinitely in cases of misrepresentation, neglect, or fraud. Civil penalties can reach 25% of the unremitted tax plus compound arrears interest, and wilful evasion is criminally prosecutable under ETA section 239 with fines up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment up to five years per count. Exporters bear the burden of preserving the substantiation bundle for at least six years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely convertible on both current and capital account. Canada maintains no general exchange control legislation requiring central bank approval for current-account or capital-account transactions. Profit remittances (dividends, interest, royalties, management fees) are permitted without any statutory foreign-exchange surrender or conversion requirement, though the underlying transaction must not violate Canadian sanctions under the United Nations Act, the Special Economic Measures Act, or the Justice for Victims of Corrupt Foreign Officials Act. Cross-border wire transfers over CAD 10,000 must be reported to FINTRAC. For defense-sector exporters, EIPA export permits and Controlled Goods Directorate registration remain the primary regulatory gate for transferring hardware, technical data, or related financial proceeds.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory timeline or mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. Export proceeds may be retained in foreign currency or converted at the exporter's discretion, subject to standard bank KYC and FINTRAC large-value transaction reporting (within 24 hours for wire transfers of CAD 10,000 or more). However, for military tactical vehicles classified under EIPA Export Control List Item 2-4 or similar, the exporter must obtain an export permit before shipment; the documentary evidence of permit issuance and customs declaration must be retained for bank compliance audits in relation to cross-border disbursement and incoming funds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) and the Department of Finance Canada; the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) administers AML/CFT oversight; the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) administers trade controls under the Export and Import Permits Act (EIPA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low to Medium. Canada does not have a dedicated EMI (Electronic Money Institution) license equivalent to the EU PSD2 framework. Non-bank fintech platforms generally operate as Money Service Businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. For high-risk defense sales, federal Schedule I banks are the only practical full-service banking rail; offshore EMIs often lack direct membership in the Canadian Payments Association and cannot clear CAD wire payments into government accounts or issue documentary letters of credit without a correspondent Canadian bank. Use of an unlicensed offshore EMI for defense proceeds creates sanctions-compliance gaps and likely fails Tier 2 bank due diligence.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity or shareholder loan funds should be remitted via SWIFT MT103 (or MT202 for larger transfers) to the Canadian corporate account with explicit purpose codes, typically using bankable descriptions such as 'Share capital injection under BC Business Corporations Act', 'Equity contribution for CA_CORP ISIC 3040 project', or 'Subordinated shareholder loan'. Canadian banks require the originating bank to provide documentary evidence of the source of funds (board resolution, subscription agreement, or loan agreement). The recipient GST/HST account and CRA business number must match the corporate name. For defense contracts, the bank may require the inbound wire to reference a specific Global Affairs Canada permit number for the ultimate export transaction. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 5,000,000 aggregate value of non-arm's length transactions with a related party in a single taxation year triggers the transfer-pricing penalty regime under s.247(3) and mandates contemporaneous documentation, including a Local File and, where applicable, a Master File and Country-by-Country Report.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime, but the CRA applies s.245 GAAR and substance-over-form principles to disregard transactions without real economic substance or business purpose. Industries manufacturing controlled or military goods are additionally required to meet substantive presence, Canadian-resident board oversight, and licensed export controls under the Export and Import Permits Act and Defence Production Act, which in effect create an enforceable economic substance standard.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate Income Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Prosecution under s.238 of the Income Tax Act: fine up to CAD 100,000 plus 50% of the tax evaded and up to five years imprisonment; CRA civil re-assessment includes gross negligence penalty (50% of tax understated) and arrears interest.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who participate or conspire in tax evasion face personal civil restitution for evaded taxes and penalties, criminal conviction with the fine and imprisonment, court-ordered travel bans restricting departure from Canada, and involuntary bankruptcy examinations on Swiss and Singapore bank accounts; corporate veil is pierced where s.160 or s.239 of the Income Tax Act applies.
Shell Corporation and Abuse of Corporate Veil

Statutory sanction: CRA may ignore the shell under GAAR, re-assess on the 27% combined rate with a 25% transaction penalty under s.245(5); BC Registrar of Companies may dissolve the corporation under the Business Corporations Act; export permits in respect of Canadian military equipment will be suspended or cancelled by Global Affairs Canada.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign statutory declarations enabling a shell structure are jointly liable for the corporation's unpaid Canadian taxes, employee remittances and VAT/GST as a deemed director, and may be disqualified from board service across Canada, issued travel bans in anti-money-laundering investigations, and held civilly liable for fraudulent preference under bankruptcy provisions in the Bankruptcy and Insolvency Act.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement on Defence Contracts

Statutory sanction: Penalty under s.247(3): 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment, plus 12% to 20% late filing penalties, and full re-assessment of taxable income under arm's length principle; denial of any treaty-based MAP relief absent documentation.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly or negligently approve TP policies or who fail to maintain Canadian TP records may be personally assessed as officers under s.247 for the penalty because they control the information required; CRA may also demand director personal surety for foreign transactions to permit release of a travel embargo in cross-border audits.
Export Controls and Anti-Money-Laundering Breach

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA): administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation, and criminal fines up to CAD 500,000 together with imprisonment up to five years for second indictable offence; under the Export and Import Permits Act, each unauthorized export can result in a fine up to CAD 500,000 per count and imprisonment up to 10 years, plus cancellation of all Canadian export permits.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers personally face prosecution as parties to the export/AML violation, seizure of personal assets via proceeds-of-crime forfeiture proceedings, travel bans preventing departure pending criminal hearing, and extradition to the United Nations or United States jurisdiction under the mutual legal assistance treaty for controlled military exports; they cannot invoke the corporate veil to shield personal criminal responsibility for wilfully placing military goods in the hands of a designated terrorist organization or sanctioned country.
