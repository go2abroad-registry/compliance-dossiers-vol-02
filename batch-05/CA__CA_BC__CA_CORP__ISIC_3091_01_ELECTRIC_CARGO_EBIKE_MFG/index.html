Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) for federal GST/HST and corporate income tax; British Columbia Ministry of Finance for BC PST. Official portals: canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html and eTaxBC tax.gov.bc.ca.

E-government filing portal

Foreign entities must apply to the CRA for a 15-digit Business Number (BN) and relevant Programme Accounts (RT, RP, RC, RM) using Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1. Required documentation includes certified articles of incorporation/registration, registered office address, director/officer particulars, and a completed non-resident tax form (Form RC459 or equivalent) when applicable. BC PST registration is then completed through the BC eTaxBC portal using the BN.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — Foreign entities must apply to the CRA for a 15-digit Business Number (BN) and relevant Programme Accounts (RT, RP, RC, RM) using Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No check-the-box election under Canadian domestic law. A Canadian incorporated corporation is a separate taxpayer; small business deduction eligibility requires Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) status. GST/HST related corporations can elect to file as a single registrant (Section 156 election); non-residents may make a Section 216 election for net-basis taxation on Canadian rental property income. Provincial income tax is collected by CRA under the Tax Collection Agreement with no separate provincial election for BC.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

For GST: CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated) per any 12 consecutive calendar quarters; for BC PST: immediate registration required upon carrying on business in BC with no simplified low-revenue threshold, including for non-resident vendors with a physical or substantial presence.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under Excise Tax Act Schedule VI, Part V, Section 1 for tangible personal property (electric cargo e-bikes) supplied by a commercial manufacturer and exported by the supplier or a customer acting as agent, provided the goods are physically delivered outside Canada within the required time frames and the supplier maintains documentary evidence establishing export. Services supplied to non-residents are zero-rated when performed wholly outside Canada and no benefit is conferred inside Canada.

  • Canada Border Services Agency B13A Export Declaration (customs export declaration)
  • Bill of Lading or through-waybill naming a consignee outside Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank evidence of foreign currency proceeds repatriated to Canada)
  • Commercial invoice issued to the non-resident purchaser with zero-rating indicator
  • Packing list showing export marks, HS classification, and country of origin
  • Transport and delivery evidence (marine/air/land) including proof of arrival at foreign destination
  • CBSA or logistics custodian certification of export transaction

Canada does not mandate real-time e-invoicing, fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or continuous transaction controls (CTC). The CRA accepts electronic records and supports compliant EDI/API transmission; BC PST requires prescribed sales invoices and detailed records but has no prior-approval clearance mechanism.

CRA can deny the zero-rating and claw back GST/HST net tax, input tax credits, and refunds when export evidence is deficient; normal reassessment period is four years after the return, extended to the later of six/ten years or indefinitely for misrepresentation. Civil penalties include the Excise Tax Act s.280 false representation penalty (24% of the stated amount), s.285 gross negligence penalty (50% of the net tax amount), and s.288 recapture of input tax credits. Criminal sanctions for willful evasion under ETA s.327-329 include fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to five years. Exporters must retain all substantiating records for at least six years (BC PST records: five years) and face summary dismissal of any contested claim without the mandatory export bundle.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full free convertibility on both current account and capital account. The Canadian dollar is an independently floating major reserve currency with no exchange-control permits, quotas, approval gates, or repatriation requirements. Trade payments, dividends, royalties, intercompany settlements, and direct equity injections move freely without prior authorization. The only material frictions are (i) Part XIII withholding tax of 25% on dividends and certain payments to non-residents, reduced to 5% or 15% under applicable income tax treaties for corporate shareholders holding at least 10% voting power; (ii) FINTRAC international electronic funds transfer (ETF) reporting for every cross-border wire of C$10,000 or more; and (iii) transfer-pricing and thin-capitalization limits under ITA sections 247 and 18(4), which cap deductible interest on shareholder debt at a 1.5:1 debt-to-equity ratio.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. Canada imposes no statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no surrender obligation on export receipts. A BC corporation may retain USD or third-currency proceeds indefinitely in domestic or offshore accounts. Compliance is confined to (i) filing electronic export declarations with the Canada Border Services Agency through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) for commercial goods valued at C$2,000 or more per shipment, using HS 8711.60 for electric cargo bicycles with auxiliary electric motor and HS 8711.90 for other cycles; (ii) maintaining statutory books and CRA income recognition for export revenue; (iii) executing a USMCA Certificate of Origin for United States-bound shipments; and (iv) accepting automatic FINTRAC reporting by the bank for any international EFT of C$10,000 or more.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with regulatory perimeter shared by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential bank supervision, the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML/ATF and cross-border wire reporting, and Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (ISED) for inbound investment review under the Investment Canada Act. Canada maintains no dedicated foreign exchange administration; exchange liberalization is the statutory norm and the Minister of Finance retains macro-financial oversight under the Bank of Canada Act and the Financial Administration Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium as a supplementary settlement rail; Low as a primary treasury substitute. A foreign or domestic EMI operating in Canada must register as a Money Service Business (MSB) with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and, if carrying on business in British Columbia, obtain registration under the BC Money Services Act. EMIs are not deposit-taking institutions under the federal Bank Act, cannot issue documentary letters of credit, do not have direct access to Payments Canada settlement systems (Lynx and Real-Time Rail), and funds held with them are not CDIC-insured. The commercially legal architecture is hybrid: (i) a Tier-1 Schedule I bank account for equity injections, LC-backed component imports, CAD payroll, and CRA remittances; (ii) an EMI wallet such as Wise Business or Airwallex used strictly for low-value collection concentration from fragmented wholesale and direct-to-consumer orders; and (iii) systematic sweeps from the EMI wallet to the Tier-1 account with full transaction-level evidence. The OEM itself does not become an MSB by merely receiving EMI transfers and requires no MSB licence provided it does not provide money remittance services to third parties.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Fresh incorporation (under 24 months) receiving the first inbound equity wire from a foreign parent in a jurisdiction on the FATF monitoring list, combined with an ultimate beneficial owner who is not a Canadian resident.

Root cause: Fresh incorporation (under 24 months) receiving the first inbound equity wire from a foreign parent in a jurisdiction on the FATF monitoring list, combined with an ultimate beneficial owner who is not a Canadian resident.

Defense evidence: Short-form incorporation records including Articles and Notice of Articles; register of directors and share register; executed share subscription agreement; board resolution authorizing the account and the capital contribution; the foreign parent's certificate of good standing; source-of-funds confirmation from the foreign parent's own bank; percentage breakdown of all UBOs with certified identification; organizational chart; and a Canadian counsel opinion confirming the transaction has no money-laundering or sanctions nexus.
Export sales invoices settled by wires from a third-party remitter whose name does not match the contractual buyer, which is a layering red flag in trade transactions.

Root cause: Export sales invoices settled by wires from a third-party remitter whose name does not match the contractual buyer, which is a layering red flag in trade transactions.

Defense evidence: Three-party payment direction letter signed by the buyer, the seller, and the remitter; amended commercial invoices and packing lists; signed purchase orders; bill of lading or air waybill; CBSA CERS export declarations; USMCA Certificate of Origin for US-bound cargo; remitter bank statements; agency or consolidator agreement explaining the payment chain; and written explanation of the commercial rationale.
Concentrated large prepayments on component imports from suppliers domiciled in non-AEOI or high-risk trade corridors, particularly when prepayment terms exceed 60 days and no documentary credit is used.

Root cause: Concentrated large prepayments on component imports from suppliers domiciled in non-AEOI or high-risk trade corridors, particularly when prepayment terms exceed 60 days and no documentary credit is used.

Defense evidence: Supplier master contract and current purchase orders; commercial invoices and thermographic packing lists; confirmation of the underlying SWIFT MT103; cargo and marine insurance certificates; customs entry evidence on the Canadian side; OECD-transfer-pricing-compliant documentation demonstrating arm's length pricing of any related-party prepayments; and a cash-flow rationale memo supporting the prepayment terms as a supply-chain security measure.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant routing: (i) The foreign parent remits via SWIFT MT103 strictly from its own bank account, with the beneficiary being the BC corporation; the remittance information field must read 'SHARE CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION - [corporate name] - [allotment reference]'; personal accounts of directors, third-party nominees, or commingled funding must never be used. (ii) For USD funding, the Canadian bank will accept MT103 through its US clearing correspondent (CHIPS/Fedwire) with onward book transfer into the corporation's USD-denominated operational account; no Canadian 'purpose code' is required by banks, although narrative remittance references must align with the underlying allotment to satisfy the bank's FINTRAC international-EFT report for wires of C$10,000 or more. (iii) Corporate housekeeping must be simultaneous: allot shares in accordance with the BC Business Corporations Act or the federal CBCA, file the share structure update in the BC corporate registry, and execute share certificates held in the corporate minute book; a federal corporation must file the change with Corporations Canada. (iv) Tax treatment: an equity injection is non-taxable and is not subject to Part XIII withholding; if funds arrive as intercompany shareholder debt, the corporation must apply arm's length interest, respect the 1.5:1 thin-cap ratio, and file CRA Form T106 when non-arm's length transactions exceed C$1,000,000 in the year; later dividend remittances to a treaty-resident parent carry Part XIII at the applicable treaty rate. (v) Investment Canada Act: establishment of a new Canadian business by a non-Canadian below the multi-billion-dollar WTO direct-acquisition threshold requires no pre-closing review; only a short-form notification is considered, and for a de novo BC manufacturing subsidiary the transaction is outside review.Rails available to the BC OEM: (i) Domestic CAD high-value payments execute through Payments Canada Lynx (RTGS) initiated from the Tier-1 treasury portal, and operational low-value payments through the Real-Time Rail (RTR) where posted payment-size limits apply; (ii) cross-border USD disbursements for battery cells, motors, controllers, and frame components are cleared by SWIFT MT103 through CHIPS or Fedwire with the bank's nostro relationships; (iii) trade finance settlements use an irrevocable commercial documentary letter of credit compliant with UCP600, issued via SWIFT MT700 where suppliers require bank payment security, or a deferred-payment LC for repeat suppliers; standby LCs and documentary collections (MT400/MT500) are available for performance and collections; (iv) CAD-USD conversion is executed on the bank's daily FX desk or via pre-negotiated forward cover; (v) for US dealer receivables, the exporter may use a USD concentration account, cross-border receivables discounting, or forfaiting, provided full invoice, bill of lading, and customs entry evidence is retained for each drawdown.Deliverable plain-vanilla FX forwards with a licensed Schedule I bank, covering CAD/USD and CAD/CNH tenors up to 12 months, documented under an ISDA Master Agreement or single-transaction treasury confirmations; no exchange-control approval is required for a BC manufacturing company with transactional exposure.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) in currencies without a fully accessible onshore deliverable market (notably offshore CNH, KRW, or INR) used strictly to hedge identified commercial exposures; these are permissible when booked with an approved derivatives dealer and must respect OSFI margin requirements for non-centrally cleared derivatives, including variation margin mechanics imposed on the bank counterparty.Cross-currency swaps and FX swaps aligning USD import payables with CAD functional-currency cash flows, entered under ISDA terms and limited to balance-sheet hedging, supported by a board-approved FX risk policy containing an explicit no-speculation mandate; the end-user hedging exemption applies to the corporation.
