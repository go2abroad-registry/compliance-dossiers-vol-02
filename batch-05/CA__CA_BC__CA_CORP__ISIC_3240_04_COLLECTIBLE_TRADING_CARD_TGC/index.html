STEP 2: For a BC-incorporated corporation, the CRA automatically issues the 9-digit Business Number (BN) upon integration with the BC corporate registry; using the CRA access code, the corporation activates program accounts through Business Registration Online, including RP (corporate income tax), RT (GST/HST), RC (payroll deductions if employees are engaged) and RM (import/export accounts for export clearance). Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: a Canadian-resident Business Corporation is a separate legal taxpayer; there is no pass-through eligibility for a corporation and no Canadian equivalent of the US check-the-box election. The federal general corporate rate is 15% and the small-business rate is 9%; BC provincial corporate rates are 12% general and 2% small-business, producing combined headline rates of 27% general and 11% small-business for a qualified Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) on active business income up to the CAD 500,000 business limit. A corporation controlled by non-residents will generally be taxed at the 27% combined rate. Territorial scope: a resident corporation is subject to Canadian tax on its worldwide income with foreign tax credits and the exempt-surplus regime for active foreign business income; a non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada is taxed on Canadian-source business income under Part I and is additionally subject to a 25% branch tax under Part XIV on after-tax earnings remitted or deemed remitted (subject to treaty relief). No tax-exempt zone or offshore incentive regime applies in British Columbia.

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box election. Available statutory elections and structural mechanisms include: (1) ITA s. 85(1) rollover elections for the tax-deferred transfer of qualifying property into or out of the corporation; (2) ITA s. 261 functional-currency election allowing a qualifying corporation to compute Canadian taxable income in a foreign functional currency (e.g., USD), relevant to an export-heavy trading-card publisher; (3) ITA s. 219(2) election for non-resident corporations in computing Part XIV branch tax; (4) ITA s. 216(1) optional net-basis return election for non-residents deriving Canadian rental or timber-royalty income, not applicable to active trading-card manufacturing; (5) Excise Tax Act s. 156 joint election for closely related corporations to treat taxable intercorporate supplies as made for nil consideration, simplifying GST/HST group administration; (6) the GST/HST Quick Method election available to small suppliers with annual worldwide taxable supplies not exceeding CAD 400,000, useful for simplified remittance accounting; and (7) the BEPS hybrid mismatch rules under ITA s. 18.4, introduced by Bill C-59, which operate mechanically with no elective carve-out and require self-assessment of any interest or dividend deduction disallowance arising from hybrid financial instruments or hybrid entities such as tax-transparent US LLCs in the distribution chain.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full current-account and capital-account convertibility under IMF Article VIII; the Canadian dollar (CAD) is a free-floating currency with no exchange controls, no prior-approval regime for foreign investment inflows, and no administrative barrier to profit repatriation; dividend and management-fee remittances to non-residents are subject only to Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (withholding tax at the statutory 25%, generally reduced under tax treaties to 5%-15% for dividends and 0%-15% for royalties/management fees) and to standard corporate solvency and board-authorization rules under the Business Corporations Act (British Columbia); there is no foreign-exchange licensing requirement for establishing capital escrow or operational accounts in Canada

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender timeline and no mandatory conversion ratio; exporters may retain 100% of foreign-currency receipts in domestic or offshore accounts; however, persons importing or exporting currency or monetary instruments equal to or exceeding CAD 10,000 must report to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); financial intermediaries must file Large Cash Transaction Reports (LCTRs) and Electronic Funds Transfer Reports (EFTRs) with FINTRAC for transactions at or above the CAD 10,000 threshold; commercially, the trading-card manufacturer should evidence export invoices, bills of lading, and HS/ISIC coding (3240 / NAICS 323111) to support the legitimate trade narrative of every receivable collected

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as the monetary and clearing authority; Payments Canada as the national clearing and settlement systems operator; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as the prudential regulator of Schedule I banks; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as the AML/ATF supervisor and financial intelligence unit; Canada has no centralized foreign-exchange-control administrative body, and the Minister of Finance exercises residual policy authority under the Currency Act and the Payment Clearing and Settlement Act

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium, conditional and supplementary only; Canadian Money Service Businesses (MSBs) and digital Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) may lawfully operate if registered with FINTRAC and, where conducting provincially regulated activity in British Columbia, licensed under the BC Money Services Businesses Act; practical viability as the primary treasury node is low because EMIs are not direct participants in Payments Canada's Lynx or the Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS), cannot take deposits, and must segregate client funds in trust accounts at a Schedule I bank; acceptable roles are e-commerce collection sub-accounts (Stripe, Authorize.Net), cross-border vendor disbursement, and multi-currency payout wallets; a Schedule I corporate operating account must remain the anchor clearing relationship for capital escrow, trade finance, and FX hedging

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined (Canadian federal 15% general corporate rate plus British Columbia 12% general provincial corporate rate); qualifying federal Manufacturing and Processing deduction reduces the federal component to 13.5% for a 25.5% combined rate.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (ITA s.212(2)) on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident person.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T2 return due no later than 8 months after the fiscal year-end. Final balance of tax due no later than 2 months after fiscal year-end (3 months for a qualifying CCPC if its taxable income is within the small business limit in the current or prior taxation year). Quarterly tax instalments are due on the last day of each quarter when the prior year balance of tax exceeds C$3,000.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File / contemporaneous documentation: mandatory where the aggregate value of non-arm's-length transactions with a non-resident exceeds C$1,000,000 for a taxpayer with gross revenue of C$50,000,000 or less, or exceeds C$5,000,000 (C$25,000,000 for loans/advances) for a taxpayer with gross revenue exceeding C$50,000,000, per CRA Information Circular IC 89-14R. Master File: required for fiscal years beginning on or after January 1, 2022 where the multinational group's consolidated gross revenue is at least C$100,000,000; CbC reporting applies where the group exceeds EUR 750,000,000 consolidated turnover.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply a UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations code. Instead the ITA deems a corporation incorporated in Canada to be resident in Canada (ITA s.250(4)(a)) and taxes it on worldwide income; a non-resident corporation carrying on business through a Canadian permanent establishment is taxable on Canadian-source business income under Part I. GAAR (ITA s.245) denies tax benefits from transactions lacking a genuine business purpose or commercial substance. For TP purposes, ITA s.247(2)/(3) requires the documented commercial substance of intercompany arrangements and permits CRA recharacterization where arrangements differ from those a non-taxpayer would have made. The BC Registry additionally requires a registered office, corporate records, minute book, and annual corporate report filing to maintain valid legal substance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: ITA s.239(1): fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to 2 years (indictable); higher sanctions for tax evasion in relation to offshore distributions may extend custody exposure; civil gross negligence penalty under ITA s.163(2) of 50% of the understated tax, plus accrued daily arrears interest.

Director personal liability: A director who signs false returns or directs concealment is jointly and severally liable for corporate penalties under provincial and federal civil fraud principles; personal restitution, director disqualification, seizure of personal assets, criminal arrest warrant risk (enforced by CBSA), and imprisonment upon conviction.
Shell Company / Profit Stripping

Statutory sanction: Thin capitalization rules (ITA s.18(4)) disallow interest on debt from specified non-resident shareholders in excess of a 1.5:1 debt-to-equity ratio (and for years after 2023, the Excessive Interest and Financing Expenses Limitation (EIFEL) caps net interest expense at 30% of EBITDA); GAAR recharacterization under ITA s.245; TP transfer pricing reassessment under ITA s.247(2); penalty up to 10% of the net adjustment (s.247(3)); unpaid tax, interest, and compounded penalties remain collectable from corporate assets.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in artificial stripping may be assessed penalties as 'participants' under ITA s.163.2 (up to C$100,000 plus the planner's gross compensation); civil liability for conspiracy; denial of the director's discharge on bankruptcy; non-resident directors face Canada Border Services Agency arrest warrants and travel bans where criminal prosecution is initiated.
Unremitted Source Deductions (Payroll and GST)

Statutory sanction: 10% penalty on late remittances plus prescribed-rate interest compounded daily; the unpaid source deductions are deemed held in trust (ITA s.227(4)) and cannot be discharged by a company's bankruptcy; CRA may freeze bank accounts and issue garnishments without prior court approval.

Director personal liability: ITA s.227.1 imposes strict joint and several personal liability on every director for unremitted withholdings; the CRA may assess the director personally even where the corporation is insolvent; non-resident directors cannot avoid assessment via treaty; travel bans and denial of cross-border exit may occur upon criminal arrest warrant for fraudulent failure to remit.
AML / FINTRAC Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: administrative monetary penalties up to C$1,000,000 per violation, criminal fines and imprisonment of up to 5 years on indictment; misuse of the corporate account for money laundering may constitute a criminal offence under s.462.31 of the Criminal Code attracting asset forfeiture.

Director personal liability: A director who knowingly accepts laundered funds or directs unreported cash transactions can be personally prosecuted; conviction may bring imprisonment, criminal restitution, freezing and forfeiture of personal property, prohibition from acting as a director, and travel restrictions; even without criminal participation, a director who fails to implement a FINTRAC compliance program may be assessed a personal penalty under the Act's due-distinct-officer liability provisions.
