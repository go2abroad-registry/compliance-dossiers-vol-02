Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — Business Registration Online (BRO) portal for the federal Business Number (BN), GST/HST, and payroll accounts; BC Ministry of Finance — eTax BC portal for Provincial Sales Tax (PST) registration in British Columbia.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) by registering with the CRA through Business Registration Online (BRO) or by submitting Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Accounts). The foreign entity must designate an authorized representative or Canadian agent, provide its registered name, head office address, legal entity type, estimated taxable supplies, and bank details for refund direct deposits. Upon registration, separate accounts are established: Corporation Income Tax (RC0001), GST/HST (RC0001 RT0001), and any applicable payroll (RP0001). For British Columbia PST, the entity must separately register with the BC Ministry of Finance through eTax BC; a BC PST account (PST-BC) is required if taxable sales will be made in the province. The BN may be obtained electronically by a non-resident registrant, but proof of foreign incorporation and a non-resident director's personal tax number may be requested.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not maintain a US-style 'check-the-box' entity classification election. A Business Corporation is automatically taxed at the entity level; it files a T2 corporation income tax return and is a separate taxpayer from its shareholders. Available elections relevant for a group structure include: (i) GST/HST closely related corporation election (allowing inter-company zero-rated or tax-free supplies), (ii) GST/HST joint venture election (registration of an operator as representative of the joint venture), (iii) Income Tax Act section 256 amalgamation election, and (iv) partnership or co-ownership elections for joint operations. Hybrid mismatch elections under the Income Tax Act (sections 94A, 96, 142.7) may be used by multinational groups to align tax characterisation but must be documented with the CRA.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; for GST/HST, a person whose taxable supplies exceed this amount must register. A non-resident carrying on business in Canada may also voluntarily register if required to claim input tax credits on Canadian expenses. Separate BC PST registration threshold generally applies to 'taxable sales' exceeding CAD 10,000 per annum unless an exemption applies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A GST/HST registrant may zero-rate supplies of goods exported from Canada under section 1 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act. For tangible die-cast scale models, the supply is zero-rated where the registrant exports the goods in accordance with subsection 1(1), meaning the goods are shipped by the supplier or at the supplier's direction to a destination outside Canada, and possession of the goods is transferred to the non-resident recipient outside Canada, or the goods are exported as commercial goods. Evidence of export must be obtained within two years. Services rendered in respect of goods destined for export may be zero-rated under Part V, Schedule VI if the services are supplied directly to a non-resident and acquired solely for business purposes. The compatibility of zero-rating must be verified against Incoterms and shipping documents.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Export Reporting requirement under the Canadian Customs Act / Export Declaration)
  • Commercial invoice describing the goods as zinc alloy die-cast scale automotive replicas
  • Bill of Lading (B/L) or Air Waybill showing consignee outside Canada
  • Transport document evidencing departure from a Canadian port or border
  • Packing list
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank wire receipt proving receipt of proceeds from the non-resident customer
  • Purchase order or contract with the non-resident buyer
  • Proof of payment of any applicable customs duties/fees, if applicable

Canada does not mandate a real-time electronic invoice clearance or fiscal stamping regime. GST/HST invoices may be issued in paper or approved electronic formats provided they satisfy the required information content (e.g., supplier name, BN, date of invoice, GST/HST amount). The CRA accepts electronic invoices transmitted by email, API, EDI, or portal if records are retained in an accessible, permanent format. British Columbia PST does not require e-invoicing. No digital stamping or pre-clearance by the CRA/BC Ministry of Finance is required. Non-resident suppliers must retain digital invoice and transaction data in English or French and make it available to the CRA upon audit.

CRA audit scrutiny focuses on documentary proof of export; a supplier that cannot provide sufficient evidence of export must self-assess GST/HST on the supply. Reassessment may be made within four years of the return date; the period is extended indefinitely if misrepresentation, fraud, or willful default. Clawback of the zero-rating can arise where goods are subsequently returned into Canada, diverted to a Canadian destination, or where documentation is falsified. Penalties include: (i) a failure-to-file penalty of 5% of the GST/HST.HTM due plus 1% per month; (ii) a 10% repeat-failure penalty; (iii) a 50% gross negligence penalty for knowingly making false statements under the Excise Tax Act; (iv) interest at the prescribed rate compounded daily; and (v) criminal prosecution for fraudulent export claims. Provincial PST obligations remain separate, and exporters must preserve records for a minimum of six years.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains unrestricted current-account and capital-account convertibility under IMF Article VIII. Dividends, interest, royalties, trade settlements and capital redemptions may be remitted cross-border without central-bank approval. Remittance hurdles are limited to Part XIII withholding tax on outgoing payments to non-residents (default 25%; treaty-reduced to 5% for qualifying corporate shareholders holding at least 10% of voting shares under the Canada-China DTA, 15% otherwise) and the statutory solvency test under the Business Corporations Act (British Columbia). No BC-specific profit-remittance barrier exists.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender obligation and no mandatory conversion ratio. Export receipts from sales of die-cast scale automotive models may be retained indefinitely in USD, EUR, CNY or other foreign currencies. However, all electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported to FINTRAC, and supporting trade documentation (commercial invoices, bills of lading, customs declarations) must be retained for audit readiness.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC); coordinated supervision by Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and anti-money-laundering reporting to Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada has no dedicated foreign-exchange-control agency because the currency regime is fully liberalised.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium feasibility with material legal constraints. Canada does not issue an Electronic Money Institution (EMI) licence; non-bank payment platforms (PayPal, Wise, Stripe) operate only as FINTRAC-registered Money Services Businesses and cannot accept deposits, extend liquidity, or provide cheque-clearing grade settlement. These rails are permitted as collection and disbursement channels for low-value transactions but must be structured to sweep balances daily to a Schedule I bank operating account. PIPEDA applies to all stored customer payment data.

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% (federal 15% plus British Columbia provincial 12%, combined general corporate rate; reduced rates may apply for Canadian-controlled private corporations on active business income up to the business limit)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on gross dividends paid or credited to a non-resident shareholder, absent treaty reduction

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Filing deadline is six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end for the T2 return; balance of tax payable is due two months after fiscal year-end (three months for certain Canadian-controlled private corporations claiming the small business deduction); mandatory instalments are due quarterly on the 15th day of the month following each calendar quarter

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory contemporaneous Local File and Master File documentation is required when the total gross revenue of the corporation plus all non-resident persons with whom it is not at arm's length exceeds CAD 5 million, or when transactions with non-arm's length non-resident persons exceed CAD 1 million in a fiscal year; in British Columbia there is no additional provincial TP documentation threshold because provincial income tax is administered by the CRA; entities below the threshold must still maintain reasonable support for transfer prices to defend against reassessment and penalties

Economic substance scope: Applicability of Economic Substance Regulations (ESR): The federal Income Tax Act does not have a codified economic substance doctrine as in the US, but the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under subsection 245(3) disallows tax benefits arising from avoidance transactions unless the transaction is not an avoidance transaction; the Supreme Court of Canada in Canada Trustco and Lipson established that GAAR applies where there is a single predominant purpose of obtaining a tax benefit, the transaction defeats the object, spirit, or purpose of the relevant provisions, and a mis-use or abuse of the provisions is present; economic substance is inferred from the actual legal rights and obligations of the parties, not solely from the form; additionally, British Columbia's corporate statute (BC Business Corporations Act) imposes a registered records office requirement and statutory records maintenance but does not impose economic substance regulations; the federal Canada Not-for-profit Corporations Act and Income Tax Act do not require annual economic substance filings akin to Cayman Islands ESR, but CRA requires a corporation to have a real business presence, commercial activity, and employees to substantiate residence and PE status; for transfer pricing, the arm's length principle requires substance analysis of functions, assets, and risks; whipsaw or circular transactions solely to reduce Canadian tax may be caught by GAAR, leading to full reassessment, gross negligence penalties, and, in serious cases, prosecution for tax evasion

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Failure to Report Canadian-source Sales

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 238(1) of the Income Tax Act, conviction on indictment for tax evasion carries a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, and imprisonment for up to five years; summary conviction carries a fine of 50% to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment for up to two years; the CRA may also impose a civil gross negligence penalty under subsection 163(2) equal to 50% of the understated tax; interest and arrears are payable at the prescribed rate, compounded daily

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who participate in or authorize the evasion may be jointly and severally liable for the corporate tax debt under subsection 227.1(1) (amounts withheld) or the common-law 'director liability' for negligence; the corporate veil is pierced where the corporation is a sham or an agent for the principals; personal criminal liability extends to imprisonment up to five years and fines; courts may issue a director prohibition order for up to five years under section 241; CRA can demand security or obtain a search warrant; travel bans are possible under the Canada Evidence Act or under extradition treaties for fugitive directors, but not for mere civil tax debts
Transfer Pricing Manipulation / Thin Capitalization

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess the taxpayer under section 247(2) using the arm's length price, adjust the debt-to-equity ratio, and disallow the excessive interest under section 18(4); the taxpayer may be subject to a 10% transfer pricing penalty under subsection 247(3) if the net adjustment exceeds CAD 5 million or, where smaller, the lesser of 10% of the adjustment and CAD 10,000; in egregious cases, subsection 247(3) penalty is added to the tax and interest; GAAR may apply to re-characterize a transaction that is abusive; no criminal fine is imposed for transfer pricing penalties unless willful evasion is also established, but the threat of secondary transfer pricing adjustments and double tax may be mitigated only by a mutual agreement procedure under a DTA

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for transfer pricing adjustments, but where corporate tax is unpaid after three months, the director may be sued under subsection 227.1(1) for amounts withheld (e.g., employee source deductions); if the director signed the T2 return knowing that transfer pricing was false, the director may be prosecuted for counselling or participating in tax evasion; civil fraud claims by minority shareholders or minority interest holders may pierce the veil if the transaction is a fraud on the shareholders
Sham / Corporate Veil Piercing for Personal Profit

Statutory sanction: The court may disregard the corporate entity under the legal doctrine of sham and hold the shareholder(s) personally liable to creditors and the CRA for unpaid corporate taxes, trade debts, and tort liabilities; under section 128(1)(e) of the Income Tax Act, the Minister may treat a corporate disposition of property that has no legitimate business purpose as a shareholder benefit, taxed as division B income; the BC Business Corporations Act s. 141 allows the court to order rectification or liquidation in oppressive conduct applications; for unpaid source deductions, the director's liability may be the full amount withheld plus interest and penalties; a fine of CAD 100,000 and imprisonment of up to two years is possible for fraudulent concealment; travel bans may be imposed under the Criminal Code if a warrant is issued for failure to appear, but not for civil judgment alone

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for unpaid wages, source deductions, and certain environmental and employment liabilities; the veil is pierced when the corporation is a mere agent, the shareholders are the undisclosed principals, or the corporate structure is a fraud; personal assets, including foreign real estate and bank accounts, may be seized through reciprocal enforcement in treaty jurisdictions; travel bans under judicial interim release conditions or an arrest warrant are issued where criminal fraud is charged; officers may face a prohibition on being a director under the BC Business Corporations Act for up to 10 years if convicted of indictable fraud
AML / Anti-Money Laundering Red Flags

Statutory sanction: Under PCMLTFA, a money services business or any entity that is engaged in specified transactions must report suspicious transactions, attempted transactions, and large cash transactions exceeding CAD 10,000 to FINTRAC; failure to comply can lead to administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 10 million for a corporation and up to CAD 500,000 for a director or employee; for e-commerce selling die-cast models, the business is not automatically a reporting entity unless it operates as a money services business or a dealer in precious metals, but if PayPal or wire transfers are received and the business is not a money services business, the AML obligations are limited; nevertheless, if the corporation is used to launder proceeds of crime by invoicing for fictitious sales of die-cast models, the Criminal Code money laundering offence carries a maximum imprisonment of 10 years and a fine at the discretion of the court

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly cause the corporation to launder proceeds or fail to implement AML controls can be charged as parties to the offence under section 21 of the Criminal Code; personal fines and imprisonment up to 10 years may be imposed; FINTRAC can issue a penalty directly against the director for failing to report, and the director may be personally assessed for the corporate penalty if the corporation is unable to pay; travel bans are a judicial intermediate measure imposed at bail stage if there is a risk of flight
Failure to File Information Returns and Non-Resident Reporting

Statutory sanction: Penalties for failure to file T106/T1134 are CAD 100 per day for a maximum of 100 days (CAD 10,000) per return, rising to CAD 2,500 per month if the corporation's gross revenue exceeds CAD 5 million, up to a maximum of 24 months; failure to file a foreign affiliate information return T1134 can result in 12 months of penalties and the loss of the foreign accrual property income (FAPI) exemption; failure to withhold Part XIII tax makes the Canadian payer liable for the full amount of unwithheld tax plus interest and penalties; criminal prosecution for failure to remit withheld amounts can carry imprisonment up to five years

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are personally liable under subsection 227.1(1) for the unremitted withholding amounts, and are jointly and severally liable with the corporation for the taxes that were required to be withheld and remitted; the CRA may assess a director even after the corporate tax is no longer collectable; the due diligence defence requires that the director exercised the same degree of care, diligence, and skill that a reasonably prudent person would have exercised; travel bans or personal criminal liability may be imposed only if the director knowingly failed to remit, but the civil director liability is a routine and powerful collection tool
Immigration / Tax Compliance Nexus and Permanent Establishment Red Flags

Statutory sanction: Under Article V of the Canada-US Tax Convention and similar DTAs, a dependent agent PE arises where a person habitually exercises authority to conclude contracts in Canada; the non-resident parent becomes liable to Canadian tax on Business Income attributable to the PE under section 2(3); failure to file a Part XIV branch tax return can lead to a 25% branch tax on after-tax income remitted to the home country plus reassessment penalties; if the entity is in fact a sham, the CRA can ignore the separate legal identity and assess the parent directly; criminal penalties for making false statements in a return (subsection 163(2)) are up to 100% of the tax evaded, and wilful tax evasion can lead to imprisonment up to five years

Director personal liability: Directors of the foreign parent may be assessed for the Canadian branch tax and penalty if they are officers of the Canadian permanent establishment and have authorized the reckless non-filing; the CRA can seek to collect against the foreign parent's Canadian assets and, through mutual legal assistance, against foreign assets; directors may be personally liable under the ATG doctrine if they signed a false certificate of compliance; travel bans are possible where a warrant for fraud or evasion charges is issued; willful blindness may be sufficient for criminal liability under the Criminal Code
