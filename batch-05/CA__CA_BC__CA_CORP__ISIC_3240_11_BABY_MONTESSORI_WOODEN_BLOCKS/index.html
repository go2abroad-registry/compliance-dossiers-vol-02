Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) - GST/HST and Corporate Income Tax Directorate, via My Business Account portal; British Columbia Ministry of Finance for Provincial Sales Tax (PST) and BC corporate tax administration

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must obtain a Business Number (BN) from the CRA. Registration is made online through CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) by selecting the GST/HST account and the Import/Export account. BC PST registration for a BC place of business is completed through the BC OneStop/BC Revenue portal using the BN reference number. The resulting BN plus a two-digit program account suffix (e.g., RT for GST/HST) constitutes the statutory TIN/VAT ID.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must obtain a Business Number (BN) from the CRA. Registration is made online through CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) by selecting the GST/HST account and the Import/Export account. BC PST registration for a BC place of business is completed through the BC OneStop/BC Revenue portal using the BN reference number. The resulting BN plus a two-digit program account suffix (e.g., RT for GST/HST) constitutes the statutory TIN/VAT ID.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Not applicable. Canada does not provide a check-the-box or hybrid mismatch entity election for domestic or inbound corporations under the Income Tax Act. A Canadian business corporation is taxed at the entity level. Non-resident corporations may elect to claim treaty-based relief and may voluntarily register for GST/HST even below the mandatory threshold, but no domestic entity classification election is available.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000; mandatory registration is required when worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in any calendar quarter or in the four preceding consecutive calendar quarters. Voluntary registration is permitted for exporters to claim zero-rating and recover input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods are zero-rated under the Excise Tax Act when they are sold for export to a non-resident and the exporter retains proof that the goods were physically delivered outside Canada. Eco-friendly beechwood Montessori blocks exported by a BC corporation qualify as zero-rated when title passes to the non-resident buyer, shipping originates from Canada, and the goods leave the country within the timeframe accepted by CRA administrative policy. Services performed for non-residents on the goods also qualify for zero-rating if not part of a Canadian taxable supply.

  • CBSA Export Declaration (A8) for goods valued at CAD 2,000 or more
  • Commercial invoice and customs invoice describing buyer, seller, and export transaction
  • Bill of Lading or other multimodal transport waybill showing pickup in Canada and foreign destination
  • Packing list with HS classification for wooden toys
  • Inward remittance certificate or bank remittance advice confirming receipt from the non-resident customer
  • Non-resident purchase order or written contract establishing the supply to a foreign person

Canada does not mandate real-time electronic fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government API clearance for GST/HST or BC PST. E-invoicing may be exchanged voluntarily under the Pan-Canadian Peppol BIS 3.0 network through a Canadian e-Invoicing Service Provider. CRA requires GST/HST returns to be filed electronically via GST/HST NETFILE or GST Access, and electronic records supporting zero-rated export claims must be retained in readable format.

CRA may audit export transactions for several years after filing and can retrospectively reassess a zero-rating claim if the documentation is incomplete or the goods did not leave Canada. Unsubstantiated zero-rated sales are clawed back at the 5% GST/HST rate plus provincial sales tax exposure where applicable, with interest and penalties under the Excise Tax Act for false statements or omissions. Willful misstatements or fraudulent documentary schemes can trigger gross-negligence penalties up to 25% of the tax sought to be evaded, criminal prosecution, and joint and several liability for directors or officers.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully convertible and liberalized currency regime, consistent with IMF Article VIII obligations. No exchange control licenses, prior approvals, or capital transfer permits are required for current or capital account transactions, including foreign direct investment, intercompany loans, trade payments, or dividends. Profit remittances to non-resident shareholders are unrestricted from a banking and FX perspective; however, they are subject to Canadian Part XIII withholding tax (typically 25%, reducible under applicable tax treaties) and must be substantiated by corporate documentation, directors' resolutions, and financial statements to satisfy the remitting bank's AML/KYC policies. The Canadian dollar is free-floating, and both residents and non-residents may hold, settle, and convert foreign currency without statutory limitation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory surrender, mandatory conversion, repatriation timeline, or designated foreign currency account requirements for export receipts under Canadian law. Exporters may retain proceeds in CAD or any foreign currency at Canadian or offshore banks at their discretion. For corporate tax and customs purposes, the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate is used to convert foreign currency receipts into Canadian dollars for income recognition and GST/HST reporting. Export documentation must be retained to support trade statistics reporting and any potential CBSA or CRA audit.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as the central monetary authority; foreign exchange and currency administration policy is vested in the Department of Finance Canada, with operational AML/CFT oversight by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) and trade compliance by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low as a primary treasury depository or capital escrow vehicle; Medium-to-High as a complementary settlement rail. Canada does not recognize 'Electronic Money Institution' (EMI) as a distinct banking charter. Non-bank fintechs operating in Canada must be registered as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC, and can offer digital wallets, cross-border payments, and foreign exchange conversion. Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections from foreign founders, venture investors, or intercompany parent entities must be routed via SWIFT wire transfers (MT103 or MT202) to the Canadian corporate operational account. Recommended BPM (Balance of Payments) purpose codes include: 'DTC' for direct investment equity, 'DD' for intercompany debt, and 'DE' for reinvested earnings or capital transfers. The bank will require a completed foreign currency wire instruction referencing the corporate account number, legal entity name, and a narrative matching the purpose. For equity contributions, upload the signed subscription agreement and board resolution; for intercompany loans, upload the loan agreement and CRA thin-capitalization / transfer-pricing documentation. For conversions from USD/EUR to CAD, the receiving bank's spot rate may be used, or alternatively a scheduled forward trade with the bank's treasury desk can be executed to lock in the conversion. Verification within the bank's compliance system will include source-of-funds proof from the transferring entity, such as audited financial statements, investment fund statements, or shareholders' personal bank statements.For settlement of overseas trade liabilities—raw wood materials, water-based paints, packaging, and finished goods from Chinese or European suppliers—the recommended clearing rails are: (1) SWIFT MT103 single/multiple bank-to-bank wires for open-account transactions under documentary control; (2) Commercial Letters of Credit (L/C) issued via SWIFT MT700, with MT707 amendments and MT798 electronic instructions, providing documentary security for high-value bulk raw-material imports; (3) CAD, USD, and EUR clearing through Lynx (Canada's high-value payment system) for domestic leg disbursements, and SWIFT/CHIPS/TARGET2 for respective cross-border legs; and (4) treasury workstations or banking APIs (e.g., RBC, TD, BMO's XML payments) for batch settlement. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 10,000,000 (gross revenue threshold for preparing Local File and Master File; Form T1062 required for transactions exceeding CAD 1,000,000 for goods or CAD 500,000 for services)

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have specific ESR, but applies General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and requires economic substance for treaty benefits; transactions lacking substance may be recharacterized.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Penalty of 50% to 200% of tax evaded, plus interest; criminal prosecution with fines up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unpaid corporate taxes under Section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act if they failed to withhold or remit; also criminal liability for fraud if they participated in evasion.
Shell Company

Statutory sanction: GAAR may recharacterize transactions; penalties up to 25% of tax avoided; potential criminal charges if fraudulent.

Director personal liability: Directors may be held liable for tax debts if the company is a sham; also personal liability for fraudulent conveyances.
AML Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 for individuals and CAD 2,000,000 for entities; criminal fines up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for penalties if they knowingly participated or failed to exercise due diligence.
