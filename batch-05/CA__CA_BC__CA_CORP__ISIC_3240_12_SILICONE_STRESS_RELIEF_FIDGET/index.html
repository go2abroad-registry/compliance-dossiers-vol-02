Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada – federal GST/HST and income tax administration; British Columbia Ministry of Finance – Provincial Sales Tax (PST) administration. Primary portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-business-portal.html); BC PST via eTaxBC (https://etaxbc.gov.bc.ca).

E-government filing portal

A foreign corporation obtains a 9-digit Canadian Business Number (BN) with a GST/HST program account (RT) by applying through CRA Business Registration Online or by filing Form RC1. Non-resident corporations must provide certified copies of constituent documents, registered office or Canadian agent/service address particulars, and director identification. Once the BN is issued, the taxpayer must register separately with BC for PST using the BC Provincial Sales Tax application where retail sales in BC are contemplated; all registrations can be completed remotely and are necessary before filing obligations arise.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation / Territorial_Scope: A Canadian resident business corporation is taxed on its worldwide income; a non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada is taxed on Canadian-source income. The entity is a separate taxpayer, not pass-through, and is a taxable supplier for GST/HST purposes unless the small-supplier exemption applies.

Available election forms

Canada has no equivalent to the U.S. check-the-box entity election; corporate status is determined by the corporate statutes. Relevant elections include Income Tax Act section 216 (non-resident rental and timber income optional return), section 217 (non-resident elective return on employment income), and various GST/HST administrative elections such as the net-tax simplified accounting election for small suppliers. There is no elective hybrid-mismatch regime; Canada applies mandatory anti-hybrid rules under the Income Tax Act and earnings-stripping / excessive interest and financing expenses limitations.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold for GST/HST: a non-resident entity becomes required to register once taxable supplies (worldwide, excluding exempt supplies) exceed CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter or in the last four consecutive calendar quarters; registration is effective as of the date the threshold is exceeded. For BC PST, a separate dealer registration obligation arises for sellers making taxable retail sales of tangible personal property in British Columbia unless an enumerated exemption applies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Part V, Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act, supplies of tangible personal property (including food-grade silicone fidget toys) qualify for the 0% GST/HST rate when the supplier ships or delivers the goods to a common carrier, consignee, or customs broker for immediate export, or when the goods are delivered outside Canada in the supplier's ordinary course of business and the recipient exports the goods within 60 days. The supplier must obtain and retain documentary evidence of actual export. Certain services supplied by a resident to a non-resident in respect of property outside Canada may also be zero-rated, but services of quality assurance, product testing, or domestic handling remain taxable unless a specific zero-rating provision applies.

  • B13A / Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) export declaration where the export is subject to reporting or controlled / regulated goods are involved
  • Customs Export Declaration (air waybill / bill of lading / cargo control document showing export routing)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical movement out of Canada
  • Commercial invoice stating the GST/HST registration number and the contractual export terms
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / SWIFT confirmation / bank FX transfer evidence from the overseas buyer
  • Carrier proof of export, terminal receipts, or bonded warehouse removal records
  • Production and title-transfer records evidencing the sale and export of the food-grade silicone compression-molded fidgets

Canada imposes no real-time fiscal clearance, no mandatory pre-clearance API, and no digital stamping requirement for invoices. Suppliers must issue tax invoices containing the supplier's GST/HST registration number and prescribed invoice particulars, and records must be retained in English or French. Electronic invoices issued through EDI, Peppol, or other interoperable platforms are accepted by CRA; BC PST retail receipts are subject to less formal invoice rules, but transaction records must substantiate exemptions claimed.

CRA aggressively audits export zero-rating claims. Missing or insufficient export documentation triggers reassessment to impose full 5% GST/HST on the export sale, with compound interest from the original filing date. False statements or omissions in relation to export zero-rating may result in penalties up to 25% of the net tax, gross negligence penalties up to 50% of the tax liability, and criminal sanctions including fines and imprisonment. Goods diverted to domestic consumption or not exported within the statutory 60-day period are subject to immediate clawback of zero-rated treatment and full tax re-collection, with the normal reassessment period extended where misrepresentation or fraud is found.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a liberalized and fully convertible currency regime. Capital account transactions are generally unrestricted, and the Canadian dollar (CAD) floats freely under a flexible exchange rate system. There are no exchange control laws requiring central bank approval for current account items, profit remittances, or foreign direct investment capital returns. Dividends, interest, royalties, and service payments may be remitted freely, subject to corporate law solvency tests (e.g., British Columbia Business Corporations Act s.73 for dividends) and tax withholding compliance under the Income Tax Act (e.g., Part XIII withholding on non-resident payments). No profit repatriation hurdles exist other than standard tax documentation (e.g., NR301 form for reduced withholding treaty rates).

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory export proceeds surrender requirements in Canada. Export receipts may be retained in any currency, held offshore, or converted freely. The Bank of Canada does not mandate conversion ratios or timelines for export earnings. All foreign currency receipts are subject to ordinary anti-money laundering (AML) reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), including FINTRAC reporting of large cash transactions (CAD 10,000+) and cross-border wire transfers of CAD 10,000 or more, but no monetary conversion obligation applies.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-High with legal constraints. Canada permits non-bank financial technology companies and electronic money institutions (EMIs) to hold funds and provide payment services under a money services business (MSB) registration with FINTRAC (for forex dealing, money transferring, or issuing/redeeming money orders). Canadian entities may use international digital EMIs (e.g., Wise, Payoneer, Airwallex) for cross-border collections and disbursements, but these EMIs are not licensed as banks in Canada and cannot take 'deposits' in the legal sense. Funds held with non-bank EMIs are not covered by the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC). For capital preservation and segregation, a tier-1 Canadian chartered bank account remains mandatory as the primary treasury anchor. SMEs with a Canadian incorporated entity may open EMI accounts remotely; however, the EMI's onboarding policies often require a Canadian operating address and director identification. For inbound capital from a non-resident parent, routing through a Canadian bank via SWIFT is safer for proof of compliance with the British Columbia Business Corporations Act and tax treaty benefit claims.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant route: (1) Shareholder executes a subscription agreement and pays a wire to the BC company's CAD or USD bank account at a Schedule I Canadian bank (e.g., RBC, TD) from its home-country bank via SWIFT. (2) Use payment purpose/code 'Shares and other equity – BO (B) / DEBT' or more specifically SWIFT MT103 field 20-58 with narrative 'INVEST-CA-Equity-CA-BC'; for U.S. wire transfers use Federal Reserve Wire (Fedwire) with OBI 'Equity injection under BCBCA s.68 / share subscription'. (3) Provide to the receiving Canadian bank: share subscription agreement, board resolution, updated register of directors/ shareholders, and possibly Form 1 – Notice of Article update if share issuance alters authorized capital. (4) For amounts over CAD 10,000, FINTRAC must receive an electronic funds transfer report (EFTR) if the transfer is internationally initiated / termininated in Canada; for amounts over CAD 100,000, Canadian banks must report to FINTRAC; ensure the supporting evidentiary bundle is pre-uploaded to the relationship manager to avoid a hold. (5) Alternative SVF (securities settlement) or structured loan injections: use a cross-border shareholder loan agreement, but the injection should be characterized as equity to avoid Part XIII withholding and thin-capitalization issues (Income Tax Act s.212, s.18(4)). E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium-High with legal constraints. Canada permits non-bank financial technology companies and electronic money institutions (EMIs) to hold funds and provide payment services under a money services business (MSB) registration with FINTRAC (for forex dealing, money transferring, or issuing/redeeming money orders). Canadian entities may use international digital EMIs (e.g., Wise, Payoneer, Airwallex) for cross-border collections and disbursements, but these EMIs are not licensed as banks in Canada and cannot take 'deposits' in the legal sense. Funds held with non-bank EMIs are not covered by the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC). For capital preservation and segregation, a tier-1 Canadian chartered bank account remains mandatory as the primary treasury anchor. SMEs with a Canadian incorporated entity may open EMI accounts remotely; however, the EMI's onboarding policies often require a Canadian operating address and director identification. For inbound capital from a non-resident parent, routing through a Canadian bank via SWIFT is safer for proof of compliance with the British Columbia Business Corporations Act and tax treaty benefit claims.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined federal and British Columbia general corporate income tax (15% federal Part I plus 12% BC; federal rate is after the 10% provincial abatement and 13% general rate reduction).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Canadian Income Tax Act (section 212(2)) on dividends paid to a non-resident shareholder, unless a reduced rate is available under an income tax treaty (e.g., Canada-US treaty: 15% portfolio, 5% substantial interest, and down to 0% for qualifying pension plans).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Contemporaneous Local File and Master File documentation is required where the aggregate fair market value of reportable cross-border non-arm's length transactions exceeds CAD 5 million in a taxation year (penalty triggers under s.247(3)). The federal Country-by-Country Report (CRA Form RC2948) applies to MNE groups with consolidated group revenue of at least CAD 750 million. Even below the CAD 5 million threshold, the CRA will demand documentation for the silicone fidget raw-material imports and foreign intercompany commission arrangements if the facts reasonably indicate a transfer-pricing risk.

Economic substance scope: Canada imposes no separate codified Economic Substance Regulations (ESR), but the CRA applies the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines and the statutory General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245 ITA). Absence of real risk, functions, employees, premises, and independent financial decision-making in the BC entity will allow the CRA to disregard or recharacterise the fidget selling structure. A zero-employee 'sleeve' entity used only to repatriate profits is audit-high-risk, exposing the entity to reallocation of all profit into Canada.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Civil: 50% gross-negligence penalty on the unreported tax and interest. Criminal: s.239(1) ITA indictment carries up to 14 years imprisonment and an unlimited fine; summary conviction carries up to 2 years imprisonment and a CAD 10,000 to CAD 100,000 fine; property forfeiture may accompany conviction.

Director personal liability: A director who knew or ought to have known of the underreporting may be held jointly liable for the corporation's Part XIII and payroll withholdings (s.227.1 ITA); criminal directors face travel bans, extradition requests, passport denial, and imprisonment.
Artificial Shell / General Anti-Avoidance Rule

Statutory sanction: CRA will apply GAAR to deny any 'tax benefit' (s.245(2)), reassess on a substance-over-form basis, impose the 50% gross-negligence penalty, and disallow loss-carryover benefits; interest accrues from the original filing date.

Director personal liability: The court may pierce the corporate veil where the corporation is a 'sham'; directors become personally liable for unpaid corporate taxes and may be prohibited from operating other businesses; enforcement can include Personal Property Registry liens and asset attachment.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA reallocates taxable income under s.247(2); a transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment is assessed under s.247(3), and the corporation loses the ability to claim treaty relief for the same amount through double-taxative adjustments; interest and daily compounded penalties on the deficiency accrue.

Director personal liability: Directors personally liable for failure to withhold non-resident tax on mispriced cross-border fees; those signing false TP certificates may face civil fraud claims by minority shareholders and CRA 'responsible officer' investigations.
AML and Economic Sanctions Evasion

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation; contraventions of the Criminal Code money-laundering provision (s.462.31) are serious indictable offences carrying up to 10 years imprisonment; customs fraud penalties include seizure and forfeiture of the shipment and a penalty equal to 100% of the value of the goods.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly implement structured payment designs face personal criminal prosecution and money-laundering charges, travel bans, and extradition; personal in rem orders against their bank accounts are possible; the corporate veil is peeled where the company is used as an instrument of financial crime.
