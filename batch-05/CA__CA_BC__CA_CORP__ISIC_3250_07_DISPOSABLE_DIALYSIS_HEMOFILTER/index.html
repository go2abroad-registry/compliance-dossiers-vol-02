Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — federal tax authority and online compliance portal 'My Business Account' (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html); British Columbia Ministry of Finance (Provincial Sales Tax (PST) administration, PST e-Services portal) and BC Registries and Online Services (extra-provincial corporate registration and annual reporting).

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity must first obtain a federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA, which is a 9-digit base number followed by a two-letter four-character program account code (e.g., 'RM' for corporate income tax, 'RT' for GST/HST, 'RP' for payroll). Procedure: (1) incorporate or extra-provincially register in BC through BC Registries and obtain the BC incorporation/extra-provincial licence number; (2) file CRA Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) together with Form RC171 (Application for Registration of a Non-Resident Corporation) and certified constitutional documents, a current director list, and the foreign corporation's certificate of status; non-residents are generally processed by the CRA Non-Resident and International Tax Services Office; (3) on approval the CRA issues the BN, and the GST/HST 'RT' account is established if the entity is not a small supplier or voluntarily registers; (4) the entity then registers separately for BC PST through the BC Ministry of Finance (Form FIN 419 or online PST registration) because BC does not participate in the federal HST and maintains an independent retail sales tax regime; (5) the BN is the single federal tax identifier used for corporate income tax, GST/HST, payroll, and customs (bonds and importer number) and is also the VAT/registration identifier recognized by foreign counterparties for output tax invoicing.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a British Columbia incorporated Business Corporation (Inc./Corp., ELF code 8615) is a Canadian-resident corporation for Income Tax Act purposes and is fully taxed at the entity level on worldwide income; Canada has no US-style pass-through check-the-box for corporations; limited liability and separate identity are recognized, and the only pass-through or bifurcated treatment available arises through explicit statutory elections (e.g., s. 216(1) rental income election, s. 217 employment election, and partnership or trust classifications that are inappropriate where the entity is incorporated).

Available election forms

Canada employs a legal-entity classification system rather than an elective pass-through check-the-box. Relevant elections include: (i) Income Tax Act s. 85 tax-deferred rollover election on transfers of property to a corporation in exchange for shares; (ii) s. 86(1) share reorganization election; (iii) s. 216(1) election enabling a non-resident landlord to file a net-income Canadian return for rental income in place of Part XIII 25% withholding; (iv) s. 216.1 election for timber royalties; (v) s. 217 election for non-resident employees to report Canadian employment income on a net basis; (vi) CRA capital cost allowance pooling elections and subsection 1100(2.2) rate elections for manufacturing and processing (M&P) equipment and dialysis-related medical device machinery; (vii) GST/HST election under s. 156.1 for closely related corporations to deem taxable supplies at fair market value or nil consideration; (viii) hybrid mismatch measures are mandatory anti-abuse rules (deduction denial and inclusion rules aligned with OECD BEPS Action 2) rather than elective check-the-box mechanisms, with limited transactional elections for foreign branch timing and section 20.8 branch-loss recapture.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies over any 12 consecutive months (or exceeding CAD 30,000 alone in a single calendar quarter) triggers mandatory GST/HST registration; a non-resident carrying on business in Canada must register before making taxable supplies unless qualified as a small supplier, and voluntary registration is allowed to claim input tax credits for export manufacturing activities. BC PST: there is no statutory PST registration threshold in CAD; a vendor carrying on business in BC, including a non-resident vendor or online marketplace operator with BC-connected economic nexus, must register with the BC Ministry of Finance, and PST applies to retail sale of dialysis consumables delivered to BC customers, while exports and sales for resale are generally exempt with the appropriate exemption certificate.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods are zero-rated under s. 1 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act where the supplier makes a taxable supply by way of sale and delivers the goods to a destination outside Canada or ships them by common carrier to a foreign consignee, with the supplier retaining evidence proving exportation within a reasonable period. For dialysis hemofilters (hollow-fiber PES dialyzers manufactured and potted under ISIC 3250 / NAICS 339112), the sale to a non-resident purchaser will be zero-rated at 0% GST even if the goods are later resold or used in a foreign dialysis clinic; services performed on goods destined for a non-resident may also be zero-rated under Part V s. 7 if the service supplier is not registered and the service relates to tangible personal property delivered outside Canada. Zero-rating is a privilege that requires the exporter to: (i) have a valid BN and GST/HST registration; (ii) invoice without charging GST; (iii) physically export the goods within the normal course of business; (iv) receive payment from a non-resident in convertible foreign currency; and (v) maintain a contemporaneous export evidence file. Article 9018.90 (HTS) medical device export classifications remain zero-rated regardless of the domestic GST treatment of medical supplies.

  • Customs Export Declaration — CBSA B13A / Canada Automated Export Declaration (CAED) filing through the Canada Border Services Agency for goods valued above CAD 2,000 or controlled or regulated goods, showing HS 9018.90, exporter BN, ultimate destination country, and value
  • Commercial invoice and packing list describing the disposable dialysis hemofilter product, quantity, unit value, terms of sale (Incoterms), and indication 'Zero-Rated Export — GST Not Applicable'
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill in the name of the Canadian exporter, endorsed with the foreign port of discharge and clearly evidencing physical export from Canada (e.g., CBSA export-stamped B13A or carrier proof of sail/departure)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) or Swift MT103 wire confirmation from a Canadian bank evidencing payment in foreign currency from the non-resident buyer within 180 days of invoice
  • Proof of non-resident status of the purchaser (e.g., foreign registration certificate, purchase order on foreign letterhead, or non-resident declaration) supporting the place of supply
  • CBSA export transaction report (A6 or carrier manifest extract) and, where applicable, a certificate of origin if the buyer claims preference under a free trade agreement

Canada does not impose a real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control (CTC), API clearance, or digital stamping obligation for GST/HST invoices. The GST/HST return for a non-resident registrant must be filed electronically once annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 1,000,000, using CRA's electronic services (GST/HST NETFILE or My Business Account); all other filings may be by paper. E-invoicing is voluntary and Canada is aligned with the Peppol framework on a de facto basis, with Quebec mandating future electronic invoice clearance for B2B transactions, but no federal mandate for dialysis manufacturers exporting from BC. Invoices for zero-rated exports must be retained in paper or electronic form and must explicitly indicate 'Zero-Rated Export' with the exporter's BN and the purchaser's foreign address, because CRA assesses exporters for uncollected GST where the 5% output tax was not charged and export evidence is deficient.

CRA rigorously audits exporters of medical consumables for 'phantom exports', shell buyers, and split-order schemes. If export evidence is incomplete or false, CRA will: reassess the zero-rated supply at 5% GST with statutory daily compound interest; deny input tax credits attributable to the export (including ITC recovery of embedded Vancouver/BC development costs translated into CRA currency); issue a s. 296(1) penalty equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the unremitted GST or false refund where the exporter knowingly supplied false documentation on routine 4-year reassessment (or indefinitely where misrepresentation is shown); impose s. 280 GST/HST penalties for negligent false statements (up to 50% of the amount at issue); apply Income Tax Act s. 163(2) gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understated corporate income if export proceeds were diverted; and refer criminal evasion under Income Tax Act s. 239(1)(a) and Excise Tax Act s. 327, for which a summary conviction carries up to CAD 10,000 in fines plus up to one year imprisonment and an indictable conviction carries up to 5 years imprisonment and fines of between 25% and 125% of the evaded amounts. Additional clawback consequences include: revocation or denial of the corporate income tax manufacturing and processing deduction for the same facility, mandatory re-filing of GST/HST NETE files for all prior export periods, and inclusion of the exporter on CRA's international audit case list; directors are jointly and severally liable under s. 323 ETA for unremitted GST.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital accounts, with no surrender or license requirement for any payment type. Dividend, interest, royalty, and management-fee remittances clear freely via the Canadian banking system subject only to non-resident withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (28% pre-treaty standard is reduced under the Canada-China Income Tax Agreement to 10-15% for dividends depending on ownership, 10% for interest, 10% for royalties). Structural hurdles are limited to: (i) thin capitalization restrictions in s. 18(4)-(7) of the Income Tax Act capping debt-to-equity at 1.5:1 for foreign equity owners (effective 2023, replaced with the Excessive Interest and Financing Expenses Limitation (EIFEL) rules for tax years beginning 1 October 2023); (ii) paid-up capital maintenance rules for capital escrow releases; and (iii) the Investment Canada Act net-benefit review regime for acquisitions by foreign state-owned enterprises above the CAD 519 million threshold (2024): BLUE-print requires pre-filing with Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada where a PRC parent company is an SOE.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no repatriation requirement applies to export proceeds in Canada. The exporter CA_CORP is entitled to hold USD, EUR, CNY, or any fully convertible currency indefinitely in a designated foreign-currency account with a Schedule I bank. Mandatory compliance events: (i) FINTRAC Large Cash Transaction Report (CTR) when any cash deposit or withdrawal is CAD 10,000 or more in a single transaction or 24-hour sequence; (ii) CBSA Cross-Border Currency Reporting (Form E311) for physical import/export of currency or bearer instruments valued at CAD 10,000 or more; (iii) realization of foreign exchange gains/losses under s. 39(2) of the Income Tax Act upon settlement of export receivables, requiring a documented hedging policy to avoid anomalous taxable income; and (iv) Canada Border Services Agency Export Declaration through the Canadian Export Reporting System for shipments over CAD 2,000 (the medical device category is not an export-controlled good).

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) exercises monetary policy and manages official international reserves; prudential regulation of authorized banks rests with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); all AML/KYC-related transaction surveillance and large-value record keeping are governed by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); cross-border physical currency reporting is enforced by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA). Canada does not maintain an exchange-control authority; there are no restrictions on currency conversion or remittances beyond fiscal and reporting statutes (Currency Act R.S.C. 1985, c. C-52; Financial Administration Act R.S.C. 1985, c. F-11; Income Tax Act Part XIII).

Airwallex Fit: High

Low-Feasibility (Restricted): Canadian fintech entities (Wealthsimple for Business, Koho, Vault, Dave) are not authorized foreign banks and cannot provide commercial letter of credit issuance, trade finance, or CAD/USD corporate FX forward lines. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: There is no fixed statutory minimum transaction threshold below which transfer-pricing documentation is not required. All controlled cross-border transactions between the Canadian corporation and non-resident affiliates must be supported by contemporaneous documentation under section 247. Canada requires Country-by-Country Reporting for multinational groups with consolidated revenue of EUR 750 million or more (approximately C$1.1 billion), and the CRA will generally expect Local File/Master File-style documentation for material intercompany transactions involving dialysis consumables manufacturing, IP royalties, and cost-sharing.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime comparable to UAE/Caribbean offshore centres. However, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under section 245 and the treaty-limitation-on-benefits provisions require genuine economic substance, commercial rationale, and actual business risk in BC. A dialysis filter manufacturer claiming manufacturing profit must have real facilities, employees, inventory, orders, purchasing decisions, and production risk in Canada; otherwise CRA may recharacterize transactions, deny treaty benefits, and assess penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: Civil penalty under subsection 163(2) is 50% of the tax evaded, plus compound daily interest. Criminal prosecution under section 239 exposes fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years on indictment.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate, cause, or acquiesce in filing false returns are jointly and severally liable for unpaid withholdings under section 227.1, subject to transferee assessments under section 160, and can be personally prosecuted; corporate veil piercing is available for fraud or sham arrangements.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR can recharacterize the entire arrangement, deny deductions and treaty relief, and reassess tax as if the substance existed at the operating company level. The BC Registrar under the Business Corporations Act may strike the company for non-filing; CRA can impose section 162 administrative penalties for failure to file required information returns.

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell company may be assessed personally for unremitted GST/HST and source deductions; courts may disregard separate corporate personality if the entity is used as a sham or cloak for fraud, and directors can face civil restitution, asset freezing, travel bans, and imprisonment.
Transfer Pricing / Documentation Defects

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust taxable income to the arm's length amount. Under subsection 247(3), a 10% penalty is applied to the net transfer-pricing adjustment if the taxpayer cannot reasonably demonstrate that the price is arm's length or that contemporaneous documentation existed. Gross negligence penalties and disclosure penalties for reportable transactions may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve or sign returns with knowledge of transfer-pricing non-compliance may be treated as tax preparers for gross negligence penalties; personal liability attaches for knowingly condoning fraudulent misstatements. In a criminal case, directors involved will be jointly liable for fines and imprisonment, and the corporate veil will not shield fraudulent conduct.
Anti-Money Laundering / Financial Crimes

Statutory sanction: Violations of the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act expose administrative monetary penalties of up to C$1,000,000 per violation for corporations. Criminal money laundering offences under the Criminal Code carry imprisonment of up to 10 years and unlimited fines at the discretion of the court. Federal financial institutions may freeze accounts, and regulatory licenses under the Medical Devices Regulations may be suspended.

Director personal liability: Directors directly participating in or knowingly facilitating money laundering are personally exposed to criminal prosecution, travel bans, forfeiture of assets, restitution orders, and imprisonment; the corporate veil cannot protect individuals when the corporation is a tool for illicit financial flows.
