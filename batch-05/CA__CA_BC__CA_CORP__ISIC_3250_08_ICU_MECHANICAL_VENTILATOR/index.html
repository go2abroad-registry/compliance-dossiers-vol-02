Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — federal corporate income tax and GST/HST — portal: CRA My Business Account and Business Registration Online (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html); British Columbia Ministry of Finance (Consumer Taxation Branch) — BC Provincial Sales Tax — portal: eTaxBC (https://etax.gov.bc.ca). Customs-related Business Number accounts are administered by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) in coordination with CRA.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) and, as applicable, a GST/HST account (RT), federal corporate income tax account (RC), payroll account (RP), and customs account (RM). Non-resident corporations apply via Business Registration Online or Form RC1, identifying the corporate charter, registered particulars, and a Canadian agent or representative for service. GST/HST registration is mandatory for a non-resident person making taxable supplies in Canada who is not a small supplier (worldwide Canadian taxable supplies, including zero-rated exports, exceeding CAD 30,000 over four consecutive calendar quarters or in a single calendar quarter) or who carries on a commercial activity in Canada; CRA may require security under ETA s. 246. The corporation separately registers for BC Provincial Sales Tax (PST) with the BC Ministry of Finance through eTaxBC when it makes taxable retail sales in BC, whether through a physical establishment or, for remote suppliers without physical presence, once taxable sales into BC exceed the small-seller exemption threshold.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a US-style check-the-box election; a Business Corporation is mandatorily taxed at the entity level. Available statutory election mechanisms include the ITA s. 85 rollover election for tax-deferred property transfers into the corporation, the ITA s. 89 paid-up capital designation, the ITA s. 261 functional currency election, the Excise Tax Act s. 156 election and nil-consideration election for closely related corporations, and the hybrid-mismatch limitation elections aligned with OECD BEPS Action 2 (disallowance or deduction-denial mechanics addressed to duplicate deductions on hybrid instruments and payments through hybrid entities). All elections are made by prescribed CRA forms or schedules attached to the T2 corporate income tax return and none confers discretionary entity-classification latitude.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — a supplier ceases to be a small supplier and must register for GST/HST when its total taxable supplies made in Canada (including zero-rated export supplies) exceed CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter or over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; for non-residents, registration is mandatory upon carrying on commercial activity in Canada or exceeding the threshold, whichever triggers first. In BC, PST registration is required of a person carrying on business by making taxable retail sales in the province; remote suppliers without physical presence must register once their taxable sales into BC exceed CAD 10,000 in the trailing 12 months, subject to marketplace facilitator rules.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under s. 22 of the Excise Tax Act, a supply of tangible personal property — including ICU invasive mechanical ventilators — is zero-rated for GST/HST where the supplier sells the goods and, at or before delivery, they are destined for export, under terms that require the property to be delivered outside Canada, and the goods are in fact exported as soon as reasonably practicable. Qualifying ventilators that are Health Canada-licensed medical devices enumerated in Part II of Schedule VI to the ETA may also be zero-rated on domestic sales to qualifying purchasers. Offshore services (installation, commissioning, calibration, and training) supplied in respect of the exported ventilators are zero-rated where the recipient is a non-resident, the service is performed or consumed outside Canada, and place-of-supply rules are satisfied. Export zero-rating preserves input tax credit recovery on manufacturing costs, yielding a net refund position in the exporter's GST/HST return. Canada imposes no statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export zero-rating; the 180-day benchmark reflected in this dossier is the customary trade-finance and CRA transfer-pricing audit benchmark, and longer settlement terms under a documentary letter of credit remain valid if properly evidenced.

  • Commercial invoice or pro-forma invoice identifying the non-resident purchaser, ultimate destination country, and Incoterms delivery terms

Canada imposes no statutory real-time fiscal clearance, no digital stamping, and no government pre-approval of invoices. A GST/HST registrant must issue an invoice containing the supplier's legal name, BN, date, description of the supply, and amount of tax. Electronic filing of GST/HST returns is mandatory for registrants whose annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 1.5 million, using GST/HST NETFILE, CRA telecommunication services, or XML gateway integration; CRA supports PEPPOL-based cross-border e-invoicing and API data exchange, but invoice clearance is not a condition of validity. For exported goods, the CBSA outward export declaration (CADEX/CARMM) is the operative clearance evidence.

CRA export audits require the complete documentation bundle and strict retention for six years. If export evidence is missing or inconsistent, CRA re-characterizes the supply as taxable domestic, assesses 5% GST/HST on the full consideration, denies corresponding input tax credits and refunds, and imposes arrears interest (statutory corporate rates compounded daily, typically 8% to 10%) plus civil penalties under ETA s. 280 for failure to register, collect, file, or remit (up to 10% of net tax), and gross-negligence penalties of 50% of the under-assessed net tax for false statements or wilful misrepresentation. The normal four-year reassessment window extends to six years for neglect or misrepresentation and is unlimited for fraud; criminal exposure survives under Criminal Code s. 380, and the CRA may demand additional security under ETA s. 246 before restoring zero-rating privileges. For BC PST, an improperly claimed export exemption gives rise to reassessment of the 7% PST, late penalties of up to 10%, compound interest, and a minimum four-year audit lookback.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Form T106 reporting is mandatory where the aggregate value of transactions with a non-arm's length non-resident exceeds CAD 1,000,000 in a taxation year. Canada maintains no statutory CAD threshold for contemporaneous Local File / Master File documentation, but CRA expects full OECD-aligned documentation for any material intercompany transaction. Country-by-Country (CbC) reporting applies to Canadian multinational groups with consolidated revenue of CAD 750,000,000 or more.

Economic substance scope: Canada is not an Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) jurisdiction in the UAE/Caribbean sense, but economic substance is embedded in Canadian law through: (1) the corporate residence test of central management and control; (2) ITA s.245 General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) which recharacterizes avoidance transactions lacking genuine business substance; (3) treaty access requiring real, substantive Canadian operation and not artful treaty shopping; (4) transfer pricing recharacterization under s.247(2) for arrangements that would not have been made by arm's length parties. A Vancouver entity engaged solely in invoicing and dividend routing without administrative presence, employees, or decision-making functions will be treated as a nominee or conduit, risking GAAR reassessment, treaty denial, and inclusion of profits in the ultimate parent's Canadian income.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: ITA s.239(1): upon summary conviction, a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and up to 2 years imprisonment; upon indictment, a fine of 100% to 200% of the tax and up to 5 years imprisonment. Civil gross-negligence penalty under s.163(2) equals 50% of the understated tax. Restitution orders, seizure of assets, and publication of taxpayer defaults may also follow.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize, assent to, or participate in the filing of false statements can be jointly and severally assessed under ITA s.160 and s.227.1, subjected to civil restitution, asset freezes, and criminal prosecution. Travel restrictions/passport surrender may be imposed as a bail condition awaiting trial; conviction can result in imprisonment of up to 5 years.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Deficiency

Statutory sanction: ITA s.247(3) penalty: 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the statutory de minimis threshold, in addition to reassessment of taxable income and compounding interest. T106 late filing attracts penalties of up to CAD 2,500 per return, with additional penalties for knowingly filing false information. CRA may also recharacterize the transaction under s.247(2).

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for TP penalties, but directors who sign false or incomplete T106 filings or instruct manipulation of intercompany prices may be prosecuted under s.239 and be personally liable for the resulting tax, interest, and penalties where the corporate entity is a sham or conduit.
Shell Company / Economic Substance Abuse / GAAR / Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: Reassessment under GAAR (ITA s.245) disallowing the tax benefit and imposing interest; treaty benefits may be denied under the MLI Principal Purpose Test or applicable limitation on benefits clauses. Penalty for avoidance transactions under s.163.2 may apply to tax planners, and s.247(2) can reallocate profits. Monetary exposure frequently exceeds 200% of the tax benefit once interest and penalties accrue.

Director personal liability: Courts may pierce the corporate veil where the corporation is a mere facade or agent. Directors of the shell can be held personally liable for unremitted Part XIII withholdings under s.227.1, for wages under the BC Business Corporations Act, and may face civil conspiracy or fraudulent conveyance claims; serious cases attract criminal liability and imprisonment.
AML, Trade Sanctions, and Export Invoice Fraud

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, entities may face administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation plus suspension; criminal sanctions include fines and 5 years imprisonment. The Customs Act imposes penalties up to 50% of the value of goods for false import/export declarations. Violation of Canada's sanctions regulations (SEMA/UN) carries fines and up to 10 years imprisonment for facilitation of sanctioned exports.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance officers can be prosecuted directly for money laundering and sanctions offences; personal fines range into the hundreds of thousands of CAD and imprisonment up to 10 years. Confiscation orders may extend to personal assets where proceeds of unlawful activity are traced, and senior officers may be barred from acting as directors under the Canada Business Corporations Act.
