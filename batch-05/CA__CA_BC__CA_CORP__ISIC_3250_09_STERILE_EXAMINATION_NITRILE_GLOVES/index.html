Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC), via the Business Registration Online (BRO) portal at canada.ca; provincial tax administration for British Columbia is the Ministry of Finance, Consumer Tax Branch, and BC Corporate Registry for extraprovincial registration

E-government filing portal

A foreign corporation must first obtain a federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA through the BRO portal, with the program account suffix RC for corporate income tax, followed by applicable program accounts (e.g., GST/HST account starting with the BN plus a two-letter program identifier). For British Columbia, the corporation must also file an extraprovincial registration with BC Corporate Registry under the Business Corporations Act and obtain a BC incorporation/extraprovincial number; if the entity carries on business in BC and has a permanent establishment, it may need BC Corporate Income Tax registration with the BC Ministry of Finance. Required documents include the certificate of incorporation or equivalent foreign charter, registered office address, directors' information, and a completed RC348 or online BRO application; a GST/HST registration can be obtained simultaneously if taxable supplies are anticipated to exceed the registration threshold or if the entity voluntarily elects to register. A TIN for federal purposes is the Business Number (BN), and the GST/HST account number is a derivative code; Canada does not issue a separate VAT ID distinct from the BN.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a check-the-box regime comparable to the US. A corporation or foreign corporation is statutorily classified as a taxpayer subject to entity-level Part I tax, unless it is a qualifying 'Canadian-controlled private corporation' (CCPC) using the small business deduction, or a non-resident corporation that under the Income Tax Act is taxed on Canadian-source income through a permanent establishment. Hybrid mismatch rules under the Income Tax Act generally deny deductions for payments characterized differently in other jurisdictions, and an election can be made under section 216 (for certain rental or timber income), subsection 115.1 (for dispositions by non-residents), or by treaty tie-breaker resolution through the mutual agreement procedure; there is no general 'check-the-box' election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies over any four consecutive calendar quarters (GST/HST registration threshold under Excise Tax Act); for British Columbia PST, there is no registration threshold — every seller making taxable sales of goods or taxable services in BC must register. Additionally, a non-resident vendor that is a 'non-registered non-resident' may be required to register if it solicits orders in BC or delivers goods into BC. For the GST/HST, small supplier status exceptions may apply; once registered, GST at 5% federal rate applies in BC (no provincial HST component because BC uses a separate PST).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of qualifying medical-grade nitrile examination gloves are generally zero-rated under section 1 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act, provided the goods are exported by the supplier for supply outside Canada, or delivered outside Canada by common carrier or freight forwarder, and are not acquired for use in Canada. Suppliers must maintain evidence of export within a reasonable time; supplies of goods made by a registrant who exports them are zero-rated when the supplier satisfies the CRA that the goods were exported as part of the supply. Additionally, services performed on exported goods and certain transportation services may qualify for zero-rating. The supplier may claim input tax credits (ITCs) on the inputs attributable to zero-rated exports, resulting in a net refund of the GST/HST paid on purchases. No provincial PST zero-rating exists for exports from BC; the PST is generally not applied to goods delivered outside Canada by the vendor, but the vendor should retain documentation of delivery to a point outside Canada. Under Part IX of the Excise Tax Act, the exporter must not be a non-registrant making a false representation to obtain a rebate, and the exported goods must not be acquired for consumption or supply in Canada.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) Form B13A, or automated export declaration through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CAED)/CEX)
  • Proof of the commercial invoice and sale contract evidencing sale to a non-resident
  • Bill of Lading (B/L) or air waybill showing the foreign destination and delivery outside Canada
  • Canadian Bankers Association (CBA) / bank inward remittance certificate or SWIFT/TT wire confirmation evidencing payment in foreign currency
  • Packing slip and HS classification certificate confirming the goods are 39.13 / 40.15 category eligible nitrile gloves (HS 4015.12) and sterile medical classification, if applicable
  • Customs power of attorney / broker authorization where export clearance is completed by a licensed customs broker

Canada currently does not mandate a real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control (CTC), or digital stamping regime for standard e-invoicing in the private sector. The CRA has implemented mandatory electronic reporting requirements for digital platform operators under Part IX of the Excise Tax Act, but there is no universal mandatory e-invoicing API clearance mandate. Tax invoices (GST/HST) must contain prescribed information under the Excise Tax Act (e.g., vendor name, BN/GST/HST registration number, date of invoice, consideration, tax rate, and tax amount). Since July 2020, federal GST/HST returns and certain filings must be filed electronically, but that is not an e-invoice clearance obligation. Businesses may use EDI or XML invoices, and the CRA accepts electronic records; however, no pre-clearance digital stamp is required. For BC PST, vendors are required to issue a sales invoice containing prescribed PST information, maintain records, and report through the BC eTaxBC portal; there is no fiscal stamping.

The CRA may reassess a zero-rated supply to taxable (reversing the zero-rating and requiring GST/HST at 5% or the provincial component) if the exporter fails to substantiate the export with adequate documentation or exports are not supported by the goods leaving Canada in keeping with section 143 and Schedule VI, Part V of the Excise Tax Act. Penalties under the Excise Tax Act may include a 'gross negligence penalty' of 50% of the tax payable (recoverable amount) or, where the exporter knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence files a false statement, penalties under subsection 285(2) (i.e., the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the additional tax payable), plus interest and arrears on any net tax due. Fraudulent claims can result in criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act (tax evasion penalty of 50% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to two years) and the Crown Liability and Proceedings Act. Additionally, under subsection 297(1), a person who issues a false invoice or supplies false information in relation to a rebate may be liable to a penalty of CAD 2,000 or, if the false claim is made knowingly, a fine up to 250% of the amount of the rebate claimed. CRA export verification programs routinely scrutinize high-risk refund claims, and the exporter must retain books and records for six years; the limitation period for reassessment for non-residents is generally three years from the date of the notice of assessment, extended to four years where estate or trust issues arise, and indefinitely in the case of misrepresentation or fraud. Clawbacks may include the loss of the export zero-rating retroactively plus interest, and penalties of up to 25% of the amount of tax for due diligence failures; repeated serious failures may trigger a customs compliance verification by the CBSA, and liability for the export transaction may be assumed by the exporter, customs broker, or consignor.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full currency convertibility for both current-account and capital-account transactions under IMF Article VIII. There are no exchange controls, no central-bank prior-approval requirements, and no statutory profit-remittance hurdles for a qualifying business corporation. Outbound dividend distributions are subject to Part XIII withholding tax at the domestic rate of 25%, reducible under applicable income-tax treaties (e.g., 5%-15% under the Canada-US Convention and equivalent agreements); interest and royalty cross-border payments are similarly treaty-eligible. All cross-border fund movements are monitored only through (i) reporting obligations under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), including large-transaction reports to FINTRAC, and (ii) balance-of-payments statistical reporting by the receiving/remitting bank to the Bank of Canada and Statistics Canada. No exchange-control approval certificates are required for capital injection, trade settlement, or distribution.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no surrender obligation on export receipts. A British Columbia medical-PPE exporter may retain export proceeds in the invoiced currency, hold CAD, USD, or foreign-currency operating accounts domestically or with an offshore affiliate, and freely determine the timing and means of conversion to CAD. The only applicable duties are general corporate record-keeping requirements for tax, customs (CBSA), and equipment-leasing documentation under the Income Tax Act and the Customs Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); there is no dedicated foreign-exchange control administration in Canada. Exchange-rate and FX policy are administered by the Department of Finance Canada in coordination with the Bank of Canada, while prudential supervision of authorized banks is exercised by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Low feasibility as a primary corporate treasury bank. Canada does not licence fintech electronic-money institutions (EMIs) as bank-class institutions: deposit-taking and full corporate banking powers are reserved exclusively for Schedule I and Schedule II banks under the Bank Act (Canada), which are federally chartered and CDIC-insured. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Wise, Revolut, or comparable platforms) may lawfully operate only as money-services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA; they are permitted to offer cross-border remittance and FX conversion but cannot provide escrow accounts, regulated overdraft facilities, commercial letters of credit, or chequing services. Such platforms may be used as a secondary, low-value remittance rail for supplier test payments, but the holding and disbursement of material export proceeds and capital injections must be managed through a tier-1 domestic bank, with the EMI route subject to a documented risk assessment in the treasury policy.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant wire transfer protocol: the foreign parent or investor effects the equity injection via SWIFT MT103 (single payment) or MT202 (interbank cover) with the beneficiary as the BC corporation and a narrative stating 'Share capital contribution — subscription price for [number] of common shares of [issuer legal name]'. Canada requires no prior FX approval; the receiving bank classifies the payment as inward direct investment under the balance-of-payments statistical framework (equity capital component) and credits the operating account or a dedicated capital escrow account. The corporation must hold: (i) a directors' resolution authorizing the issuance and allotment; (ii) a countersigned share subscription agreement; (iii) an updated register of shareholders filed with the BC Registrar of Companies on the next annual report; and (iv) accounting recognition of the equity instrument under IFRS 2 / IAS 32. For exports, no special purpose code is mandated in the same manner as under an exchange-control regime; the bank requires only a supporting narrative reflecting the invoice or contract reference, though FINTRAC large-transaction reporting thresholds apply (record for transactions at or above CAD 10,000; report suspicious transactions and cross-border transfers of currency or monetary instruments at or above CAD 10,000). Verification at account opening includes provision of the executed subscription agreement and a notarized identification statement from the funding bank confirming the origin of the wire.Clearing rails for trade settlements: (1) SWIFT MT103 direct telegraphic transfers for supplier payments where counterparties accept T/T terms — appropriate for chemical feedstock, coagulation additives, packaging materials, and miscellaneous customs charges; (2) commercial irrevocable letters of credit issued via SWIFT MT700/MT707 for high-value machinery and major raw-material contracts, with settlement by presentation of compliant documents and interbank reimbursement via SWIFT MT103/MT202; (3) documentary collections (documents-against-payment D/P or documents-against-acceptance D/A) using SWIFT MT4xx/MT5xx as a cost-reduced alternative for repeat suppliers; (4) USD domestic clearing through correspondent bank networks (CHIPS and Fedwire) for USD-denominated payments within the United States; and (5) CAD domestic clearing through Payments Canada (LVTS and ACSS) for inland disbursements such as payroll, utilities, and Canadian suppliers. The treasury policy should route all major outflows through the BC operating concentration account, with the bank's trade-services desk confirming each LC, and all export receipts from medical-PPE customers repatriated by SWIFT MT103 into the same concentration account to preserve liquidity visibility and reduce FX conversion leakage.Deliverable USD/CAD forward contracts (plain vanilla, standard tenors of 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months) documented under an ISDA Master Agreement or bilateral FX confirmationFX swaps (spot-forward rollovers) to manage inter-month working-capital mismatches between USD export receipts and CAD operating disbursementsCross-currency swaps (CCIRS / long-date currency swaps) hedging long-term USD-denominated machinery capex and export financingVanilla and zero-cost collar FX options on USD/CAD to cap conversion cost while retaining upside on favourable ratesNon-deliverable forwards (NDFs) on permitted cross-pairs where physical CAD delivery is operationally impractical, noting that CAD is freely convertible and deliverable in all standard market tenors

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Low feasibility as a primary corporate treasury bank. Canada does not licence fintech electronic-money institutions (EMIs) as bank-class institutions: deposit-taking and full corporate banking powers are reserved exclusively for Schedule I and Schedule II banks under the Bank Act (Canada), which are federally chartered and CDIC-insured. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Wise, Revolut, or comparable platforms) may lawfully operate only as money-services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA; they are permitted to offer cross-border remittance and FX conversion but cannot provide escrow accounts, regulated overdraft facilities, commercial letters of credit, or chequing services. Such platforms may be used as a secondary, low-value remittance rail for supplier test payments, but the holding and disbursement of material export proceeds and capital injections must be managed through a tier-1 domestic bank, with the EMI route subject to a documented risk assessment in the treasury policy.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined general (15% federal + 12% British Columbia); 11% combined for eligible CCPC active business income up to the C$500,000 small business deduction limit (9% federal + 2% BC).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Form T106 (Information Return of Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents) must be filed when the aggregate value of transactions with any one non-resident non-arm's-length person exceeds C$1,000,000 in a taxation year. CRA Information Circular 87-2R requires contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation. The Local File is the Canadian transfer pricing documentation package; the OECD Chapter V Master File equivalent is not statutorily required for all taxpayers, but Country-by-Country reporting (Form RC4649) is mandatory for ultimate parent entities of MNE groups with consolidated group revenue of C$750,000,000 or more.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply Gulf-state style Economic Substance Regulations; however, economic substance is tested through the statutory General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245), the 'business purpose' and 'sham' doctrines at common law, and the transfer pricing 'reality' principle in section 247(2). For cross-border structures, the Canada–U.S. treaty's beneficial ownership and purpose tests, plus Canada's foreign affiliate dumping rules (section 212.3), require a resident corporation to demonstrate real employees, real operational activities, and actual risk assumption. A BC-resident entity with no office, no staff, and no purchasing or production functions in respect of nitrile glove dipping contracts will be disregarded or recharacterized, with the tax benefit reversed.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements (Income Tax Act s.239)

Statutory sanction: On indictment, imprisonment for up to five years plus a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded; on summary conviction, a fine and/or imprisonment of up to two years. The tax, interest, and gross-negligence penalties (50%) remain collectible, and CRA has statutory power to seize bank accounts and freeze assets.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorize or participate in the misstatement are personally liable under section 227.1 for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST, and as parties to the offense they may face criminal conviction, court-ordered restitution, travel restrictions, and removal from corporate office.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement / Deficiency (Income Tax Act s.247(3))

Statutory sanction: A 10% penalty on the net transfer pricing adjustment, plus full reassessment of income, compounded daily interest, and loss of loss-carryforward erosion if the adjustment re-characterizes transactions.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign the T106 with false statements are exposed to the criminal sanctions of section 239; civil restitution may be sought for dividends distributed while the corporation was insolvent after a TP adjustment.
Shell Company / GAAR and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: The tax benefit is denied entirely; the CRA reassesses and recaptures the avoided tax plus compound interest; a gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understated federal tax may be imposed under section 163(2), and in cross-border cases the CRA may apply a general anti-avoidance penalty equal to 25% of the denied benefit where the avoidance is supported by misrepresentation.

Director personal liability: Directors responsible for the artificial structure may be assessed as co-conspirators in a fraudulent conveyance if assets were transferred abroad to defeat CRA collection; personal liability and travel bans can be ordered by the court under collection proceedings.
AML / FINTRAC Compliance (PCMLTFA; Criminal Code s.462.31)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to C$500,000 for a first contravention and up to C$1,000,000 for subsequent contraventions; criminal prosecution for money laundering under the Criminal Code carries imprisonment up to 10 years and fines in the court's discretion; assets are subject to restraint and forfeiture orders.

Director personal liability: Officers and directors who knowingly direct or tolerate the laundering scheme face personal criminal liability, imprisonment up to 10 years, individual fines, and travel bans; professional licenses of directors and beneficial owners can be revoked.
