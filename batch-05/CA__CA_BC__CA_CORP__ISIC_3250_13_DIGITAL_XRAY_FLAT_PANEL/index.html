Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), Corporate Taxation Directorate and GST/HST Rulings Directorate; registration portal: Business Registration Online (BRO) at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/business.html. For British Columbia provincial PST: BC Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch (eTaxBC portal). For customs/export controls: Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) via Canadian Export Reporting System (GTR).

E-government filing portal

Non-resident Business Corporation: (1) File Form RC1 (Application for Registration) or register through BRO to obtain a 9-digit federal Business Number (BN); (2) simultaneously open GST/HST account (RT prefix) where annual worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 or the corporation carries on commercial activity in Canada; (3) obtain a CBSA import/export account (RM prefix) and customs client identifier for electronic export declarations; (4) if physical operations or employees exist in BC, register for BC PST (7%) via eTaxBC and for payroll deductions (RP account); (5) submit foreign incorporation certificate, Canadian address for service, and corporate biographical details; (6) GST/HST account activation occurs upon CRA written confirmation or deemed registration. A Canadian representative or agent may be required for non-resident corporations without a permanent establishment.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation - a Business Corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or British Columbia Business Corporations Act is a separate legal taxpayer subject to federal Part I tax (15% general rate for active business income) and BC provincial corporate tax (12% general rate, administered by CRA under the Tax Collection Agreement). No pass-through eligibility exists for corporate shareholders; the corporation files its own T2 corporate income tax return. Non-resident corporations are taxed on Canadian-source business income under Part I and may be subject to Part XIII withholding and Part XIV branch tax (25%, subject to treaty reduction, generally 5%). Territorial Scope: Canada taxes worldwide income of resident corporations; BC imposes income tax on income attributable to its permanent establishment. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation - a Business Corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or British Columbia Business Corporations Act is a separate legal taxpayer subject to federal Part I tax (15% general rate for active business income) and BC provincial corporate tax (12% general rate, administered by CRA under the Tax Collection Agreement). No pass-through eligibility exists for corporate shareholders; the corporation files its own T2 corporate income tax return. Non-resident corporations are taxed on Canadian-source business income under Part I and may be subject to Part XIII withholding and Part XIV branch tax (25%, subject to treaty reduction, generally 5%). Territorial Scope: Canada taxes worldwide income of resident corporations; BC imposes income tax on income attributable to its permanent establishment. Tax_Exempt_Zone: not applicable in Canada.

Available election forms

Canada has no federal check-the-box or elective entity classification regime; corporate form is fixed by the incorporating statute. Available elections include: (i) Subsection 85(1) Income Tax Act rollover for tax-deferred transfers of property to the corporation; (ii) Section 216 election permitting non-residents to file on a net basis for Canadian rental income; (iii) Subsection 219(1) treaty-based branch tax waiver elections; (iv) GST/HST Quick Method of Accounting election (ETA subsection 228(4)) and combined fiscal year elections; (v) Section 189 election for non-resident insurers; (vi) hybrid mismatch rules (BEPS Action 2): a Canadian corporation is never fiscally transparent for domestic law purposes, and no elections alter residence-based classification — hybrid outcomes are suppressed through specific deductibility/withholding denials rather than elective mechanisms.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies (for non-residents: carrying on commercial activity in Canada with taxable supplies exceeding CAD 30,000 over four consecutive calendar quarters). BC PST: no threshold relief — out-of-province vendors making taxable sales of goods in BC must register immediately before sale.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Section 1, Part V, Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act zero-rates supplies of goods exported by the GST/HST registrant supplier. Conditions for the digital X-ray flat panel detectors (HS 9022.14 / 9018.14): (i) the supplier must be a registrant; (ii) goods must be physically shipped to a destination outside Canada and exported as part of the supply; (iii) supplier must obtain documentary evidence of export; (iv) zero-rating also extends to freight, insurance, and handling services associated with the export sale (Part VII Schedule VI); (v) sales to non-resident purchasers for use solely outside Canada qualify if goods are not acquired for consumption in Canada. Services (e.g., post-sale installation or calibration performed offshore) are zero-rated under Sections 7 and 9 of Part V Schedule VI when performed for non-residents. BC PST (7%) does not apply to exported goods; zero-rating for GST purposes simultaneously exempts the transaction from PST where the vendor ships outside BC.

  • Commercial Invoice in English stating Incoterms, HS classification, unit value, and foreign destination

  • Packing List with container and serial number details

No real-time fiscal clearance, mandatory API stamping, or government clearance of individual invoices exists in Canada. Mandated electronic filing: (i) GST/HST returns via CRA GST/HST NETFILE where annual revenue exceeds CAD 1.5 million, with monthly filing required for large registrants (over CAD 6 million); (ii) export declarations (B13A) must be transmitted electronically through the Canadian Export Reporting System (GTR) for goods exceeding CAD 2,000 in value; (iii) BC PST returns electronically filed via eTaxBC. Voluntary PEPPOL-based e-invoicing is being adopted under the Pan-Canadian Business Connect framework. GST/HST records, including export documentation, must be retained for six years.

CRA's Export Review Program audits exporters' books to substantiate physical export, payment receipt, and 6-year records retention. Reassessment window: standard 4 years; extended to 7 years for misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default; no limitation period for fraud. Failure to substantiate zero-rating triggers reassessment of 5% GST on the gross export proceeds plus interest. Penalties: ETA section 280 late-filing/shortfall penalty of 5% plus 1% per month (maximum 12 months); section 285 penalty of 50% of net tax attributable to false statements; section 327.1 evasion fine up to CAD 25,000 and/or imprisonment up to 2 years. Directors are jointly and severally liable under section 323 for corporate GST liabilities absent a reasonable care defence. The 180-day FX repatriation benchmark represents an OECD-aligning Canadian transfer pricing expectation for intercompany export receivables; untimely outward remittance can trigger reinstatement of tax on undistributed income and interest imputation. Clawback of previously claimed input tax credits (ITCs) attributed to exported components is also available where export documentation is incomplete.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined (15% federal general corporate income tax rate plus 12% British Columbia general corporate income tax rate); active business income of a Canadian-controlled private corporation may qualify for the reduced federal rate of 9% and BC small business rate of 2% on the first C$500,000 of taxable income.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder. Treaty relief typically reduces the rate to 15% for portfolio shareholdings and 5% for a beneficial owner that holds at least 10% of the voting shares, as in the Canada–US tax treaty, subject to all treaty-linking conditions and anti-avoidance provisions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Final T2 return must be filed no later than six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Corporate income tax balance-due day is generally two months after the fiscal year-end, or three months after the fiscal year-end for a Canadian-controlled private corporation that qualified for the Small Business Deduction in the preceding year. BC corporate tax is reported on the same T2 and is subject to the same balance-due and installment deadlines.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate fair market value of all cross-border non-arm's-length transactions for the local-file style transfer pricing documentation under ITA s.247(4). Country-by-Country Report and Master File documentation are mandatory for MNE groups with annual consolidated group revenue of C$750,000,000 or more in the preceding fiscal year, as prescribed by Canada's CbCR regulations and OECD Action 13 documentation framework.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not administer offshore-style Economic Substance Regulations, but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245), transfer-pricing rules, beneficial-ownership jurisprudence, and treaty-shopping anti-abuse doctrines impose a substantive economic-substance test on every corporate arrangement. A BC manufacturer of amorphous silicon cesium iodide DR flat-panel detectors must demonstrate actual employees, production premises, risk-bearing, operational control, and commercial functions; a corporation lacking genuine radiology-hardware business activity and dependent entirely on a foreign parent's intellectual property and sales force will be re-characterized as a conduit, and profits will be re-allocated or re-classified as royalties, fees, or dividends subject to Part XIII.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Under ITA s.239(1), a person who evades tax is guilty of an offence; on conviction on indictment, the fine is not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, plus imprisonment up to five years. Civilly, CRA applies a gross-negligence penalty equal to 50% of the misstated tax amount, plus compound daily interest and reassessments.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false T2 returns or direct the evasion are personally subject to criminal prosecution and imprisonment; civilly, CRA may collect against directors who received or controlled property for the corporation, and collection measures include freezes on personal bank accounts, enforcement against assets, and travel bans when a criminal investigation is underway. In egregious cases, courts pierce the corporate veil and impose joint and several personal liability for the unpaid tax, interest, and penalties.
Shell Company / Conduit Abuse and Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: The General Anti-Avoidance Rule in s.245 ITA may deny the tax benefit, re-characterize the series of transactions, and reassess Part XIII withholding at the statutory 25% dividend rate on the entire disguised distribution. Transfer-pricing adjustments under s.247 add a 10% penalty on the net upward adjustment, and CRA may also impose a 50% misrepresentation penalty under s.163.2 where false documentation was provided.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly execute the conduit arrangement are exposed to s.227.1 joint liability for unremitted withholdings and source deductions; they may also be sued by other shareholders or creditors for breach of fiduciary duty. If the shell is used to repatriate funds or hide assets, a court may lift the corporate veil and order restitution from directors' personal assets; a travel ban may be sought by CRA during a criminal investigation.
Anti-Money Laundering and Beneficial Ownership Compliance

Statutory sanction: BC Business Corporations Act offences for failing to record or update significant-individual information attract fines of up to C$10,000 for the corporation and up to C$5,000 for an officer or director. Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, a person who fails to comply with reporting requirements can be fined up to C$500,000 and, in aggravated cases, prosecuted under s.462.31 of the Criminal Code with imprisonment of up to ten years.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve inaccurate ISC filings or who structure invoice transactions to conceal the beneficial owner of the enterprise can be personally liable for the penalties and, if the concealment is used to launder illicit funds, may be imprisoned. Civil restitution can also be ordered for damages suffered by third parties, with courts disregarding the corporate veil where the company is used as an instrument of fraud.
Permanent Establishment and PE Misstatement

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess the foreign parent on Canadian business profits attributable to the permanent establishment under the applicable treaty and allocate more income to the BC subsidiary, resulting in additional Part I tax, interest, late-filing penalties, and Part XIII withholding on any redirected payments. Fraudulent PE misstatements attract the 50% gross-negligence penalty and criminal prosecution under s.239.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false PE declarations or who direct the foreign parent not to file a Part I return for the Canadian establishment are personally at risk of prosecution and imprisonment; CRA can freeze travel of individuals involved in a PE investigation, and courts may impose a remedial constructive trust on personal property when the subsidiary is a puppet entity and the corporate form is a disguise for personal gain.
