Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) for federal GST/HST and corporate income tax (Business Number; online portal: canada.ca/cra-business); British Columbia Ministry of Finance for BC Provincial Sales Tax (PST) (eTaxBC; portal: gov.bc.ca/pst); Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for customs and export controls.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity must first obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 or using CRA Business Registration Online, selecting the applicable program accounts: RC (corporate income tax), RT (GST/HST), and RP (customs/import-export). For a British Columbia business corporation, provincial incorporation with BC Registrar of Companies generates legal existence, but the BN is the statutory tax identifier for CRA. If the non-resident corporation has a Canadian permanent establishment or carries on commercial activity in Canada, it must register for corporate income tax and GST/HST using Form RC382 (GST/HST registration for non-residents). The entity must also register separately for BC PST through the BC Online Business Registry if it makes taxable sales in British Columbia, providing banking and legal entity details. Customs importer/exporter status is established through the CBSA with the same BN, enabling declarations under the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. A Business Corporation incorporated in Canada is deemed resident and taxed at the corporate level on worldwide income under Part I of the Income Tax Act; it is not a pass-through entity. Corporate income tax rates are the federal rate plus BC provincial corporate rate, subject to the small business deduction if eligible. For GST/HST and PST purposes, the corporation is the legal registrant and must separately account for indirect taxes on taxable supplies and importations.

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box or general entity-classification election. Specific elections that may apply to a corporation in this structure include: (i) subsection 85(1) election for tax-deferred transfer of property into the corporation on incorporation; (ii) related corporation agreement/allocation of the small business deduction under section 256; (iii) section 216 election for non-resident persons earning Canadian rental income, generally not applicable to coffee bean wholesale trade; (iv) certain provincial PST vendor/voluntary registration elections. Canada's hybrid-mismatch anti-avoidance rules under sections 18.4 and 18.5 of the Income Tax Act apply automatically, and no elective mechanism is available to override disallowance of deductions in hybrid-arrangement cases.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies made in or connected to Canada during the preceding four calendar quarters triggers mandatory GST/HST registration for non-residents; BC PST requires vendor registration when annual taxable sales into British Columbia exceed CAD 10,000.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of green coffee beans is zero-rated under GST/HST as an export of tangible personal property if the supplier exports the goods from Canada or delivers them to a carrier for shipment to a destination outside Canada, and proof of export is retained. The BC PST similarly exempts tangible personal property sold for export outside British Columbia where possession is transferred in BC and the goods are shipped to a foreign destination. Freight, handling, and ancillary services performed in Canada that are directly linked to an exported supply may also qualify as zero-rated services. The taxpayer must maintain contemporaneous evidence sufficient to demonstrate that the goods were not further processed in Canada and left Canada within a reasonable time.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA CERS / Form B13A)
  • Bill of Lading (original or electronic, dated with port of loading and discharge)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank SWIFT MT103 or equivalent proof of proceeds)
  • Detailed Commercial Invoice identifying green coffee beans, quantities, HS classification
  • Packing List and container/unit-number documentation
  • Freight/Cargo Manifest or air waybill for air-freight export legs
  • Proof of foreign customs entry or foreign port arrival record

Canada does not currently operate a real-time fiscal invoice clearance or digital stamping mandate. However, commercial export shipments are subject to electronic filing under the CBSA Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) for shipments valued above CAD 2,000. GST/HST registrants with annual taxable supplies above CAD 1.5 million must file returns electronically via CRA GST/HST NETFILE/My Business Account; PST returns and registrations are submitted electronically through BC eTaxBC. Customs declarations for imports/exports use the CBSA eManifest and CERS framework; no API-based continuous tax clearance is in force.

CRA can audit export zero-rating claims and assess disallowed GST/HST refunds if export substantiation is inadequate, including retrospective reassessment with interest. Gross-negligence penalties under section 285 of the Excise Tax Act may be imposed at 25% of the overstated net tax or refund. Intentional misrepresentation or fraudulent export schemes are subject to criminal prosecution under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act, with fines of up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment. Claims will also be denied under the Customs Act for false export declarations, and CBSA may impose monetary penalties and seizure or forfeiture of goods. For PST, false export exemption claims can be challenged by the BC Ministry of Finance, resulting in assessments, 25% penalty on unreported PST, and interest.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility. Canada has accepted IMF Article VIII obligations: current-account payments and transfers are unrestricted, and the capital account is open with no currency-conversion licensing, no two-tier FX market, and no repatriation or surrender framework. The Canadian dollar is freely floating and major currencies may be held in domestic accounts. Profit-remittance hurdles are fiscal rather than exchange-control in nature: dividends paid by a Canadian corporation to a non-resident shareholder are subject to Part XIII withholding tax of 25%, reduced under applicable income tax treaties such as the Canada-Colombia, Canada-Peru, and Canada-Brazil treaties, typically to 5% for a 10% or greater corporate shareholder and 15% for portfolio shareholders; no repatriation deferral, capital-admin approval, or mandatory conversion is imposed. Branch remittance tax and thin-capitalization interest rules require treasury planning but do not block convertibility.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None under Canadian law. There is no statutory timeline for repatriation of export or commodity-trade proceeds and no mandatory conversion ratio into Canadian dollars. Export receipts may be retained indefinitely in foreign currency, held in Canadian or offshore accounts, netted within corporate treasury, or converted at commercial rates. The only compliance overlay is FINTRAC regulation: electronic funds transfers and cross-border currency movements of CAD 10,000 or more are subject to large-value transaction reporting, and suspicious transaction reporting applies to trade-based money-laundering indicators; sanctions screening under the United Nations Act and the Criminal Code applies to every cross-border payment.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), headquartered at 234 Wellington Street, Ottawa, Ontario K1A 0G9. Canada has no separate Foreign Exchange Administration; monetary and exchange-rate policy is conducted by the Bank of Canada under the Bank of Canada Act, with the Minister of Finance retaining final policy authority. The Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) administers cross-border monitoring under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) regulates deposit-taking institutions. No exchange-control license or central-bank consent is required for inbound or outbound payments in Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium-to-low as a primary relationship bank; medium as a payment adjunct. Canada is outside the EU/EEA EMI passporting regime, so an offshore digital EMI holding only a European e-money licence cannot lawfully take deposits or operate as a bank in British Columbia. A foreign fintech may (i) sponsor its services through a Canadian Schedule I bank, or (ii) register as a money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC and obtain a licence under British Columbia's Money Services Act. Legal constraints include the absence of Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC) coverage for fintech float, tighter domestic KYC obligations, and the inability of an EMI to issue documentary letters of credit. Feasible deployment: USD-denominated supplier payments, multi-currency sweep and netting, and treasury visibility rails, while capital escrow, LC issuance, and primary cash management remain with a Schedule I bank.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined federal-provincial general rate (15% federal + 12% British Columbia general corporate rate); 11% combined rate for Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) on active business income up to CAD 500,000 (9% federal + 2% BC small business rate).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Filing due date is the later of six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end or the fiscal year-end date; for a December 31 year-end, the filing deadline is June 30. Any final balance of tax payable must be remitted two months after year-end for non-CCPCs (or three months for CCPCs with refundable tax credits), subject to quarterly instalment obligations.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1 million aggregate annual value of non-arm's length transactions with a non-resident related party triggers Form T106 reporting and contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation; Country-by-Country notification applies to multinational groups with consolidated revenue exceeding CAD 750 million.

Economic substance scope: No standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) framework exists in Canada; however, transactions lacking economic substance may be disregarded or recharacterized under the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245 of the Income Tax Act), the transfer pricing rules (section 247), and the substance-over-form doctrine applied by Canadian courts.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion

Statutory sanction: Prosecution under subsection 239(1) of the Income Tax Act; upon conviction, fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to 5 years for indictable offenses (2 years for summary conviction); gross negligence penalties under subsection 163(2) of up to 50% of the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize or acquiesce in the evasion can be held personally responsible for unremitted corporate taxes and may face criminal prosecution; the corporate veil is pierced where the director is a participant in the willful default.
Failure to remit withholding tax

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unremitted tax amount, compound daily interest, and penalties under subsection 227(9) and section 227.2; repeated failures may lead to criminal prosecution under section 227.4.

Director personal liability: Under subsection 227.1(1), directors are jointly and severally liable, without limitation, for the full amount of withholdings the corporation failed to remit, provided the corporation has not been fully collected; a due diligence defence may reduce liability.
Transfer pricing violation

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing penalty under subsection 247(7) of 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the greater of CAD 5 million or 10% of gross revenue; the CRA may also reassess taxable income and charge arrears interest.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for penalties if they knowingly participated in the mispricing or signed false documentation; bankruptcy does not shield directors in cases of fraudulent misrepresentation.
Shell company / treaty shopping

Statutory sanction: Reassessment under the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245), denial of treaty benefits, and possible civil penalties for false statements; interest accrues on any reassessed tax.

Director personal liability: Directors who cause the corporation to function as a shell may face gross negligence penalties personally and, where the structure is used to commit fraud, the court may disregard corporate personality and impose personal liability for taxes and damages.
Anti-money laundering (AML)

Statutory sanction: Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) may impose administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1 million per violation; deliberate non-compliance may lead to criminal prosecution with fines up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Officers and directors are exposed to personal administrative penalties where they have authority to direct the money laundering compliance program and knowingly fail to take corrective action; personal criminal liability may attach for intentional participation.
