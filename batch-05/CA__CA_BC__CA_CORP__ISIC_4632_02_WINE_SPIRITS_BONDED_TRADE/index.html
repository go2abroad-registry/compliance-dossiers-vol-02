Tax ID Application Infrastructure

      

        
Competent tax authority
Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – Business Registration Online and My Business Account portals for federal GST/HST, excise duty and corporate income tax accounts; British Columbia Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch (eTaxBC) for Provincial Sales Tax (PST); Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for customs and export declaration administration; BC Liquor Control and Licensing Branch (LCLB) and BC Liquor Distribution Branch (LDB) for liquor licensing and distribution channels.

        
E-government filing portal
A foreign-owned corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the British Columbia Business Corporations Act is automatically issued a CRA Business Number (BN) upon incorporation. To obtain program accounts, the entity must register for GST/HST using Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number' or Business Registration Online. Non-resident corporations may register using the simplified non-resident GST/HST procedure (Form RC141) and must appoint a Canadian representative through Form RC59. Excise warehouse licensing requires a separate CRA account under the Excise Act, 2001 and a bonded warehouse licence from CBSA under the Customs Act for duty-suspension of imported spirits and wine. BC PST registration is mandatory through eTaxBC where the corporation has a permanent establishment, place of business, or physical presence in British Columbia; there is no small-supplier threshold for PST, and a BC liquor wholesale/special-warehousing licence must be obtained from the LDB/LCLB before any commercial sales in the province.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

      

        
Default tax classification
Entity_Level_Taxation: a Canadian Business Corporation is a separate legal and taxable person resident in Canada for income tax purposes; it is taxed at both the federal and provincial levels on worldwide income, with the BC provincial corporate income tax administered by CRA under the federal-provincial tax collection agreement. The corporation is the registrant for GST/HST; its shareholders are not individually liable for corporate tax or GST/HST obligations. Canadian excise duty on spirits and wine is levied at the point of manufacture or importation and is suspended inside an excise warehouse, so the licensed bonded warehouse operator is the excise taxpayer.

        
Available election forms
Canada does not operate a US-style check-the-box regime; classification as a corporation is conclusive for Canadian tax purposes. Available statutory elections relevant to a cross-border bonded trade corporate structure include: subparagraph 216(1) elections on Canadian-source rental or royalty income; section 217 election on certain pension trust payments; section 85 rollover election for transfer of property to the corporation on a tax-deferred basis; GST/HST elections under Excise Tax Act section 25(1) to treat intra-group corporate supplies as nil-valued, section 156 election for closely-related corporations to zero-rate inter-company supplies; and the hybrid mismatch arrangement rules addressing deduction/non-inclusion outcomes under section 18.4 of the Income Tax Act for Canadian members of an MNE group; a Canadian corporation with US check-the-box hybrid branches must document its status separately for CRA purposes.

      

    

    

      
VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

      

        
Standard VAT / GST rate
5%

        
Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)
CAD 30,000: the GST/HST small-supplier threshold is based on total annual worldwide gross revenues of taxable supplies (including associated persons); registration is mandatory once the threshold is exceeded. For BC PST there is no monetary threshold – registration is required if the corporation has an establishment in BC or carries on business in BC. For excise duty and bonded warehousing there is no exemption threshold: the warehouse licensee and every removal of spirits/wine for home consumption attracts excise duty from the first unit.

      

      
      

        Export 0% zero-rating conditions
        
Supplies of tangible personal property (including scotch whisky, aged Bordeaux, and other spirits/wine) are zero-rated under GST/HST Schedule VI, Part V, item 1 where the property is exported from Canada as part of the supply. The registrant must have the goods exported within 60 days after the supply and retain evidence of exportation in the manner required by the GST/HST Regulations – Proof of Export. Exports from a licensed excise warehouse or CBSA bonded warehouse qualify for zero-rating of GST/HST and for duty relief under section 26/34 of the Excise Act, 2001 and Customs Act; duty-suspension is lifted only upon proof of lawful removal to a foreign destination. Offshore services supplied to non-residents are also zero-rated if performed wholly outside Canada, but are not the primary scope of this bonded goods trade.

Canada does not impose real-time fiscal clearance, government API stamping, or pre-approval of invoices; compliance is achieved through self-assessment and audit. Mandatory electronic GST/HST filing (NETFILE) applies to registrants with annual gross revenue exceeding CAD 1.5 million, and all GST/HST and excise duty remittances must be made electronically via CRA My Business Account or participating financial institutions. BC PST filings are submitted through eTaxBC. The absence of real-time clearance does not reduce compliance: the CRA requires full audit trail documentation and applies electronic audit analytics to cross-border spirits and wine flows.

      
CRA may assess GST/HST, excise duty, and income tax for the normal GST/HST reassessment period of up to four years and without time limit in cases of misrepresentation, fraud, or neglect. False export documentation attracts civil penalties under Excise Tax Act section 280 (up to 25% of the tax-required amount and 100% for gross negligence), and a section 327 conviction for tax evasion carries fines up to 200% of the evaded net tax plus imprisonment. Under the Excise Act, 2001, evasion penalties may reach 200% of the excise duty, with fines up to CAD 100,000 and up to three years' imprisonment on indictment; CBSA penalties under section 159 of the Customs Act are CAD 25,000 per false statement or up to CAD 75,000 in certain cases. Additionally, CRA and CBSA will retroactively strip zero-rating and excise duty relief, reassess all duties, GST/HST, interest, and penalties, disqualify the exporter from future duty-suspension, and share findings with the RCMP for criminal prosecution. Exporters relying on paper-only transactions, unrepatriated proceeds, related-party invoicing, or artificial re-routing face high audit scrutiny and clawback risk.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

      

        
Current-account convertibility
Canada maintains unencumbered IMF Article VIII status with full capital-account and current-account convertibility. There is no foreign exchange control law, no central bank pre-approval requirement for cross-border payments, no deposit of proceeds obligations, and no restriction on repatriation of dividends, interest, royalties, service fees, or intercompany loan repayments. The only structural friction on profit remittance is the non-resident withholding tax regime under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (Canada), generally 25% absent treaty relief, which is a tax administration obligation rather than a convertibility or exchange-control barrier. Estates of bonded wine and spirits held under customs and excise bond are not regarded as repatriated or current-account receipts until customs release and taxable sale occur; duty-deferred inventory movements are governed by CBSA and Excise Act, 2001 authorizations, not by currency regulations.

        
Export proceeds surrender rules
None exist in Canadian law. The Bank of Canada, the Department of Finance, and the Canada Border Services Agency impose no statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no requirement to surrender or repatriate export proceeds, whether regarded as bonded re-exports or wholesale domestic disposal. Export receipts may lawfully be denominated in USD, EUR, GBP, or any freely convertible currency and retained offshore or onshore at the exporter's commercial discretion, subject to three compliance boundaries: (i) FINTRAC cross-border and electronic funds transfer reporting for transactions at or above CAD 10,000; (ii) transfer-pricing and section 247 documentation for intercompany payable/receivable balances; and (iii) functional-currency and section 261 Income Tax Act elections applicable to a Canadian-resident corporation maintaining books in a foreign functional currency. For bonded-warehouse inventory, the export proceeds recognition is deferred until physical removal from bond.

      

      
Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) in coordination with the Department of Finance Canada; prudential regulation of deposit-taking institutions is administered by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); cross-border AML/CFT supervision, large cash transaction reporting, and electronic funds transfer monitoring are administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); payment-system clearing and settlement infrastructure (LVTS and Real-Time Rail) is operated under the Payments Canada framework.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

      

        Inbound equity capital injection is executed by SWIFT MT103 single customer credit transfer from the overseas holding company to the corporation's CAD-denominated operational account maintained at a Schedule I Canadian bank, with an accompanying MT199 or MT202 cover where interbank correspondent routing is required. The remittance purpose code should identify 'Equity capital / shares and other equity' and the beneficiary reference line must cite the share subscription agreement and the corporate file number; documentary verification comprises the directors' resolution authorizing the share issuance, the executed share subscription agreement, a completed share certificate ledger, and a statutory capital journal entry credited to contributed surplus or share capital. Settlement occurs over Payments Canada's Large Value Transfer System or the Real-Time Rail, with no Bank of Canada approval required; however, the receiving bank will apply FINTRAC electronic-funds-transfer record-keeping and report any single-recipient transaction of CAD 10,000 or more. For intercompany debt financing, the MT103 should use the OECD purpose category for intercompany borrowing, the funding must be supported by a written convertible or non-convertible loan agreement at arm's-length terms, and the borrower must address Part XIII withholding tax exposure, thin-capitalization rules under section 18(4) of the Income Tax Act (Canada), and Form T106 reporting where a controlled foreign affiliate or non-resident shareholder relationship exists.Primary outbound rails: (1) SWIFT MT103 with unique end-to-end transaction reference for direct supplier invoice settlement, principally GBP payments to Scottish distilleries and EUR payments to French negociants, executed by the Canadian bank's own dealing desk to optimize conversion cost; (2) Commercial Letters of Credit opened through the institutional trade-finance desks of RBC or Scotiabank for large high-value barrel and case-lot purchases, using documents-against-payment terms and full Incoterms compliance; (3) USD clearing via U.S. correspondent banks, with dual-currency settlement capability enabling CAD-to-USD conversion in-bank; (4) bonded-transfer disbursements within Canada under CBSA duty-deferral use a statistical transfer document and require an excise customs security in the form of a surety bond or cash deposit, with excise duty settled at the time of removal of goods from bond rather than at invoice date; (5) same-day domestic settlement of excise and GST payments via Payments Canada Real-Time Rail. The treasury blueprint directs that GBP-exposed payables be settled in GBP, EUR-exposed payables in EUR, and Canadian excise obligations in CAD, while USD receivables are swept into an integrated USD account within the same Canadian bank to minimize foreign exchange transaction costs and to enable natural balance-sheet hedging.Deliverable FX forward contracts on CAD/USD, CAD/EUR, and CAD/GBP pairs executed with the dealing desks of Schedule I banks under an ISDA Master Agreement or the bank's standard master forward agreement, with tenors aligned to the bonded-inventory maturation and release cyclePlain-vanilla FX options (European calls and puts) on CAD cross-rates for downside protection on unbudgeted inventory liquidation or for competitive tender exposureCross-currency interest rate and principal swaps (CCS) for multi-year supplier financing denominated in GBP or EUR against CAD funding legsFX-swap arrangements (swap-pont) for short-term rolling of settlement currency positions between USD, GBP, EUR, and CAD without disturbing the underlying trade-position universeBasis swaps between USD and CAD funding legs in the Canadian dollar market to optimize cross-currency funding costs where the bank's balance-sheet position creates a C$ basis opportunityNon-deliverable forwards (NDFs) are legally permissible for Canadian-resident corporates only where the underlying currency is not freely deliverable onshore, such as KRW, INR, or BRL; NDFs are neither required nor recommended for CAD/USD/EUR/GBP pairs, which are fully convertible and deliverable through the Canadian banking system
      

      
E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: LOW as primary treasury clearing rail; MEDIUM as ancillary vendor-payment corridor. Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and its regulations, any non-bank electronic money institution or payment service provider performing funds transfer, cross-border remittance, or virtual currency dealing must register as a Money Service Business with FINTRAC; additional provincial licensing may apply where lending or certain Quebec deposit-taking activities are involved. Offshore digital EMIs that lack a Canadian MSB registration, do not maintain a Canadian presence, and have no Payments Canada direct membership cannot clear through the Large Value Transfer System or Real-Time Rail, and therefore cannot lawfully function as the settlement institution for bonded-duty payments, CBSA customs security postings, or capital injections. Fintech platforms such as Wise Platform, OFX GlobalPay, and Visa B2B Connect may be engaged only as auxiliary execution channels for low-value supplier disbursements, with the master clearing relationship vested in a Schedule I bank.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

      
Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 (sum of cross-border non-arm's-length transactions involving property, services, or intangibles) triggers mandatory contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation (local file / master file); documentation must be available within a reasonable time and generally maintained in English. Multinational groups with consolidated group revenue of at least CAD 900 million must file Form RC5211 Country-by-Country Information Return, and any entity resident in Canada must file Form RC5186 appointed representative notification. BC does not impose a separate greater threshold.

      
Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply UK-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR); instead, the general anti-avoidance rule under Income Tax Act section 245, the civil sham doctrine, and the OECD principal purpose test under Canadian tax treaties are applied. CRA may reassess or deny treaty benefits for corporate structures lacking substantive business activity, commercial substance, and valid business purpose. For bonded wholesale and warehouse trade, legal ownership of inventory, operational staff in BC, risk exposure, and storage control are the core substantive indicators. Foreign investment entities with mailbox addresses, nominee offices, and no real business functions face GAAR reassessments and potential treaty-shopping denial.

      

    

    

      
Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

      
      

        Tax Evasion and False Statements
        
Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act section 239: on summary conviction, fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 2 years; on indictment, fine up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years. Civil penalties for gross negligence under section 163(2): 50% of the understated portion of tax, not subject to mitigation. CRA may also seize American-deposit assets, garnish receivables, and issue third-party demands.

        
Director personal liability: Directors who have knowingly instigated or acquiesced in filing false returns may be subject to criminal liability as 'party to the offence' under Income Tax Act section 242 and Criminal Code section 21/section 22. Additionally, directors face civil liability for unpaid amounts subject to a compliance order; restitution orders, travel bans under Criminal Code subsections 462.33(2) and 490.1, and imprisonment for contempt are available. CRA implements section 227.1 only for withheld source deductions, but for tax evasion a personal fraud proceeding may pierce the corporate veil where funds are used personally.

      

      
      

        Shell Company / Sham / Abuse of Separate Legal Personality
        
Statutory sanction: GAAR reassessment disallowing the tax benefit; penalties under section 247(3) of 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment (minimum CAD 100) if the transfer pricing documents and reasonable effort standards are deficient, or 50% under section 163(2) for gross negligence. Treaty benefit denial may result in full Part XIII withholding on repatriated amounts. CRA can vacate the VAT/GST registrations, and Excise Act licences may be suspended for non-compliance.

        
Director personal liability: Directors of a sham/mannequin corporation may be personally assessed for unpaid taxes under section 160 (transfer between shareholders/insolvency) and under the corporate-veil-piercing doctrine where the corporation is an agent or the apparent corporate form was maintained for a fraudulent or improper purpose. Personal injunctions, asset freezing, and contempt orders for failing to comply with unregistered tax demands are available in superior court.

      

      
      

        AML / Beneficial Ownership Disclosure Violations
        
Statutory sanction: Failure to file required information: persons convicted under Income Tax Act section 238 (offences) liable on summary conviction to a fine of up to CAD 25,000 and imprisonment up to 12 months, or on indictment to CAD 100,000 fine and up to 5 years; additionally, the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) imposes fines up to CAD 500,000 for non-compliance with recordkeeping and verification; non-registration under the Beneficial Ownership Transparency Act (BC) results in a fine up to CAD 250,000 per breach.

        
Director personal liability: Directors causing the corporation to fail beneficial ownership registration are personally liable for the corporate offence if they authorized, acquiesced, or participated in the failure; under BC's Beneficial Ownership Transparency Act, every director is jointly and severally liable for a breach resulting from their own conduct. Purposefully circumventing AML controls may lead to criminal liability for money laundering under Criminal Code section 462.31, carrying imprisonment up to 10 years and property forfeiture orders.

      

      
      

        Bonded Warehouse and Excise Duty Violations
        
Statutory sanction: Excise Act, 2001 section 237 et seq.: penalties up to CAD 25,000 for each violation plus forfeiture of goods exceeding CAD 1,000,000; unpaid excise duties may be assessed fully with daily interest. Customs Act penalties: direct contravention of a duty-related provision, CAD 1,000 to CAD 50,000 per non-repeat offence; deliberate evasion of duties is an indictable offence with imprisonment up to 5 years and fines equal to twice the evaded amount. The CRA may cancel the excise licence and close the bonded warehouse.

        
Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable permitting or assenting to duty evasion under section 228 of the Excise Act, 2001 (every director, officer, or agent who directed, authorized, assented to, or acquiesced in the act is a party to the offence). Personal penalties: fines equal to the difference between duties actually paid and duties assessed plus 200% of the unpaid duties; a director may be subject to a travel ban where proceedings are initiated under mutual legal assistance. Civil liability extends to restitution orders and seizure of personal assets under Federal Court procedures.

      

      
      

        Withholding Tax Repatriation Failures
        
Statutory sanction: Under subsection 227(8) of the Income Tax Act, a payer failing to withhold and remit Part XIII tax is liable for the full amount, plus interest and penalties: 10% of the amount due as a penalty for failure to remit (section 227(10)), interest at prescribed rates compounded daily. Repeated failure is an offence under section 229 with fines of up to CAD 50,000 and imprisonment up to 2 years. CRA can assess the tax against the non-resident directly, but generally recovers from the Canadian payer.

        
Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII withholding taxes under Income Tax Act section 227.1: a director who knew or reasonably should have known the withholding amounts were not paid and who failed to exercise diligence in preventing the non-payment is liable for the whole of the unremitted tax, plus interest and penalties. Directors' liability arises irrespective of the corporation's dissolution or bankruptcy; no limitation period in fraud scenarios (CRA may pursue in 10-year collection window, extendable for misrepresentation); civil collection may include a personal Canadian judgement enforceable through asset seizures, garnishments, or a director-specific travel restriction on an outstanding warrant.
