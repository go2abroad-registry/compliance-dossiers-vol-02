Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) - federal administrator of corporate income tax, GST/HST, and excise duties under the Excise Act, 2001; CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) and My Business Account (MyBA) are the principal registration and filing portals. Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) administers export/import declarations through the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) platform and the Canadian International Trade Single Window. The British Columbia Ministry of Finance, BC Revenue Division (eTaxBC), administers BC Provincial Sales Tax (PST), Employer Health Tax (EHT), and municipal/regional district tax levies.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate as a business corporation under the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA) via Corporations Canada or under the BC Business Corporations Act (BCBCA) via BC Registries; comply with resident-director requirements (CBCA requires at least 25% of directors to be resident Canadians, with a minimum of one resident Canadian director where the board has fewer than four directors; BCBCA generally requires at least one director who is a Canadian citizen or permanent resident ordinarily resident in Canada unless an exemption applies). Step 2: Register with CRA for a nine-digit federal Business Number (BN) using Business Registration Online or Form RC1; the BN functions as the corporate income tax account number and as the base for program accounts, including the GST/HST account (BN plus RT suffix and four digits), the payroll account, and the importer/exporter account (BN plus RM suffix). Step 3: Register with the BC Revenue Division through eTaxBC for PST before making any taxable sale or lease in British Columbia; no monetary registration threshold applies for PST. Step 4: Register for BC EHT if the employer's BC remuneration exceeds the applicable statutory exemption threshold, currently the first CAD 500,000 of BC remuneration for employers above the threshold. Step 5: Obtain the federal excise brewery licence and the provincial liquor licence from the BC Liquor and Cannabis Regulation Branch (LCRB) for manufacturing and wholesale distribution of craft beer. Step 6: Activate the CBSA importer/exporter account via CARM to file B13A export declarations and, where applicable, excise duty drawback or exportation relief claims.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation - a CBCA or BCBCA business corporation is a resident of Canada when it is incorporated in Canada or when its central management and control is situated in Canada; it is a separate taxpayer under section 2 of the Income Tax Act (ITA), subject to federal Part I corporate income tax and BC provincial corporate income tax on income attributable to a BC permanent establishment. No flow-through, partnership, or check-the-box classification is available for a Canadian corporation. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada is taxable under ITA section 2(3) on Canadian-source income and, on ceasing to carry on business, may be exposed to the 25% branch tax under ITA section 219, subject to treaty reduction. The entity is not a Tax_Exempt_Zone entity and is taxed on worldwide income, so Canada is not a pure Territorial_Scope jurisdiction for corporations resident in Canada.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a domestic check-the-box election; foreign entity classification follows CRA administrative policy (Income Tax Folio S4-F1-C1), which looks to de jure legal personality and limited liability, and hybrid mismatch arrangements are addressed legislatively under ITA sections 18.4 to 18.6, consistent with BEPS Action 2 and the Multilateral Instrument. Available elections relevant to the licensed craft beer distributor include: (i) ITA section 85 rollover election to transfer eligible property into the corporation on incorporation; (ii) GST/HST section 156 election to treat closely related corporations as a single person for VAT simplification; (iii) GST/HST section 167 election to apply no tax on a transfer of a business as a going concern; (iv) ETA section 240(3) election for a small supplier to register voluntarily so as to claim input tax credits; (v) election to use the GST/HST Quick Method where the distributorship qualifies; (vi) BC PST purchase-for-resale exemption, evidenced by a valid PST resale certificate issued by the BC Revenue Division, so that no PST is charged on wholesale keg and packaged beer supplies; and (vii) excise duty exportation or drawback elections under the Excise Act, 2001 for beer removed for export.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 - a person carrying on business in Canada, including a non-resident corporation, must register for GST/HST once the value of its worldwide taxable supplies exceeds CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (ETA section 240; small supplier rules); a non-resident making taxable supplies in Canada through a substantial physical or digital presence in Canada is also subject to GST/HST. BC PST registration has no monetary threshold, while BC Employer Health Tax applies only where BC remuneration exceeds the statutory exemption threshold currently set at CAD 500,000.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, item 1, a supply of tangible personal property is zero-rated where the property is exported from Canada as part of the supply, is delivered to a destination outside Canada, and the supplier retains proof of export; kegs of artisanal hazy IPA sold by the licensed distributor to US or other foreign consignees therefore bear GST at 0%. Services supplied to non-residents that are not performed for consumption in Canada and are unrelated to Canadian real estate or tangible property are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, items 6 and 7. The supplier must obtain contemporaneous documentary proof of export and keep it in its books and records for the statutory six-year retention period; CRA will recharacterize the supply as taxable if credible proof is not available at audit. BC PST does not apply to goods delivered to a destination outside British Columbia, and a valid resale certificate removes PST on wholesale sales to licensed BC customers. Beer exported by a licensed brewery is relieved from federal excise duty under the Excise Act, 2001, and if excise duty was already paid at removal, a drawback claim may be filed upon proof of exportation. Canada does not impose a general statutory 180-day deadline for repatriation of export proceeds; the 180-day benchmark is retained in this dossier as the prudent evidentiary ceiling for bank receipts, SWIFT payments, and foreign-exchange documentation supporting zero-rating claims and transfer-pricing consistency.

Canada imposes no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control (CTC), digital stamping, or government pre-clearance for GST/HST invoices. Electronic invoicing is legally valid, but the ETA requires invoices to contain prescribed information and supporting input tax credit documentation. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically (GST/HST NETFILE, MyBA, or a CRA-certified service provider), with mandatory electronic filing for registrants filing under prescribed categories; business records, including invoices, export evidence, and excise drawback files, must be retained for at least six years. Exports are subject to mandatory electronic customs reporting with CBSA through CARM, including the B13A Export Declaration, e-manifest, or an authorized Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) / Single Window equivalent; BC PST exemption certificates and excise duty records must also be retained for audit.

CRA may reassess GST/HST within four years of a return, extended to seven years in the case of neglect, misrepresentation, or attributable to a not-at-arm's-length person, and with no limitation period where fraud or wilful default is established. False zero-rating claims, circular export invoicing, fabricated bills of lading, or unrepatriated proceeds that reveal unreported income may trigger: (i) GST/HST false-statement penalties under ETA section 285(1) equal to the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the net tax sought to be evaded; (ii) GST/HST failure-to-file and failure-to-register penalties, including amounts under ETA section 280(1); (iii) gross-negligence penalties of 50% of understated federal income tax under ITA section 163(2), with analogous BC provincial penalty provisions; (iv) daily compound interest at prescribed rates on all reassessed net tax and income tax, with recovery of previously allowed input tax credits and refunds; (v) denial or vacation of the zero-rating treatment and export drawback where proof of export is not substantiated, causing the supply to be recharacterized as taxable in Canada; (vi) director and officer liability for unremitted GST/HST and excise duty under the ETA and Excise Act, 2001; (vii) criminal prosecution for evasion under ETA section 327 and ITA section 239, punishable on summary conviction by fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to two years less a day, and on indictment by fines ranging from 100% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to five years; and (viii) CBSA administrative monetary penalties (AMPS) under the Customs Act of up to CAD 25,000 per false declaration, export-data penalty, or contravention, plus seizure or detention of goods. CRA may claw back refunds claimed beyond the limitation period and apply general anti-avoidance, sham, or substance doctrines to reverse zero-rating benefits obtained without real exportation and genuine foreign receipt of payment.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility on both current and capital accounts. Canada is an IMF Article VIII member; no restrictions exist on dividend, interest, royalty, or capital repatriation by foreign-owned Canadian subsidiaries. Inbound equity for the BC distribution entity requires only a post-establishment notification under section 12 of the Investment Canada Act (greenfield), not a prior review. Profit remittance to non-resident shareholders is subject to Part XIII withholding tax – generally 25%, reducible to 5% on qualifying treaty dividends (e.g., US-Canada Treaty Article X). No BC provincial exchange controls apply.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. There is no statutory timeline, mandatory conversion ratio, or repatriation obligation for export proceeds. The CAD is free-float; exporters may retain USD in on-shore or offshore accounts. Customs export declarations (B13A) serve statistical and trade-security purposes only. Upon conversion, foreign-exchange gains/losses are recognized for CIT under section 39 of the Income Tax Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); oversight of the Crown’s Exchange Fund Account and foreign-exchange reserves rests with the Minister of Finance under the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act. Canada maintains an independently floating exchange-rate regime and has no exchange-control law or central-bank approval requirement for cross-border payments. AML/FX dealer oversight is exercised by FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA and by OSFI for deposit-taking institutions.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined (15% federal Part I general rate + 12% British Columbia general corporate rate); non-resident-owned or public corporations are denied the CAD 500,000 small-business threshold

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax under Income Tax Act subsection 212(2) applies to any dividend or deemed dividend paid by a resident Canadian corporation to a non-resident shareholder.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Within 6 months after the fiscal year-end (ITA subsection 150(1)); balance-due date is 3 months after year-end for Canadian-controlled private corporations and 2 months for other corporations; large corporations with existing instalment obligations pay quarterly instalments under ITA section 157.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Filing deadline is 6 months after the corporation's fiscal year-end; the T106 must be attached when the aggregate fair market value of all reportable non-arm's length cross-border transactions exceeds CAD 1,000,000 in the taxation year.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 of aggregate fair market value of non-arm's length cross-border transactions in a fiscal year triggers the mandatory filing of Form T106 and the expectation of a contemporaneous Local File; a Master File and country-by-country report are required only for MNE groups with consolidated revenue of at least CAD 750 million, which does not ordinarily apply to a standalone microbrewery distributor.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not administer a Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations regime, but the Income Tax Act contains a general anti-avoidance rule (section 245) denying the tax benefit where a transaction is an avoidance transaction that misuses or abuses the Act and lacks real economic substance; the CRA also relies on beneficial-ownership analysis in the Canada-US treaty, the statutory anti-hybrid rules (subsections 18.2 and 18.5 of the Income Tax Regulations), and the global minimum tax laws under OECD Pillar Two for large groups. A BC craft-beer distribution entity must maintain physical inventory, transfer-pricing benchmarks, real distribution personnel and board minutes demonstrating commercial activity in Canada; otherwise, GAAR, gross-negligence penalty (section 163(2)) and, in egregious cases, criminal tax-evasion charges (section 239) apply.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Criminal Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act section 239: fine of 50% to 200% of the evaded federal income tax plus up to 5 years imprisonment on indictment; the Crown imposes civil penalties and full restitution under section 240; CRA may freeze bank accounts and take judgment collection.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who direct, participate in, or acquiesce in the evasion are personally exposed to penal prosecution as parties to the offence; a conviction imposes the same fines, asset forfeiture under the Criminal Code, travel restrictions pending trial, and personal restitution orders that are not dischargeable in bankruptcy.
GAAR and Shell Company Abuses

Statutory sanction: Section 245 of the Income Tax Act recharacterizes transactions and denies the tax benefit; gross-negligence penalty under section 163(2) of 50% of the tax evaded applies when the taxpayer fails to exercise reasonable care; the related Schedule 125 GIFI misstatement can itself trigger a 50% false-statement penalty.

Director personal liability: Under CBCA section 122(1), directors who approve a sham structure breach fiduciary duty and are civilly liable in oppression-remedy proceedings for resulting shareholder losses; the CRA can assert personal liability under section 227.1 for unpaid source deductions and, where fraud is established, courts will pierce the corporate veil and impose restitution against the natural persons.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Subsection 247(3) penalty: an amount equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment (no de minimis floor once the adjustment is confirmed); adjustments are recomputed on the arm's length principle under subsection 247(2); CRA may also apply the 50% gross-negligence penalty under section 163(2) for deliberately omitted intercompany transactions.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign the T2 return with knowledge of the mispricing are exposed to the same criminal sanctions as the corporation under section 239; in practice CRA pursues the corporate entity first, but the section 227.1 director liability includes unremitted withholding on the cross-border royalty payments, and false testimonials at audit create personal exposure to the Criminal Code fraud offence (section 380) with penal conclusions of up to 14 years.
Excise Duty Evasion on Beer Production and Distribution

Statutory sanction: Excise Act, 2001 sections 100 and 214: a fine equal to the duty evaded plus an administrative penalty of 200% of the duty; for a criminal breach, imprisonment up to 5 years and confiscation of brewing equipment and stock; duty is a deemed Crown debt and survives the dissolution of the corporation.

Director personal liability: Directors of the licensed brewery are jointly and severally liable for unpaid excise duties and daily interest under the Excise Act, 2001; the classic 'director's plea' of personal liability is applied in the same way as source deductions — a director can be assessed without limiting statutory notice if the director knew or ought to have known of the duty liability.
Trade Sanctions, AML and FINTRAC Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 2,000,000 for a corporation found guilty of a serious violation; Criminal Code section 462.31 imposes imprisonment up to 10 years for laundering proceeds; Customs Act penalties apply on misdescribed export shipments.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly omit the FINTRAC report or direct the cash-skimming operation are prosecuted as co-principals; criminal conviction triggers mandatory surrender of passports, the issuance of an international arrest warrant through Interpol, and personal civil restitution to the Crown; Canada prosecutes directors individually even where the corporate entity is insolvent.
