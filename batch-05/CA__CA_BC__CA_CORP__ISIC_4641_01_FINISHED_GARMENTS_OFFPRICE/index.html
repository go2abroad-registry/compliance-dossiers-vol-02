Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); official portal: canada.ca/revenue-agency; CRA My Business Account at mybusinessaccount-cra.gc.ca. Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) is the companion customs administration for export reporting and border controls.

E-government filing portal

A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada must obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN) through CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO) service or by delivering Form RC1. The BN carries program accounts: RT0001 for GST/HST, RM0001 for Import/Export, RC0001 for corporate income tax, and RN0001 for non-resident income tax. GST/HST registration is mandatory once the corporation's taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or across the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (ETA ss. 148, 240); voluntary registration is available and is the standard path for an exporter of zero-rated goods wishing to claim input tax credits. The BN and RT account must appear on all GST/HST invoices, CBSA export declarations, and CRA filings. If the corporation also makes taxable retail sales in British Columbia, it must register separately for BC PST with the BC Ministry of Finance, using the federal BN as the BC PST account number; export sales are PST-exempt.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation. A business corporation is a separate taxpayer (ITA s. 2(2)); resident companies pay federal and BC provincial income tax on worldwide income, while a non-resident corporation is taxable only on Canadian-source income attributable to a permanent establishment. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. A business corporation is a separate taxpayer (ITA s. 2(2)); resident companies pay federal and BC provincial income tax on worldwide income, while a non-resident corporation is taxable only on Canadian-source income attributable to a permanent establishment. Canada has no U.S.-style check-the-box pass-through election for corporations.

Available election forms

No check-the-box regime exists under Canadian federal law. Principal elections relevant to a foreign-owned exporter: (i) voluntary GST/HST registration, including an election to register as a small supplier (ETA s. 240); (ii) the ITA s. 115.2 election, with U.S. competent-authority concurrence, allowing a fiscally transparent U.S. entity to be treated as a corporation for Canadian treaty purposes; (iii) the withholding-tax treaty relief election under the Canada-U.S. and other income tax conventions; (iv) the ITA s. 216 election for net-basis taxation of Canadian real-property or rental income; and (v) mandatory application of hybrid mismatch rules under ITA ss. 18.4-18.6 and Part VI, which do not offer elective avoidance.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000. Under ETA s. 148(1), a person is a small supplier unless worldwide taxable supplies (including supplies made through a permanent establishment in Canada) exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or in the aggregate over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters. Once the threshold is passed, GST/HST registration is compulsory under ETA s. 240; below the threshold, registration may be elected, and a non-resident not carrying on business in Canada is not obliged to register.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A registrant zero-rates finished-garment export supplies under ETA Sch. VI Pt. V when the goods are shipped by the registrant, its agent, or a common carrier to a destination outside Canada, to a consignee outside Canada, or to a carrier for direct export. The goods must leave Canada within 60 days after the day on which consideration becomes due, or within the extended 2-year period under the Zero-Rated Supplies (Taxable Supplies of Goods) Regulations where the purchase price is fully paid prior to export. GST paid by the registrant on Canadian purchases of overstock inventory and on overhead (freight, storage, commissions) is recoverable as an input tax credit provided the export transaction is fully documented and conducted in the course of a commercial activity.

  • Canadian Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A filed electronically through the Canadian Export Reporting System 'CERS'; mandatory for commercial shipments valued above CAD 2,000)
  • Commercial invoice showing foreign buyer, HTS/Schedule B code, Incoterms 2020, currency, quantities, and declared export value
  • Bill of Lading, Air Waybill, or Cargo Manifest evidencing physical delivery to a destination outside Canada
  • Packing list and foreign purchase order / sales contract establishing transfer of risk of loss offshore
  • Certificate of Origin and supporting documentation where CUSMA, CPTPP, or other preferential tariff treatment is claimed
  • Bank remittance evidence (e.g., SWIFT MT103, treasury ledger, or correspondent-bank filing); Canada does not issue a statutory 'Inward Remittance Certificate', so settlement tracing must be through banking records
  • GST/HST input tax credit support: Canadian supplier invoices and customs entry documents evidencing GST/PST treatment on the exported inventory and consumed Canadian inputs

No mandatory real-time e-invoice clearance, API fiscal authentication, or digital stamping exists in Canada. The CRA supports, but does not impose, the Peppol/pan-Canadian e-invoicing standard. Registrants may issue GST/HST invoices on paper or electronically, provided the document contains the particulars prescribed by ETA s. 221 and the Input Tax Credit Information Regulations. All records and books of account must be retained in Canada for six years from the end of the period to which they relate (ETA s. 286) and must be produced to the CRA in a readable, machine-processable format on request.

CRA may reassess within the normal four-year limitation period (ETA s. 298(1)), extended to eight years in cases of misrepresentation, neglect, or fraud (ETA s. 298(3)). Failure to substantiate the offshore export causes reversal of the zero rating, reassessment of 5% GST on gross consideration, denial or clawback of previously claimed input tax credits, and accrual of interest and penalties. Specific penalties include: (i) the general ETA s. 280 penalty of up to CAD 25,000 for non-compliance; (ii) a mandatory 25% penalty on the net tax that would have been evaded for a false or misleading statement or omission (ETA s. 285.2); (iii) a 50% penalty for gross-negligence participation or planning of false documentation (ETA s. 285.3); and (iv) criminal prosecution for wilful evasion (ETA ss. 325-330 and the Criminal Code), carrying custodial exposure and monetary fines. Canada does not impose a statutory export-proceeds repatriation deadline; the 180-day benchmark in this dossier operates as the contractual/trading settlement standard, and materially delayed or unexplained remittance creates heightened audit-risk indicators at the CRA.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible with no exchange control restrictions on current or capital account transactions. Profit remittances via dividends are unrestricted provided the corporation satisfies the solvency test under the Canada Business Corporations Act (or the British Columbia Business Corporations Act) and the source of funds complies with corporate and tax record-keeping requirements. Non-resident dividend withholding tax of 25% (or reduced by tax treaty) applies. No central bank approval or conversion licence is required to repatriate profits, though financial institutions will apply standard anti-money laundering and know-your-client checks.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender or mandatory conversion requirement exists for export receipts. Canadian exporters may retain foreign currency in designated bank accounts without a time limit. All export and foreign exchange transactions must be supported by commercial invoices, customs entry documents, and banking records sufficient to satisfy Canada Revenue Agency audit requirements and FINTRAC's cross-border wire transfer reporting threshold of CAD 10,000.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); cross-border transaction reporting and anti-money laundering supervision administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium. Offshore digital electronic money institutions (EMIs) can process cross-border treasury flows as payment service providers, but they are not licensed deposit-taking institutions under the Bank Act or the British Columbia Financial Institutions Act. They must register as money services businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC if they provide foreign exchange or money transfer services to Canadian residents. Correspondent banks may impose restrictive due diligence on EMIs, especially for US-dollar clearing, making them less reliable than tier-1 Canadian banks for escrowed capital or high-value liquidation settlements.

Capital injections should be settled through a SWIFT MT103 wire transfer from the ultimate parent or shareholder to the Canadian corporation's Capital Escrow Account at a tier-1 Canadian bank. For any single inbound cross-border transfer equal to or above CAD 10,000, the Canadian bank will file a Large Cash Transaction Report or Cross-Border Wire Transfer Report with FINTRAC as applicable. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined Canadian federal and British Columbia general corporate income tax rate (Federal effective 15%; BC general 12%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII Canadian withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder, subject to income tax treaty reduction.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate amount of specified non-arm's-length cross-border transactions in a taxation year triggers mandatory contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation under Income Tax Act section 247 and CRA Form T106 filing. Canada does not impose a separate Local/Master File requirement but CRA examines documentation (CUP, Resale Price, Cost Plus, TNMM, Profit Split) in audits.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate an Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime like certain offshore centres. Corporate residence is based on central management and control; CRA adopts substance-over-form and the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) to recharacterize artificial transactions. In BC, minimum substance is not statutorily required, but a shell corporation engaged mainly in holding passive income may be disregarded or reassessed when facts indicate no real commercial purpose.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under ITA section 239(1) for tax evasion: fine up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years. Civil reassessment plus gross-negligence penalty equal to 50% of understated tax and interest under section 163.

Director personal liability: Directors who instruct, sign, or knowingly benefit from false returns may be convicted as parties to the offence; under ITA section 227.1, directors are jointly and severally liable for unpaid employer-like withholdings. For tax evasion itself, corporate veil does not shield personal wrongdoing where the director exercised control or committed fraud.
Withholding Tax Non-Compliance (Dividends, Royalties, Interest)

Statutory sanction: Under ITA section 215, withholding agent is liable for the unwithheld amount; section 227 adds fines up to 200% of unremitted amounts and daily interest. The corporation and person responsible are jointly liable.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable to CRA for unremitted Part XIII tax under ITA section 227.1, including interest and penalties, if they knew of the failure and did not exercise due diligence. No proof of fraud is required; a director's obligation to ensure remittance triggers the liability.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement and Deficiency

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act section 247 authorizes adjustment to arm's-length amount; section 247(3) penalty of 10% on the net adjustment plus interest, subject to applicable de minimis thresholds.

Director personal liability: Directors are generally not automatically liable for corporate TP penalties. However, directors who cause the corporation to sign false or misleading documentation, or who use TP arrangements to disguise dividends or criminal proceeds, may be personally liable under fraud or civil conspiracy theories; criminal prosecution for conspiracy under Criminal Code section 380 applies.
Shell Corporation and Treaty-Abuse / General Anti-Avoidance

Statutory sanction: CRA will apply GAAR (section 245) to deny the tax benefit, add penalties, and reassess the conduit arrangement. Under treaty, entitlement to relief may be denied for lack of full facts and substance, with possible interest and gross negligence penalties. Criminal charge for conspiracy to defraud the Crown under Criminal Code section 380 is possible.

Director personal liability: Directors who act as nominees in a staged corporate structure are not shielded by limited liability if the corporation is a mere agent or sham. CRA may pierce the veil for fraud; directors face personal restitution, director travel-ban orders in serious tax disputes, and imprisonment for fraudulent evasion or conspiracy.
