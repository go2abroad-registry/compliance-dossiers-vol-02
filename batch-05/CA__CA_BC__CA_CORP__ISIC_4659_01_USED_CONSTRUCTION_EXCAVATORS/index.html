Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); online portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-business-account.html)

E-government filing portal

A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada must register with the CRA for a Business Number (BN), GST/HST account, and, where applicable, payroll and import/export accounts. Registration may be completed through the CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) service. A corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the British Columbia Business Corporations Act is issued a BN with a 9-digit program account suffix: RT (GST/HST), RC (payroll), RM (import/export), or RP (corporate income tax). For GST/HST purposes, a non-resident not resident in Canada but engaged in a commercial activity in Canada must register unless the taxable supplies fall below the small supplier threshold. The CRA Form RC1, 'Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts', is the standard application. For non-residents, a Canadian representative or agent may be required for service of process and record keeping.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A business corporation is, by default, an entity-level taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act (Canada), with no check-the-box pass-through election analogous to the U.S. regime. Relevant statutory elections include: (i) deemed resident election under subsection 250(2) for a non-resident corporation that was previously a Canadian resident; (ii) joint election under subsection 216(1) for non-resident corporations earning Canadian rental or timber royalties to be taxed on net income; (iii) hybrid mismatch elections and recharacterization rules under sections 18.2 and 18.3 of the Income Tax Act; (iv) election under subsection 219(2) for a non-resident corporation to file Part I tax returns instead of Part I.3 and Part XIII withholdings in respect of Canadian business income. A U.S.-resident LLC may file Form 8832 check-the-box documentation for U.S. purposes; however, Canada does not recognize the U.S. check-the-box election for domestic tax purposes. Under the Canada-U.S. tax treaty, an LLC treated as a corporation for U.S. tax purposes is generally a corporation for Canadian treaty purposes, whereas a fiscally transparent LLC may be analyzed on a look-through basis.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

Small supplier threshold: CAD 30,000 per calendar quarter (i.e., CAD 30,000 or less in taxable supplies in a single calendar quarter and in the preceding four calendar quarters; globally aggregated for non-resident persons). Once exceeded, mandatory GST/HST registration. For PST, there is no registration threshold for a dealer selling taxable goods in British Columbia if the person has a place of business in the province or makes taxable sales in the province.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating (GST/HST at 0%) applies to a supply of tangible personal property made by a registrant where the property is exported from Canada by the supplier or on behalf of the supplier, or where the supply is a zero-rated export of goods under section 10 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act. For used construction excavators and heavy machinery exported from British Columbia, the following conditions must be met: (i) the supplier must be a GST/HST registrant; (ii) the goods must be delivered to a destination outside Canada and physically exported within a reasonable time; (iii) the supplier must retain evidence of exportation, which if the goods are shipped by the supplier or an agent of the supplier generally includes a bill of lading, customs export declaration, or similar documentary proof. Supplies of services performed on goods destined for export are also zero-rated under sections 7 and 9 of Part V if the services relate to tangible personal property that is exported as part of the supply, but commission and trading services may be taxable unless the intermediary is acting as an agent for a non-resident and the goods are exported. The sale of used excavators to a non-resident for pick-up in Canada and subsequent export by the purchaser is not zero-rated unless the supplier is also the exporter or holds a certificate in prescribed form confirming exportation (Form GST11, 'Tax Adjustment Worksheet' is insufficient alone; CRA often requires the export bill of lading or a completed Form GST418 as commercial invoice evidence).

  • Commercial Invoice and Ocean or Air Waybill / Bill of Lading evidencing shipment from a Canadian port to a foreign destination
  • Proof of foreign destination and consignee (e.g., foreign port of discharge, foreign warehouse receipt, or customs clearance certificate from the destination country)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank payment/Bank Settlement Advice (BSA) showing receipt of sale proceeds from a non-resident purchaser in foreign currency

Canada does not currently operate a real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping mandate comparable to Latin American CDF/DIF systems. GST/HST-registered suppliers must issue invoices containing prescribed information under the Excise Tax Act; however, electronic invoicing is not mandatory. The CRA publishes a Secure Electronic Invoicing (SEI) framework permitting e-invoices in structured XML/EDIFACT formats. The federal government has announced a phased roadmap toward a continuous transaction controls (CTC) model, with voluntary real-time digital reporting via the CRA's GST/HST reconciliation and tax data exchange; nevertheless, no binding API clearance is currently imposed. In British Columbia, PST-registered vendors must obtain a PST registration number and file returns electronically, but no digital tax clearance stamp is applied to invoices.

The CRA may assess net tax, interest, and penalties under sections 280, 281.1, and 285 of the Excise Tax Act for false or inflated zero-rated export claims. A false statement made knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence may attract a civil penalty under subsection 285(1) equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the net tax understated, plus retrospective reassessments within the normal four-year period, extended to seven years in cases of misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default, and without time limit for fraud. Failure to register a small supplier that exceeds the threshold results in recovery of uncollected GST plus penalties under section 280 of up to 5% of the amount not remitted, plus 1% per month up to 12 months. Where a zero-rated export supply is disallowed because of insufficient documentation, the CRA will claw back input tax credits (ITCs) previously claimed on the acquisition or refurbishment of the used excavators, and interest under sections 298-299.1 of the Excise Tax Act accrues from the original return due date. Exports falsely documented to obtain refunds constitute tax evasion under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act and under section 239 of the Income Tax Act if the scheme involves income tax corruption, with possible fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to five years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is a free-floating, fully convertible currency with complete current-account and capital-account convertibility. There are no exchange-control approvals, surrender requirements, or central-bank licensing steps for capital inflows, profit repatriation, or dividend remittances. The only fiscal frictions are Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (25% statutory, treaty-reducible to between 5% and 15% for dividends under Canada's income tax conventions), and the requirement that all international electronic fund transfers of C$10,000 or more be reported to FINTRAC. Capital-contribution injections are not taxable when properly documented as issued share capital or a bona fide shareholder loan.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline for repatriation of export proceeds and no mandatory conversion ratio. A BC exporter may hold receivables indefinitely in USD or any convertible currency. For tax, foreign-currency debts and receivables are revalued under Income Tax Act s. 39(1) and (2), producing capital gains or losses on settlement or at year-end in specified circumstances; receivables and payables are also marked-to-market for accrual purposes. Funds received through the clearing system must nevertheless be traceable to the underlying sale (invoice, bill of lading, inspection certificate) to satisfy FINTRAC and bank know-your-transaction duties.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); Canada maintains no separate Foreign Exchange Administration. Monetary and payments law derives from the Bank of Canada Act, the Currency Act, and the Payment Clearing and Settlement Act; AML/ATF supervision is exercised by FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada), while the Department of Finance Canada steers exchange-rate and sanctions policy.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM — usable for ancillary operating liquidity only. Regulated but unlicensed digital EMIs such as Wise (Wise Payments Canada Inc.), Payoneer, and Airwallex hold money-services-business (MSB) registrations under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and provincial MSB licensing, not federally unrestricted Canadian bank charters. They may offer FX conversion, payroll/vendor payouts, and small-value trade settlements, but they cannot lawfully accept demand deposits as a 'bank', issue documentary letters of credit, hold trade escrow in a custodial banking capacity, or provide SWIFT-correspondent clearing for large machinery shipments. Legal constraint: funds parked in an EMI sub-account are not protected under the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation Act (CDIC), and Canadian Schedule I banks may restrict inbound clearings from EMIs as higher-risk. Use EMIs as a parallel rail for <10% of monthly liquidity; route all capital injections, LC collateral, and escrow through Tier 1 banks.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant protocol: (1) Parent executes board/shareholder resolutions approving the equity contribution and issues a corporate resolution to the BC entity; (2) funds are wired from the parent to the BC company's CAD/USD account at a Schedule I bank via SWIFT MT103 with instruction field stating 'Contribution to share capital – issue of common shares', never via third-party commingling or cryptocurrency; (3) the Canadian bank performs OFAC/Sanctions screening and reports the international EFT of C$10,000+ to FINTRAC under s. 7(1) of the PCMLTFA; (4) the BC company files an updated share register and issues share certificates with a valuation opinion if the contribution is above book value; (5) to avoid s. 212 dividend characterization, the contribution must be issued as shares within a reasonable period and never repaid as distributions out of the same account unless withholding applies. For USD injections use a USD clearing account correspondent; conversion to CAD may be executed at the bank's spot with a documented 'FX conversion of equity injection' ticket to preserve a clean tax trail.For purchases of used excavators from United States/Asia-Pacific suppliers: primary rail is Commercial Letter of Credit (documentary, UCP600) for shipments above CAD 250,000 — issued by the BC bank in favour of the seller, either sight or 30-day term, with partial shipment allowed, bills of lading, and inspection certificate as required documents; for smaller or recurring payments, SWIFT MT103 wire transfers under SWIFT gpi with extended tracking, generating an ISO 20022 pain.001 instruction for the Canadian side; CAD payments clear through Payments Canada LVTS/ACSS; USD payments clear via US correspondent banks (ABA/Fedwire) or cross-border ACH when same-day USD settlement is not critical; standby LCs (ISP98) support advance-payment guarantees demanded by sellers; 'red clause' advances may finance the 10–20% prepayment before shipping, secured against the inspection certificate.Deliverable OTC FX forwards (CAD/USD, CAD/JPY, CAD/EUR) under an ISDA 2002 Master Agreement or bank FX authorization letterEuropean-style FX call and put options (plain-vanilla and zero-cost collars)FX swaps (swap-in/swap-out), commonly used for short-term liquidity in USD clearing accountsCross-currency swaps (USD/CAD interest and principal exchange) for term funding of capital-intensive inventory purchasesNon-deliverable forwards (NDFs) on third-country restricted currencies (e.g., BRL, CLP, IDR, PHP) quoted by Canadian dealing desks where the underlying machinery procurement has an EME invoice currencyAverage-rate and participating forwards for smoothing CAD/USD settlement risk on repeating import payments

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: MEDIUM — usable for ancillary operating liquidity only. Regulated but unlicensed digital EMIs such as Wise (Wise Payments Canada Inc.), Payoneer, and Airwallex hold money-services-business (MSB) registrations under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and provincial MSB licensing, not federally unrestricted Canadian bank charters. They may offer FX conversion, payroll/vendor payouts, and small-value trade settlements, but they cannot lawfully accept demand deposits as a 'bank', issue documentary letters of credit, hold trade escrow in a custodial banking capacity, or provide SWIFT-correspondent clearing for large machinery shipments. Legal constraint: funds parked in an EMI sub-account are not protected under the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation Act (CDIC), and Canadian Schedule I banks may restrict inbound clearings from EMIs as higher-risk. Use EMIs as a parallel rail for <10% of monthly liquidity; route all capital injections, LC collateral, and escrow through Tier 1 banks.
