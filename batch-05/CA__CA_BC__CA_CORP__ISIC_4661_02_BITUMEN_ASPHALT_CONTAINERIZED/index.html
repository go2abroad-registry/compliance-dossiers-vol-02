In British Columbia, the Provincial Sales Tax is administered separately by the Ministry of Finance, Taxation Branch, through the BC Taxpayer Account / eTaxBC portal at gov.bc.ca/pst. E-government filing portal

For a foreign-owned BC business corporation or Canadian branch: (1) Incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or BC Business Corporations Act and obtain articles, certificate of incorporation, registered office and Canadian address for service; a corporation incorporated in Canada is deemed resident under s. 250(4) of the Income Tax Act. (2) Apply to the CRA for the 9-digit federal Business Number (BN) and program accounts — corporate income tax (BN-RT), GST/HST (BN-RC), payroll (BN-RP) and import/export (BN-RM) — using Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1. (3) Register in British Columbia for a PST vendor number with the BC Ministry of Finance before making the first taxable sale in BC. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not maintain a check-the-box or entity-classification election; a business corporation is mandatorily an entity-level taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act, with no pass-through classification available. Statutory elections applicable in this environment include: (1) Excise Tax Act s. 156 election permitting closely related corporations to deem qualifying intra-group supplies to be made for nil consideration for GST/HST purposes; (2) Income Tax Act s. 85(1) election to transfer property to a corporation on a tax-deferred rollover basis, relevant when contributing asphalt inventory or operating assets; (3) Income Tax Act s. 216(1) election allowing a non-resident recipient of Canadian rents or timber royalties to be taxed on a net rather than gross basis; and (4) Income Tax Act s. 93(1) election applicable to dividends from a foreign affiliate. Canada's hybrid-mismatch rules apply automatically to deny deductions on specified hybrid arrangements and instruments; no corrective election or check-the-box designation is available to override them.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold measured on total worldwide taxable supplies over the four preceding calendar quarters (zero-rated exports count toward the threshold); a non-resident vendor making taxable supplies in Canada must register once the threshold is exceeded and may voluntarily register below it. BC PST: mandatory registration for any vendor making taxable sales in British Columbia; out-of-province remote sellers must register when B.C.-destination taxable sales exceed approximately CAD 10,000 over a 12-month period. The wholesale asphalt entity in BC will be registered for both regimes.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Schedule VI, ss. 1 and 3 of the Excise Tax Act, supplies of goods — including containerised and drummed penetration-grade bitumen classified under HS 2713 / NAICS 424720 — made to non-residents are zero-rated for GST where possession transfers in Canada and the goods are exported in the same state as delivered as soon as reasonable after the supply (CRA administrative benchmark: approximately 60 days), by the supplier or the non-resident purchaser. Conditions: (1) the recipient is a non-resident; (2) the goods are not materially altered or consumed in Canada; (3) the supplier obtains and retains documentary proof of export satisfactory to the CRA; and (4) the export is reported to the CBSA. International freight and associated transport services connected to the export are likewise zero-rated under Schedule VI. For BC PST, the sale is exempt when delivery occurs outside British Columbia, evidenced by carrier records and out-of-province destination documentation. Refund mechanics: the zero-rated export itself bears 0% GST and the supplier recovers all embedded input tax credits (credits for GST paid on Canadian purchases) through the ordinary GST/HST return; no separate refund application is required.

  • CBSA B13A Export Declaration (Canada Customs Coding Form) or equivalent electronic export report filed via the Canadian Export Reporting System
  • Commercial invoice and packing list describing the asphalt/bitumen goods and the non-resident consignee
  • Bill of Lading (ocean container or rail) evidencing carriage to the non-resident buyer at an out-of-Canada destination
  • CBSA cargo exit evidence including vessel/rail/terminal outward movement records and destination customs entry documentation
  • Transport carrier and container terminal receipts (container gate-out, loaded weight certificate, load-port tally)
  • Bank inward remittance certificate or SWIFT MT103 / telegraphic transfer confirmation evidencing receipt of export proceeds
  • UN/TDG dangerous-goods certificates applicable to elevated-temperature asphalt handling, together with IMO/IMDG container packing declarations when required

Canada has no real-time continuous-control invoice clearance regime (no fiscal digital stamping, mandatory CTC/API clearance or government database pre-approval of invoices). Electronic mandates nevertheless apply: GST/HST returns must be filed electronically via GST/HST NETFILE or My Business Account under the CRA's phased mandatory e-filing expansion; remittances above CAD 10,000 must be paid electronically; export declarations are filed with the CBSA through the Canadian International Trade Single Window (CITSW) and the Canadian Export Reporting System, supported by e-manifest / ACI data for rail, highway and marine outbound movements. No government-issued digital stamping of invoices is required.

The CRA audits export zero-rating claims through GST/HST desk and field reviews, while the CBSA verifies physical exit and export declarations. Where the exporter fails to assemble the B13A export declaration, bill of lading and payment evidence within the standard 180-day substantiation window without commercial justification, the supply may be re-assessed as taxable at 5% GST; associated input tax credits are denied and compound daily interest is charged from the original return due date. If a false statement or omission is made knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence, the Excise Tax Act imposes a civil penalty of 50% of the net tax evaded (with escalation for repeat failures) and criminal prosecution for evasion is available. Directors and officers may incur personal liability for unremitted corporate GST/HST under s. 323(1) of the Excise Tax Act. For BC PST, shipments not substantiated as delivered outside the province are clawed back at 7% with interest and penalties under the Provincial Sales Tax Act. In addition, where export proceeds are not repatriated within the 180-day benchmark, the CRA may recharacterise the outstanding receivable under the transfer-pricing rules of s. 247 of the Income Tax Act as a shareholder benefit, appropriation or non-arm's length loan, triggering income inclusion with gross-up and related penalties.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full current-account and capital-account convertibility under the Bretton Woods Agreements Act. No restrictions on foreign-currency accounts, capital inflows, or profit and dividend remittances; repatriation is subject exclusively to Canadian tax withholding (Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. 1 (5th Supp.)) and transfer-pricing documentation. No exchange-control licensing or pre-approval applies to a British Columbia wholesale bitumen trading corporation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no surrender, repatriation, or mandatory conversion obligation on export proceeds; foreign currency may be retained offshore. Compliance is limited to books-and-records retention, Canadian GAAP/IFRS consolidation, and OECD transfer-pricing documentation under ss. 233.1 and 247 of the Income Tax Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary authority and fiscal agent to the Minister of Finance for the Exchange Fund Account; banking supervision by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/ATF supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada abolished statutory foreign-exchange control in 1951; no dedicated foreign-exchange administration exists.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

PayPal Fit: High

Low as a primary treasury rail; Medium as an ancillary collections layer. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined federal and British Columbia general corporate rate (15% federal General Rate Reduction after M&P adjustments, plus 12% BC general corporate income tax); reduced effective rates apply to Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPC) on active business income up to CAD 500,000 (federal 9% + BC 2% = 11%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid or deemed paid to a non-resident, subject to tax treaty reduction; under the Canada-US treaty, qualified dividends paid to a company owning 10% or more of the voting stock are taxed at 5%, and other dividend ownership at 15%.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada requires contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions involving a taxpayer and a related non-resident. A mandatory documentation penalty applies where the aggregate value of such transactions exceeds CAD 1 million, and the taxpayer has not made reasonable and adequate efforts to document the arm's-length basis. There is no prescribed CAD amount for a Local File or Master File, but CRA follows OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines; the Local File and Master File should be prepared when any controlled transaction is material, and T106 reporting is triggered at CAD 1 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations; instead, substance is tested through the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR), treaty limitation-on-benefits provisions, and corporate domestic law requiring central management and control in Canada for fiscal residence. A Canadian registered corporation that is not managed from Canada and has no operating employees, premises, or commercial activity is at risk of being treated as a shell under CRA administrative doctrine. A non-resident corporation selling containerized bitumen into BC will be considered to have a permanent establishment only if it maintains a fixed base or dependent agent in BC; however, the purchase-resale of goods on Canadian soil by a non-resident is a taxable Canadian business only if the non-resident has a permanent establishment. The substance of storage, logistics, and trading activity is critical to avoid deemed permanent establishment risk under Article V of Canada's DTAs.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: On summary conviction, fines from 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to 2 years; on indictment, fines to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to 5 years. The CRA may also assess civil penalties of 50% of the understated tax under subsection 163(2) (gross negligence), plus mandatory interest.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for T2 deficiencies, but they may be assessed for unremitted Part XIII withholding taxes and employee source deductions under subsection 227.1(1) if reasonable care was not exercised. Directors who knowingly participate in evasion face personal fines, seizure of personal assets, travel bans through court orders, and imprisonment under the Income Tax Act and the criminal law where fraud is established.
Sham and Artificial Transaction under GAAR

Statutory sanction: The CRA may disregard the scheme under the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245 of the Income Tax Act), reassess the transaction at the arm's-length substance level, impose a 10% reassessment penalty in certain aggressive cases, and deny treaty benefits.

Director personal liability: Directors and shareholders who structure the sham may be jointly liable for the tax owing under the corporate veil-piercing doctrine where the corporation is the directed instrument of fraud; civil restitution and personal liability can extend to all unpaid tax and penalties.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing

Statutory sanction: CRA transfer-pricing adjustment under section 247; adjustment penalty equal to 10% of the adjusted amount where the taxpayer fails to make reasonable efforts to establish an arm's-length price and transaction value exceeds CAD 1 million, plus interest and compounding. The penalty does not apply where the taxpayer has contemporaneous documentation.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly sign false transfer-pricing documentation are exposed to gross-negligence penalties and may be personally liable for source withholding amounts only, but in fraud-based mispricing the corporate form cannot shield deliberate tax fraud.
Boston Unreporting and Beneficial Ownership Transparency Failure

Statutory sanction: Under the Canada Business Corporations Act and BC registry rules, false or omitted beneficial ownership declarations carry administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 1 million for the corporation, and directors and officers may face fines; the BC Corporate Transparency Act (in force for obligated recipients of public funding) extends disclosure obligations.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and any person capable of exercising control who knowingly permits the continuation of an undisclosed beneficial owner may be held civilly liable for corporate debts under the doctrine of statutory liability, and criminal sanctions include fines and imprisonment up to 5 years where the concealment is part of money laundering.
AML Integration through Trade Payments

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, failure to verify beneficial own and maintain transaction records attracts fines of up to CAD 500,000 per administrative violation per day, and criminal penalties of up to CAD 2 million and imprisonment of 5 years for laundering. The FINTRAC and BC Securities Commission can also issue cease and desist orders.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers of a paying or receiving entity who knowingly engage in a transaction with a third party in Canada are personally accountable for fines to CAD 1 million and imprisonment of up to 5 years; civil recovery of proceeds is available to the Crown.
