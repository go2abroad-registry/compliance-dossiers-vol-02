Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — federal; British Columbia Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch for PST. Primary portals: CRA 'My Business Account' (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/e-services-businesses/business-account.html) and Business Registration Online (BRO) at https://www.canada.ca/en/services/taxes/business-registration.html.

E-government filing portal

Step 2: Register for the 9-digit Business Number (BN) through the CRA's Business Registration Online service or paper Form RC1, indicating the RT (GST/HST) program account and RC (corporate income tax) account. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not operate US-style 'check-the-box' classification; corporations are per se taxable entities under Part I of the Income Tax Act (ITA). Relevant elections: (1) ETA s. 273 election for closely related corporations to treat supplies as made for nil consideration; (2) ETA s. 211.1 election allowing a non-resident to claim zero-rating on goods/services acquired for export; (3) ITA s. 216 election for non-resident landlords to file on net rental income; (4) ITA s. 219 branch tax election for non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada; (5) Hybrid mismatch rules under ITA ss. 12.7–12.82 (applicable from 2021) disallowing deductions for excess hybrid mismatch amounts, with no elective carve-outs except limited transitional grandfathering. No federal-provincial tax sharing election applies to the BC PST regime.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000 small-supplier threshold over any four consecutive calendar quarters for GST/HST registration (Excise Tax Act s. 240). BC PST registration is mandatory from the first taxable retail sale regardless of threshold, but wholesale sales to licensed resellers or export sales do not require PST collection until 'sale at retail' occurs in BC.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of goods exported by the vendor are zero-rated under section 23 and Part V of Schedule VI, Excise Tax Act, provided the supplier retains evidence that the copper cathodes were exported 'as or after delivery' — typically proof of shipment out of Canada within 60 days of delivery and delivery to a non-resident purchaser. Directly exported goods, including LME Grade A copper cathodes sold for physical delivery abroad, qualify for 0% GST without the exporter collecting 5% GST. Services that directly support exported goods (freight, warehousing, inspection) supplied to non-residents are zero-rated under Part V s. 7 and Part VI s. 23. BC PST: sales of copper cathodes for export or for resale by a licensed PST registrant are exempt with a valid BC PST exemption certificate (Form FIN 441) or export documentation. Copper cathodes imported into Canada temporarily for blending/repackaging before export may qualify for relief under the Customs Tariff s. 105 if prescribed conditions are met.

  • CBSA Cargo/Export Declaration — Form B13A or electronic submission through CERS (Canadian Export Reporting System) showing HS code 7403.11 (cathodes and sections of cathodes), value in CAD, and ultimate destination country
  • Commercial Invoice reflecting the LME-based sale price, incoterms, delivery terms, and contract references
  • Bill of Lading (ocean/air/rail) or Cargo Control Document evidencing physical movement of the copper cathodes out of Canada
  • LME warrant or warehouse delivery order demonstrating title transfer of the specific cathode lots
  • Proof of payment — SWIFT MT103 inward remittance, documentary letter of credit settlement records, or bank transfer confirmation matched to the invoice
  • Packing list and weight certificate (third-party inspection certificate, e.g., SGS/Alex Stewart, confirming compliant Grade A specification)

Canada has no real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or API-based government validation of e-invoices (unlike Latin American CFDI/NF-e models). Mandates: (1) GST/HST returns must be filed electronically by all registrants with annual taxable revenues exceeding C$1 million (most large traders); (2) CRA-compliant electronic records and invoices are accepted for audits, and GST/HST registrants must retain records for six years; (3) exporters using the CBSA must submit cargo and export declarations electronically via CERS; (4) the CRA's 'Enhanced Review Module' may request invoice-line-level data on a post-clearance basis; there is no pre-shipment government e-invoice clearance step.

CRA may reassess within the normal 4-year limitation period (unlimited for misrepresentation, neglect, or fraud). If zero-rating is denied because export evidence is deficient, the supplier is assessed 5% GST on the sale plus interest (currently approximately 8%+ per annum, daily compound). Specific penalties: Excise Tax Act s. 285 — 5% penalty on unreported/dishonest amount plus 5% on any second repeated assessment; gross negligence penalty under s. 285(1.1) of 50% of the net tax amount disallowed if the false statement resulted from gross negligence; falsifying export documents may lead to prosecution under ITA s. 239 and the Customs Act with fines up to C$50,000 and imprisonment for up to 2 years. CRA's 'Strategic and Sectors' unit targets base-metal traders for false export zero-rating claims; BC PST audit may also claw back PST exemptions improperly claimed at 7% with 10% penalty interest under the BC Provincial Sales Tax Act. Note: no statutory 180-day FX repatriation deadline exists for customs or GST purposes in Canada; the 180-day benchmark is a commercial trade-finance practice used by Canadian banks to mitigate AML/CTF exposure and is referenced for internal compliance documentation.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains fully convertible current and capital accounts with no exchangerestrictions, no prior approval for profit remittances, and no distinction between resident and non-resident capital accounts. The Canadian dollar is a freely floating currency under IMF Article VIII. Profit remittances are subject only to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (statutory 25%, generally treaty-reduced, e.g., 15% under the Canada-US treaty and similar US-protocol terms for shareholders in treaty jurisdictions); a branch remittance tax under subsection 219(1) of the Income Tax Act may apply to a Canadian branch of a non-resident. No Canadian statutory hurdle applies to external dividend or equity-capital repatriation beyond routine corporate governance documentation and the bank's KYC file.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No surrender, remittance, repatriation, or conversion obligation applies to Canadian export proceeds. There is no statutory timeline requiring conversion of foreign currency into CAD, and no mandated conversion ratio for proceeds received under a metals trading contract. Exporters may freely retain foreign exchange offshore or in foreign-currency operating accounts with Canadian banks. Fiscal compliance duties are limited to the export declaration filed with the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for customs statistics, and FINTRAC reporting for electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more within a 24-hour window.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) and Department of Finance Canada for overall monetary and fiscal policy; Canada has no dedicated foreign-exchange control authority. The Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises Schedule I and II banks, banks' market conduct, and capital adequacy. The Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) administers the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) for AML/CTF compliance. Payment clearing is governed by Payments Canada, with high-value CAD settlement on the LYNX Real-Time Settlement system and retail via the Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium-low feasibility for treasury-grade purposes. Canadian law does not recognise the EU/UK electronic-money institution (EMI) licence category and prohibits unlicensed deposit-taking. Foreign EMIs cannot provide documentary letters of credit, LME warrant-linked trade finance, or hold customer funds as deposits in a manner equivalent to a bank. Since 2024, payment service providers (including cross-border digital EMIs) must register under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) with the Bank of Canada, which governs payment services but does not confer banking powers. Such fintech rails are viable only as a secondary, low-value inbound funding and FX conversion channel, not as the primary cash concentration bank for a copper cathode wholesaler.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed within six months after the fiscal period-end. The tax balance is due generally within two months after year-end for non-CCPC corporations and within three months after year-end for a CCPC entitled to the small business deduction; installments are due throughout the taxation year where prior-year tax exceeds C$3,000.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate reportable non-arm's-length cross-border transaction value triggers Form T106 filing; C$114,000,000 multinational consolidated revenue triggers Country-by-Country Report filing; no fixed statutory Canadian dollar threshold creates a formal Local File/Master File duty, but CRA requires contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation for all non-arm's-length cross-border transactions and may demand such documentation in any audit.

Economic substance scope: British Columbia and Canada do not impose Gulf-style Economic Substance Regulations. Canadian tax law applies ordinary substance-over-form principles, the sham doctrine, and the statutory General Anti-Avoidance Rule under section 245. A copper-cathode wholesale trading corporation must demonstrate real employees, decision-making authority, actual inventory or LME warrant risk, logistics functions and commercial substance in Canada. Lack of such substance may cause CRA to recharacterize the arrangement, allocate income to the foreign principal, deny treaty benefits, and impose penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion – Understatement of Copper Cathode Trading Income

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under section 239 of the Income Tax Act; conviction for indictable tax evasion attracts a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to two years. Civil gross-negligence penalty of 50% of understated tax plus daily interest and arrears interest also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who directed or authorized the evasion face joint and several criminal liability, imprisonment, court-ordered restitution, travel restrictions and seizure orders. CRA may also assess directors personally for unremitted withholding or source deductions where they were the responsible persons.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing / Profit-Shifting

Statutory sanction: CRA may re-determine income under section 247(2). A transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment applies where the taxpayer did not make reasonable efforts; a 50% gross-negligence penalty, interest, and late T106 penalties may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for intentional transfer-pricing mispricing where the corporation is a facade. CRA may invoke section 160 to recover assets transferred to non-arm's-length persons for inadequate consideration, and courts may issue asset freezes, restitution orders and, in criminal cases, travel bans and imprisonment.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: DTA relief is denied; business profits may be attributed to the foreign principal or Canadian permanent establishment; branch tax of 25% under Part XIV can apply; GAAR adjustments, 50% penalties, and interest are imposed on the resulting tax underpayment.

Director personal liability: Courts may pierce the corporate veil where the company is a mere puppet of the non-resident shareholders. Directors may face civil restitution, confiscation of assets, and, in serious criminal tax evasion or fraud prosecutions, travel restrictions and imprisonment.
AML / Money Laundering / FINTRAC Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Money laundering under section 462.31 of the Criminal Code is an indictable offence punishable by imprisonment for up to 10 years. FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties can reach C$250,000 or more per violation; the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act imposes separate compliance obligations on reporting entities.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in laundering or willfully fail to implement required AML controls are personally exposed to criminal prosecution, confiscation orders, asset seizure, and travel restrictions pending trial; regulatory bodies may also issue perpetual prohibition orders against directors in commodity and securities markets.
