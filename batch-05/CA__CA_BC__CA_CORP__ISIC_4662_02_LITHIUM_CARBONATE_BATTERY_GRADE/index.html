Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not offer a US-style check-the-box election. A corporation is a separate taxpayer for federal and provincial income tax purposes. Relevant statutory mechanisms include the Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) small business deduction under Income Tax Act (ITA) section 125, the associated corporations election / designation under ITA 256, and cross-border hybrid mismatch rules under ITA 18.4 to 18.6. No hybrid mismatch election is available domestically; the CRA applies statutory deeming rules to prevent double non-taxation.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies made in Canada over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters, or in any single calendar quarter, triggers mandatory GST/HST registration. Non-residents carrying on business in Canada are subject to the same threshold; those not carrying on business in Canada generally have a higher degree of exemption but may register voluntarily to claim input tax credits. BC PST registration and a PST account are required when the corporation makes taxable retail or wholesale sales in BC other than sales that are exempt or zero-rated as exports.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

GST/HST zero-rating applies to a supply of goods that the supplier exports directly from Canada, provided the goods are delivered outside Canada and the supplier retains evidence of export as required under CRA GST/HST Memorandum 8-2. Batteries and battery input materials such as lithium carbonate are taxable supplies at the standard rate when sold domestically, but become zero-rated when exported in commercial quantities to a non-resident customer. Offshore services supplied to a non-resident may also be zero-rated to the extent the services are performed wholly outside Canada or meet the conditions in Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act (Canada). The zero-rating election requires the supplier to have genuine commercial export documents, including contracts, customs declarations, transport documentation, and proof of payment from outside Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A, CADEX, or Canada Customs Automated Data Exchange record)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank wire transfer confirmation evidencing foreign currency receipt
  • Commercial invoice and packing list
  • Proof of delivery outside Canada (e.g., destination-country customs entry or signed delivery receipt)
  • Incoterms-based sales contract or purchase order from the foreign customer

CRA does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or API-based invoice clearance for GST/HST. Taxpayers must file GST/HST returns electronically via GST/HST NETFILE or via a CRA-approved service provider when registered. Invoices must show the supplier's BN, the date, a description of the goods, and the applicable GST/HST rate and amount. British Columbia requires PST registrants to maintain prescribed sales and purchase records and to issue invoices or cash register receipts for taxable sales; exports of goods from BC are exempt from PST when the supplier obtains and retains documentary evidence of export. The Pan-Canadian Trust Framework supports PEPPOL BIS 3.0 e-invoicing, but it is not a statutory clearance mandate.

The CRA may deny zero-rating on audit where export documentation is incomplete, impose a GST/HST reassessment at the 5% rate on the gross selling price, and assess arrears interest from the original due date. False or deceptive claims may attract penalties under Excise Tax Act section 285 (up to CAD 25,000 or 50% of the amount sought to be evaded) and Income Tax Act section 163.2 for false statements, as well as gross-negligence penalties of up to 50% of the understated tax. Wilful evasion may lead to criminal prosecution under Excise Tax Act sections 327 and 328, with fines up to CAD 50,000 or imprisonment for five years. Records must be retained for six years, and a responsible officer can be held personally liable for penalties if the corporation is a conduit for the export fraud.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is a freely floating, fully convertible currency. Canada accepted IMF Article VIII obligations in 1952 and has unrestricted current-account and capital-account convertibility. There is no statutory exchange-control approval, licensing, rationing, or repatriation restriction for either current-account (trade, freight, insurance, royalties, dividends, interest) or capital-account (foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, loan drawdowns and repayments) transactions. Inbound foreign equity into a British Columbia Business Corporation and outbound profit remittance are lawful without prior central-bank authorization. The sole friction is fiscal: non-resident dividends, interest, and royalties are subject to Canadian non-resident withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (default 25%, reduced to 15% or 5% under the Canada-US and other bilateral income tax treaties), and prescribed foreign-reporting forms (T1134, T1135, T106) and transfer-pricing documentation are required where applicable. The CAD/USD and CAD/CNH markets are deep and liquid through the interbank market; commercial measurements of profitability may face modest transactional FX spread for CAD conversion of USD trade receipts, but no convertibility hurdle or queuing system exists.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No export-proceeds surrender obligation, mandatory conversion ratio, or statutory conversion timeline exists in Canadian federal or British Columbia law. A trading corporation may lawfully retain 100% of export proceeds in USD, CNY, EUR, or any other currency in a Canadian bank account or offshore account, and may hold, convert, or remit them at commercial discretion. The binding compliance framework consists of: (i) CBSA export-reporting and permitting obligations where goods fall under the Export Control List or require an export permit under the Export and Import Permits Act (battery-grade lithium carbonate, being a dual-use-candidate industrial chemical, should be screened against the Good's Import/Export Control List and the Nuclear Suppliers Group triggers); (ii) FINTRAC obligations upon the reporting financial institution to file Large Cash Transaction Reports and International Electronic Fund Transfer Reports for transactions of CAD $10,000 or more within a 24-hour period; (iii) documentation obligations under the Income Tax Act to support arm's-length pricing of intercompany commodity trades; and (iv) anti-money-laundering record-keeping that maps each trade receipt to the underlying sale contract, bill of lading, and certificate of analysis.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), acting in cooperation with the Department of Finance (which owns and administers the Exchange Fund Account) for official reserves and exchange-rate policy; the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for trade-data and customs controls; and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for cross-border transaction monitoring under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Canada maintains no dedicated foreign-exchange-control administration comparable to a currency authority imposing surrender or approval requirements.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: LOW for the primary treasury rail; MEDIUM only as a satellite expense or pre-financing account. Canada does not operate an EEA-style electronic-money-institution licensing regime; non-bank cross-border payment providers (Wise, Payoneer, Airwallex, Ebury, Continental Transfer) operate as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC, subject to PCMLTFA registration, compliance-program, and record-keeping duties. An MSB can receive and disburse CAD and foreign currency, but cannot lawfully take deposits or provide commercial credit from Canadian-issued funds, cannot issue documentary letters of credit (including SWIFT MT700), cannot access the Payments Canada Lynx high-value settlement system or Bank of Canada settlement accounts in the capacity of a bank, and cannot issue bank guarantees. Legal constraints further include FINTRAC's client-identification requirements for MSBs, mandatory Suspicious Transaction Report filings for arbitrage-style rapid fund movements, and the practical risk that a foreign-headquartered EMI has no physical supervision in British Columbia. Accordingly, bank-grade clearing through a Schedule I Canadian bank remains mandatory for lithium-carbonate import disbursements and LC-backed arbitrage transactions.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Large or emerging wire batches to third-country beneficiaries - the bank's automated transaction-monitoring may flag high-value MT103 outflows to multiple beneficiaries shortly after account opening, characteristic of money-laundering structuring.

Root cause: Large or emerging wire batches to third-country beneficiaries - the bank's automated transaction-monitoring may flag high-value MT103 outflows to multiple beneficiaries shortly after account opening, characteristic of money-laundering structuring.

Defense evidence: Institutionalize a transaction-justification program and present it at onboarding: (i) a board-approved payment-calendar matrix linking each monthly disbursement to a specific purchase order, LC reference, vessel name, and invoice number; (ii) a treasury policy establishing per-beneficiary limits, segregation of trade payments from operating expenses, and a prohibition on third-party payment substitutions without documentary override; (iii) quarterly trade-file reconcilements to be made available to the bank; (iv) a commitment to a 24-48 hour 'payment rationale sheet' for every wire exceeding CAD $50,000, signed by the CFO or treasurer; and (v) an agreed account-turnover forecast with escalation thresholds that, when materially exceeded, trigger a proactive disclosure to the Relationship Manager rather than defensive discovery.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Equity subscriptions from non-resident shareholders into the BC operating entity flow by SWIFT MT103 (CAD, USD, EUR, or CNH) to the designated operating or capital-escrow account. Canada imposes no exchange-control approval, no capital-import licence, and no mandatory CAD conversion on inbound direct investment; the bank executes conversion through its FX desk at interbank plus standard spread. The wire's remittance information field 70 should state 'PURPOSE: PAID-IN SHARE CAPITAL / COMMON SHARE SUBSCRIPTION' and reference the shareholder name and subscription agreement date, to differentiate equity from trade debt. The bank will apply FINTRAC-driven source-of-funds verification: shareholder account statements, a certified board resolution authorizing the account opening and designating authorized signatories, and, where the invester entity is itself foreign, its corporate documents and beneficial-ownership certification. Where total inbound equity from a WTO-country investor in a direct acquisition crosses approximately CAD $2.18 billion (2024 indexed threshold), or CAD $5 million for non-WTO investors, notification or net-benefit review under the Investment Canada Act is separately triggered; for a trading vehicle in the battery-metals arbitrage segment these thresholds will ordinarily not apply, but a standard post-closing notification should be filed to preserve optionality. Funds held pre-deployment in a capital-escrow account are permitted to earn interest and may be swept daily into Money Market or Government-of-Canada treasury instruments without triggering new KYC, provided the sweep is disclosed.Outbound clearing for lithium-carbonate procurement and logistics settles through Payments Canada Lynx (CAD legs, finality at Bank of Canada settlement accounts), CHIPS/Fedwire (USD legs via each Canadian bank's US correspondent), TARGET2 (EUR legs), and SWIFT gpi messaging for straight-through processing. The operative rails are: (i) SWIFT MT103 direct or instructed wires for prepayments, open-account, or after-inspection settlement to suppliers in Chile, Argentina, China, Australia, and the United States; (ii) commercial letters of credit issued per UCP 600 via SWIFT MT700, payable at sight or 30-90 days usance, with the advising and confirming bank nominated by the supplier; and (iii) documentary collections (MT400/MT420) where the supplier extends open account terms and the BC importer needs title control. RBCLCXXXX' to avoid monitoring false positives. LC discrepancies in transport documents (bill-of-lading date, quantity certificate, purity clause) must be resolved or formally waived before settlement, and Incoterms must align with the document set (CIF/DAP import; FOB/EXW export). Trade-finance facilities should include a supplier-payment guarantee or standby LC (MT760) only where commercially justified, as Canadian banks treat the issuance as a liquidity commitment reducing available headroom. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined (federal 15% + British Columbia general corporate income tax 12%)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder, unless reduced by a double taxation agreement.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate fair-market value of controlled transactions with non-resident related parties per taxation year triggers mandatory Form T106 disclosure and expectation of contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation. Canada does not prescribe rigid Local/Master File thresholds but follows OECD transfer-pricing Guidelines and CRA Information Circular IC 87-2R for documentation retention on 6-year timelines.

Economic substance scope: British Columbia does not use a Cayman-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Canada applies the general anti-avoidance rule (s.245), the s.247 transfer-pricing substance-over-form test, and the common-law sham doctrine. A BC shell cannot simply hold contracts or issue invoices; it must carry on substantive procurement, inventory ownership, logistics, banking, invoicing, staff functions, and local decision-making with remunerated personnel and board oversight in Canada.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Returns

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.239(1) criminal tax evasion: fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, and/or imprisonment up to 5 years. Gross-negligence penalties under s.163(2) equal 50% of misstated tax plus interest; willful misrepresentations may also trigger exclusion from relief programs.

Director personal liability: Directors who are controlling minds may be jointly prosecuted under s.239; CRA may invoke director-liability under s.227.1 for unremitted source deductions and, where the corporation is a façade, BC courts will pierce the corporate veil. Criminal conviction can trigger international travel bans and extradition risk.
Abusive Transfer Pricing and Shell Company Structure

Statutory sanction: CRA reassessment under s.247(2) to apply arm's-length conditions; s.247(3) penalty of 10% of net transfer-pricing adjustment exceeding C$5 million; application of GAAR under s.245 can deny all tax benefits; loss of CCPC small business deduction; potential denial of Part XIV treaty access.

Director personal liability: Directors can be held personally liable for adjustments if they authorized the artificial pricing and received benefits; s.227.1 statutory director liability may cover unremitted withholding; fraudulent conduct by directors removes the limited-liability shield and subjects them to seizure of personal assets.
AML, Cross-Border Wire and FINTRAC Violations

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act enforcement: administrative monetary penalties up to C$2 million per contravention for a corporate entity; criminal money-laundering offence under Criminal Code s.462.31 punishable by up to 10 years imprisonment; bank-account freezing and asset forfeiture orders are available.

Director personal liability: AML program compliance cannot be legally delegated to an unreachable nominee; directors and officers who knowingly participate in laundering of trade proceeds face criminal liability, director bans, seizure/restitution orders, and Canada Border Services Agency travel holds.
