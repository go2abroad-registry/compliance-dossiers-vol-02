Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) - federal tax administration; CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) portal and 'My Business Account' portal (www.canada.ca); provincial supplementary registration: BC Ministry of Finance, eTaxBC (PST registration), and WorkSafeBC for mandatory workers' compensation coverage.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the British Columbia Business Corporations Act (9-digit federal Business Number (BN) is not required for incorporation; BC Registry provides a provincial incorporation number). Step 2: Register with the CRA via Business Registration Online, by mail, or by telephone to obtain the 9-digit BN and program accounts: RC (Corporation Income Tax - T2), RT (GST/HST), and RP (Payroll). Step 3: A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada must obtain a BN and file a T2 return under ITA ss. 150(1)(a); a non-resident that makes taxable supplies in Canada but has no permanent establishment must also register under ETA s. 240 (or may elect to do so). Step 4: Register with BC Ministry of Finance for Provincial Sales Tax (PST) if taxable retail sales (incl. POD apparel) are made in BC; note that small supplier status does not exempt PST vendor registration in BC. Step 5: Register for the BC Employer Health Tax (EHT) only if annual total BC remuneration exceeds C$500,000; collect payroll source deductions and remit to CRA on the RP account. Compliance: T2 corporate return due 6 months after fiscal year-end; GST/HST return generally due 1 month after the reporting period; annual GST/HST for small suppliers; BC PST return (Form PST-100) and monthly/quarterly remittances.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible under both current and capital accounts. There are no exchange control restrictions on profit remittance, dividends, or loan repayments to non-residents. Non-resident withholding tax (Part XIII of the Income Tax Act) applies to dividends (typically 25% but reduced by treaty), and branch remittance tax under Part XIV may apply. In practice, no prior regulatory approval is required for transfer of funds, but all cross-border movements above CAD 10,000 must be reported to FINTRAC by financial intermediaries. Capital account transactions are fully liberalized, subject only to sub-50% ownership rules in certain regulated sectors.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender requirements exist. Canadian exporters are not required to repatriate or convert export proceeds into CAD within a specified timeline, and may maintain foreign-currency receivables in domestic or offshore accounts. However, exporters must comply with PCMLTFA trade transparency and cross-border wire reporting thresholds, and for tax purposes, inventory costs and revenue recognition follow the Income Tax Act. In practice, a deemed disposition of currency gains or losses is recognized only when converted or when financial statements are compiled under functional currency rules.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BOC) and the Department of Finance Canada (policy and regulatory framework). The BOC is responsible for monetary policy and the national currency, while OSFI supervises deposit-taking institutions. Exchange control authority is exercised under the Currency Act, Canada Business Corporations Act, and Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility: Medium. Canadian law permits the use of fintech and offshore virtual banking services (e.g., Wise Business, Payoneer, Airwallex) for cross-border treasury operations, provided such entities are regulated by FINTRAC as money service businesses (MSBs) if they operate in Canada or are licensed under equivalent regimes (e.g., FCA in UK, MSB in Hong Kong). Major constraints: (i) funds held with unregulated EMIs are not eligible for Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC) protection; (ii) Canadian bank account based on EMI routing numbers may not accept CAD settlement from domestic payment gateways like Stripe or PayPal unless the EMI maintains a partnership with a Canadian schedule I bank; (iii) foreign EMIs are generally not accepted as settlement accounts for Canadian tax/GST filings or payroll direct deposits; (iv) PBOC-style controls are irrelevant but sanctions screening and UBO transparency rules under PCMLTFA may require an in-person or video-KYC process. A hybrid structure is recommended: a domestic CDIC-insured sweep account for tax and payroll, with an EMI only for low-value FX conversions and supplier payments.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Non-resident UBO with ownership above 25% and source of funds not clearly verified.

Root cause: Non-resident UBO with ownership above 25% and source of funds not clearly verified.

Defense evidence: Provide (i) corporate registry extract from jurisdiction of incorporation, (ii) notarized identification and proof of address for each UBO, (iii) bank reference letters from a regulated foreign bank, (iv) source of wealth statement accompanied by tax returns, audited financials, or a legal opinion, and (v) completed FINTRAC UBO attestation forms.
Cross-border trade from high-risk jurisdictions or unexpected intermediary payment processors.

Root cause: Cross-border trade from high-risk jurisdictions or unexpected intermediary payment processors.

Defense evidence: Provide (i) contracts with POD suppliers and fulfillment centers, (ii) commercial invoices and logistics documents for past 12 months, (iii) explanation of the payment rail (e.g., Stripe, PayPal, Payoneer) and evidence of platform’s KYC/AML compliance, and (iv) a board resolution stating that the company does not transact with sanctioned parties.
Rapid capital injection followed by immediate disbursement to third parties, potentially indicating layering or round-tripping.

Root cause: Rapid capital injection followed by immediate disbursement to third parties, potentially indicating layering or round-tripping.

Defense evidence: Provide (i) share subscription agreement or loan agreement describing the purpose of the funds, (ii) source of funds evidence (bank statement of transferor), (iii) business plan and cash-flow projection, and (iv) a corporate authorization showing the treasury mandate and expected settlement flows.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant wire transfer protocols for inbound shareholder loans or equity contributions into a BC corporation: (i) send via SWIFT MT103 from the investor's bank to a schedule I bank in Canada with a clear 70/72 field indicating 'Equity subscription' or 'Shareholder loan'; (ii) use purpose code 'S' or 'O' on Canadian bank forms (not a regulated code); (iii) ensure funds are received in CAD at a lock-up rate; (iv) file an annual report with CRA for the Capital Dividend Account if applicable; (v) for amounts above CAD 10,000, the receiving bank must file a Large Cash Transaction Report (LCTR) or Virtual Currency Transaction Report (VCTR) with FINTRAC; (vi) maintain board minutes and transfer ledger entries to demonstrate non-taxable capital contribution (for equity) or documented loan agreement with market interest rate to avoid deemed benefit.For cross-border trade settlements, permitted clearing rails include: (i) SWIFT MT103 for direct bank-to-bank transfers (standard for supplier invoices, Normally a POD app purchase is pre-paid), (ii) Commercial Letter of Credit (LC) for high-value or recurring bulk orders, advised through a Canadian bank; (iii) bulk ACH/NACHA payments for Canadian-dollar domestic payments, and (iv) third-party platforms (Stripe, PayPal, Payoneer) for micro-disbursements to independent contractors or cross-border suppliers, provided the recipient’s bank supports the corridor. For outbound conversion, CAD is sold at the bank’s spot rate with a 1.5–2.5% spread; forward contracts are available for fixed future payments. For commercial LC, the Canadian bank requires an underlying trade contract, a shipping document set, and proof of customs clearance (EIPA). Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27.0% combined general corporate rate: federal 15.0% plus British Columbia 12.0% (federal statutory 38.0% reduced by 10.0% provincial abatement and 13.0% general rate reduction)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident beneficial owner, absent treaty reduction.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 must be filed no later than six months after the corporation's fiscal period-end. The final balance-due date is generally two months after year-end, or three months after year-end for a CCPC eligible for the Small Business Deduction; non-resident corporations with Canadian business income are subject to the same six-month filing rule and applicable instalment rules.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No statutory de minimis exemption under ITA s.247(4); the CRA administratively expects contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation for cross-border non-arm's-length transactions of at least CDN 1,000,000 per fiscal year. A Master File and Country-by-Country Report are required where the multinational group consolidated revenue is at least CDN 750,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no freestanding Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime equivalent to UAE; however, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule in ITA s.245 recharacterizes transactions lacking commercial substance, and treaty protection may be denied under limitation-on-benefits and general anti-abuse principles. The CRA will examine actual business functions, staff, premises, risk assumption, and decision-making for PF/PE claims in e-commerce POD structures.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion - Unreported E-Commerce Revenue

Statutory sanction: ITA s.239(1): on summary conviction, a fine between 50% and 200% of the tax sought to be evaded plus up to 2 years imprisonment; on indictment, a fine between 100% and 200% of the tax sought to be evaded plus up to 5 years imprisonment; CRA may reassess beyond the normal statute-barred period under s.152(4) and impose gross negligence penalties under s.163(2).

Director personal liability: Directors who direct, authorize, or acquiesce in filing false returns are parties to the offence and may be prosecuted personally. Civil director liability under ITA s.227.1 attaches to unremitted payroll withholdings and GST/HST even when the corporation is bankrupt; criminal warrants and customs flags can lead to detention, asset seizure, and travel restrictions at Canadian borders.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing and Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: ITA s.247(2) adjusts transfer pricing to the arm's-length price; s.247(3) imposes a penalty of up to 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment when the taxpayer did not make reasonable efforts to determine an arm's-length price; CRA may also charge interest on the adjusted tax and apply heightened audits.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false intercompany invoices or instruct accounting staff to fabricate pricing studies can be jointly assessed for the resulting corporate tax if the CRA pierces the veil for fraudulent misrepresentations; gross negligence penalties may extend to officers who participated in the misrepresentation.
Shell Company Abuse / Treaty Shopping / GAAR

Statutory sanction: GAAR under ITA s.245 denies the tax benefit and re-determines the consequences in a manner the CRA considers reasonable; s.245(5) adjustments may be combined with transfer pricing penalties; Canada may also deny treaty benefits under the DTA's limitation-on-benefits clause, imposing withholding at the statutory 25% domestic rate.

Director personal liability: Directors may receive director penalty notices for unremitted amounts; courts may lift the corporate veil where the corporation is found to be an instrument of fraud under provincial corporate law, making directors personally liable for the repatriated or stripped profits plus penalties; serious cases can result in director disqualification and pre-trial bail conditions including surrender of passports.
Money Laundering / Proceeds of Crime Misuse

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and Criminal Code, laundering proceeds of crime carries imprisonment up to 10 years; administrative monetary penalties for compliance failures can be substantial, and criminal forfeiture of assets may be ordered.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers can be charged if they knew or were criminally negligent that business revenues were tainted; PCMLTFA violations do not normally pierce the corporate veil, but participation in laundering can make directors liable as principals and subject to travel bans or extradition holds while criminal proceedings are pending.
