Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation – a BC-registered corporation is a separate taxpayer under the Income Tax Act, subject to federal and provincial corporate income tax; no pass-through to shareholders.

Available election forms

No check-the-box or hybrid entity election is available under Canadian law. A corporation may file a Section 85 rollover election for qualifying asset transfers, and may elect a non-calendar taxation year under ITA 142.6(1). Anti-hybrid mismatch rules apply automatically; no elective treatment can override statutory classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST – CAD 30,000 worldwide taxable supplies (small-supplier threshold); BC PST – CAD 10,000 annual taxable sales in BC (small vendor exemption).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Tangible personal property is zero-rated under GST/HST when the supplier exports the goods from Canada in the course of a commercial activity and retains proof of export. Services that are performed directly on exported goods, or that are for goods outside Canada, may also be zero-rated. For e-commerce pet supplies, the seller must ship the goods to a foreign destination; supply made in Canada before export does not qualify unless physical export is verified.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A or exemption record for goods valued under CAD 2,000, unless controlled/prohibited)
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / Courier Waybill evidencing export from Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank remittance/SWIFT evidence showing foreign-source payment)
  • Commercial invoice / sales contract with terms of sale and foreign destination
  • Export packing list and customs broker release documentation (where applicable)

Canada does not impose continuous transaction control (CTC), real-time fiscal clearance, or digital stamping. A GST/HST registrant must issue invoices containing prescribed CRA information; e-commerce records and electronic invoices must be made available for CRA audit in a standard electronic format.

CRA may reassess an export zero-rating claim at full GST (5%) or combined GST/PST (12%) if export documentation is lacking or the goods never left Canada. Penalties include understated tax penalties, false-statement penalties, and interest under the Excise Tax Act; wilful tax evasion can trigger criminal prosecution and penalties up to 25% of remittance evaded. Export evidence must be retained for at least six years from the end of the reporting period. The 180-day benchmark is administrative for monitoring remittance evidence; Canadian law has no statutory 180-day repatriation rule.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely convertible on both current and capital account. Canada is an IMF Article VIII jurisdiction and maintains no foreign-exchange control law, no central bank conversion permits, and no capital-flow approval requirements for equity injections, shareholder loans, trade disbursements, or dividend remittances. The effective friction for profit repatriation is not exchange control but taxation: Part XIII of the Income Tax Act imposes a 25% statutory withholding tax on dividends and certain payments to non-residents, reducible by treaty (e.g., typically 5%-15% under the Canada-US treaty and 10%-15% under the Canada-China treaty). Cross-border electronic funds transfers of C$10,000 or more must be reported to FINTRAC by financial institutions, and all wire traffic must be screened against Canadian sanctioned-party lists (Special Economic Measures Act) and applicable OFAC lists adopted by Canadian bank policies.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no repatriation deadline for export proceeds, including USD settlement flows from Stripe/PayPal e-commerce sales. A BC-incorporated exporter may legally retain USD, EUR, or other foreign currency in domestic foreign-currency accounts or offshore accounts, with conversion at market rates on a discretionary basis. Export receipts remain subject to ordinary Canadian income tax, GST/HST registration requirements where applicable, and customs documentation; the exporter must preserve invoices, shipping records, courier receipts, and payment-provider settlement statements to evidence the commercial purpose of all inbound foreign-currency wires.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), which under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) acts as the retail payment activity supervisor, operating alongside: OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions) as prudential regulator of banks under the Bank Act; FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada) as AML/CFT supervisor and MSB registrar under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); Payments Canada as operator of the Lynx RTGS system and the Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS); and Global Affairs Canada / Finance Canada for economic sanctions administration under the Special Economic Measures Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Conditionally high viability subject to a licensing overlay. Canada has no dedicated 'EMI' licence; a non-bank payment platform operating in Canada must (i) register as a money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC where it performs foreign remittance, money transfer, or virtual currency functions, and (ii) register as a payment service provider (PSP) with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA); RPAA registration opened on 1 November 2024 with a mandatory application deadline for larger PSPs of 8 September 2025. Viable commercial rails for the CA-CORP treasury architecture: Wise (Wise Payments Canada Inc., FINTRAC-registered MSB), Payoneer (FINTRAC-registered MSB operating through Canadian sponsor-bank settlement), Airwallex (FINTRAC-registered MSB), and PayPal Canada / Stripe (which settle through chartered-bank partner accounts). Key legal constraints: regulated PSPs must segregate client funds, may not lend or invest them, and all CAD settlement must ultimately clear via ACSS or Lynx through a Canadian chartered bank or authorized foreign bank. For a Low_Risk_Standard ISIC 4791 e-commerce merchant, the optimal structure is a Tier-1 domestic CAD account as the treasury anchor, with Wise/Payoneer used only as collection or supplier-disbursement overlay rails, never as sole treasury custodian.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant routing protocol: (i) from a US parent or shareholder, transmit via SWIFT MT103 or Fedwire/CHIPS to the beneficiary CAD or USD account at the Tier-1 Canadian bank; (ii) remittance narrative must state 'Equity contribution – share issuance' or 'Shareholder loan to [legal entity name]'; (iii) the receiving bank will apply PCMLTFA identity and source-of-funds verification and automatically file a FINTRAC Electronic Funds Transfer Report for any cross-border transfer of C$10,000 or more; (iv) for amounts above approximately C$50,000, instruct the sending bank to use SWIFT gpi or a Lynx RTGS domestic leg to avoid ACSS two-day settlement uncertainty; (v) provide in advance to the bank: corporate resolution authorizing the account mandate, share subscription agreement, updated share register, and UBO register extract. Canada imposes no stamp duty on share subscriptions or capital contributions, and inbound equity injections are not taxable at the corporate level; a voluntary CBCA/BC incorporation short-form return may be required.Approved clearing rails for procuring imported pet products, feeding devices, and supplies: (1) SWIFT MT103 open-account wires settled via Fedwire/CHIPS for USD or ACSS/Lynx for CAD, with full beneficiary bank BIC, account number, and purchase-order narrative; (2) irrevocable documentary letters of credit (UCP 600; sight or usance) issued via SWIFT MT700/MT707 through the Tier-1 bank's trade desk, suitable for higher-value supplier contracts; (3) documentary collections (URC 522; D/P or D/A) for mid-risk corridors; (4) push payments from the CAD treasury to Wise/Payoneer/Airwallex virtual accounts for high-frequency, low-value supplier payouts; and (5) cross-border CAD/USD 'back-to-back' clearing through the bank's US correspondent to minimize FX conversion leakage where both inflows and outflows occur in USD. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Conditionally high viability subject to a licensing overlay. Canada has no dedicated 'EMI' licence; a non-bank payment platform operating in Canada must (i) register as a money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC where it performs foreign remittance, money transfer, or virtual currency functions, and (ii) register as a payment service provider (PSP) with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA); RPAA registration opened on 1 November 2024 with a mandatory application deadline for larger PSPs of 8 September 2025. Viable commercial rails for the CA-CORP treasury architecture: Wise (Wise Payments Canada Inc., FINTRAC-registered MSB), Payoneer (FINTRAC-registered MSB operating through Canadian sponsor-bank settlement), Airwallex (FINTRAC-registered MSB), and PayPal Canada / Stripe (which settle through chartered-bank partner accounts). Key legal constraints: regulated PSPs must segregate client funds, may not lend or invest them, and all CAD settlement must ultimately clear via ACSS or Lynx through a Canadian chartered bank or authorized foreign bank. For a Low_Risk_Standard ISIC 4791 e-commerce merchant, the optimal structure is a Tier-1 domestic CAD account as the treasury anchor, with Wise/Payoneer used only as collection or supplier-disbursement overlay rails, never as sole treasury custodian.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined (Federal 15% general CIT rate + British Columbia 12% general provincial rate); lower 11% combined rate applies to Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) qualifying active business income through the Small Business Deduction.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid to non-residents, absent a tax treaty. Under the Canada-USA tax treaty, the rate is reduced to 15% generally and 5% for a US corporation owning at least 10% of the voting stock; similar reductions exist under other applicable DTAs (e.g., UK, Germany, Australia depending on ownership).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Contemporaneous local file documentation and Form T106 (Information Return on Non-Arm's Length Transactions) must be filed where the aggregate value of non-arm's-length cross-border transactions with a non-resident exceeds CAD 1 million during the taxation year. Master File documentation is expected for Canadian members of large multinational groups with consolidated global revenue exceeding CAD 50 million, and Country-by-Country Reporting applies to groups with consolidated revenue of at least CAD 750 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply offshore-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR). However, Canadian corporate tax law requires a real business purpose and economic substance. The General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) permits CRA and the courts to disregard transactions that are primarily tax-motivated and amount to abusive avoidance. A mailbox or shell corporation with no physical presence, employees, or business activities in BC will likely be denied DTA benefits and face reassessment and treaty-shopping penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act section 239, an indictable offence carries a fine of up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to five years; summary conviction fines up to CAD 25,000 and imprisonment up to two years. Civil penalties under section 163 include up to 50% of the understated tax for gross negligence, plus interest.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly participate in or authorize evasion are personally exposed to criminal prosecution, civil restitution, director-disqualification proceedings, and CRA collection from personal assets. Serious cases may trigger travel bans through court orders or asset freezing orders.
Failure to Withhold Part XIII Tax on Non-Resident Payments

Statutory sanction: The corporation is held liable for the full unwithheld amount, plus interest and penalties under ITA sections 227(8) and 227(9). Late reporting of NR4 slips may incur separate fines and administrative penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally, jointly and severally liable under section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for unremitted Part XIII withholdings. Failing to act can lead to CRA garnishing director bank accounts, personal bankruptcy risk, and legal prosecution for aiding the evasion.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess taxable income using the arm's-length principle, with interest on additional taxes. A transfer pricing penalty of 10% of the gross adjustment applies under section 247(3) where the adjustment exceeds the CAD 1 million threshold and contemporaneous documentation is deficient.

Director personal liability: Directors can be held responsible for counseling or participating in the making of false records; CRA may seek penalties against the corporation, and directors may be examined and surcharged in tax court proceedings. Deliberate scheming exposes directors to criminal liability under section 239 and to CRA Civil Fraud investigations.
Shell Company / Anti-Money Laundering (AML) Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Criminal money laundering under the Criminal Code is punishable by imprisonment up to 14 years and substantial fines. FAILURE TO COMPLY with FINTRAC obligations under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act may result in administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, and forfeiture of assets.

Director personal liability: Directors who direct or facilitate such arrangements risk personal criminal conviction, asset forfeiture, travel restrictions, and extradition. Canadian courts have upheld director prosecutions for knowingly participating in shell-company schemes designed to launder e-commerce receipts.
