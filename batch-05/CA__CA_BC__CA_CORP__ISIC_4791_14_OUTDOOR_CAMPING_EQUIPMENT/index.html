Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada — Business Registration Online (BRO) and My Business Account portals; British Columbia Ministry of Finance (eTaxBC portal) for BC Provincial Sales Tax (PST) registration

E-government filing portal

Foreign corporations carrying on business in Canada register for a federal Business Number (BN) and a GST/HST program account (RT) via the CRA Business Registration Online service or by filing Form RC1; non-residents without a physical Canadian presence may register through the CRA International Tax Services Office (Sudbury) using Form RC176 and may obtain a simplified GST/HST account. A BC PST vendor registration must be filed through eTaxBC with the BC Ministry of Finance where taxable sales into BC exceed the remote vendor threshold or where the corporation maintains a place of business in BC. Sequential procedure: (1) incorporate as a federal/provincial business corporation; (2) obtain the BN with a GST/HST remittance account suitable for the export/resale activity; (3) register for BC PST once nexus is established; (4) activate the CRA My Business Account credential for electronic filings and audit correspondence.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a Canadian business corporation is a taxable Canadian corporation subject to federal Part I income tax on worldwide income, with no pass-through election available. Territorial_Scope: resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income; non-resident corporations filing under section 115 of the Income Tax Act are taxable only on Canadian-source business income attributable to a Canadian permanent establishment. Tax_Exempt_Zone: none; no tax-exempt zone applies to this commercial model.

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box regime exists in Canada. Available statutory elections include: voluntary GST/HST registration for small suppliers (Excise Tax Act s. 240(4)); the closely related corporation election for intercompany supplies (Excise Tax Act s. 156); the GST/HST quick-method accounting election; the income tax election under s. 216 for non-residents earning rents or royalties; and the BEPS hybrid-mismatch narrowing rules under ITA s. 18.2–18.4. No election permits a Canadian business corporation to be treated as a pass-through entity.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: mandatory registration where taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in four consecutive calendar quarters (small-supplier threshold) for a person carrying on business in Canada; non-resident digital or fulfillment-based suppliers may be required to register without an applicable threshold. BC PST: mandatory registration for any vendor with a place of business in BC; for non-resident remote sellers and marketplace facilitators, registration is required where taxable BC consumer sales exceed CAD 10,000 in the preceding 12-month period (effective 1 July 2021).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods from Canada are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act where the supplier obtains documentary evidence of exportation in the prescribed form and the goods are shipped or delivered outside Canada, normally within 60 days of the sale. Zero-rated export supplies allow full input tax credit recovery — the effective 0% GST rate applies to outbound sales of outdoor camping equipment to non-resident customers. Offshore services that are performed wholly outside Canada or relate to real property outside Canada may also qualify for zero-rating.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A Export Declaration / ACI eManifest filed with the Canada Border Services Agency)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing outbound movement from Canada
  • Commercial Invoice, packing list, and any export permits
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / proof of foreign-currency payment received by the exporter
  • Carrier and insurance documentation demonstrating delivery to a foreign destination

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or CRA API clearance mandate currently exists in Canada. GST/HST registrants generally must file returns electronically (GST/HST NETFILE or EDI) and remit payments electronically where prescribed thresholds apply. A voluntary PEPPOL-based national e-invoicing framework is under examination but has not been legislated; until implementation, compliance rests on ordinary invoice records and the CRA e-audit environment, with books and records (including the RCT-510 documentation request) to be produced in electronic format upon audit.

The CRA may deny zero-rating and assess uncollected GST with interest where export documentation is incomplete, untimely, or insufficient. False or fraudulent export claims attract penalties under Excise Tax Act s. 281.1(2) (the greater of CAD 8,000 and 50% of the net tax sought), gross-negligence penalties under Income Tax Act s. 163(2) (50% of the understated amount), a four-year normal reassessment window that is indefinite in cases of misrepresentation, and potential director liability for knowing participation in fraudulent zero-rating schemes. Retrospective clawbacks of input tax credits and export rebates are routinely issued where prescribed documentary tests are not satisfied.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has accepted IMF Article VIII obligations; the Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current account and capital account. No exchange control approvals, licensing, or prior notification requirements apply to profit remittance, dividend distribution, equity repatriation, loan repayment, or trade settlement by a British Columbia business corporation. The sole fiscal impediments on cross-border outflows are Canadian withholding taxes under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act: dividends to non-resident shareholders attract the statutory 25% rate, typically reduced to 15% or lower under a relevant tax treaty (e.g., Canada-US Income Tax Convention, Canada-China DTA), and non-treaty interest or royalty payments may be subject to 25% withholding unless an exemption applies.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline and no mandatory conversion ratio for export proceeds. An exporter may retain foreign currency earnings indefinitely in domestic or foreign currency bank accounts with authorized financial institutions. However, all cross-border electronic fund transfers of CAD 10,000 or more trigger FINTRAC reporting obligations, and Canadian businesses must continue to file statistical balance-of-payments declarations (including CBSA export documentation and Statistics Canada international transactions reporting) for macro-economic compilation purposes; these obligations are informational and do not restrict the disposition of export receivables.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); Canada does not operate a standalone foreign exchange control administration. Banking supervision and prudential oversight of authorized financial institutions are exercised by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), while cross-border payment monitoring and anti-money-laundering surveillance are administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium-High legal viability subject to the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), in force since November 1, 2024, and administered by the Bank of Canada. Overseas digital EMIs (Wise, Payoneer, Airwallex, and similar) that provide payment services to Canadian businesses must register with the Bank of Canada as retail payment service providers; unregistered foreign EMIs are not legally permitted to offer payment services to Canadian end-users. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal general rate 15% + British Columbia general rate 12% = combined headline rate of 27% for general business income; CCPC active business income up to the CA$500,000 business limit is taxed at an 11% combined rate (federal 9% + BC 2%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Statutory rate under Part XIII ITA: 25% of gross dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder; rate reduced by applicable tax treaty — commonly 5% where the beneficial owner is a company resident in the treaty state holding at least 10% of the voting shares and 15% for portfolio shareholders under the Canada–US DTA; certain Caribbean and other treaty rates range from 5% to 20%.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Where the Canadian entity (or the booked gross revenue attributable to its Canadian operations) exceeds CA$25,000,000 in gross revenue for the taxation year and it engages in transactions with non-resident related parties, mandatory transfer pricing documentation (Local File and Master File) must be prepared and contemporaneously updated under ITA s.247(4) and reported on CRA Form T2116; the documentation must be delivered to the CRA within 6 months of a written request or a penalty of CA$1,000 per month applies; the OECD-aligned Country-by-Country Report (CBCR) is required where the multinational group's consolidated revenue is at least CA$1,500,000,000 for the preceding year, filed within 12 months of the group's fiscal year-end.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not administer the UAE/Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime; substance is instead established through common law (central management and control, residence tests), the sham doctrine, and the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245 ITA) applied by the Supreme Court in Alta Energy Luxembourg S.à r.l. v. Canada (2021 SCC 7). A BC e-commerce corporation (ISIC 4791/NAICS 459110) is respected as a Canadian resident only where business decisions, board meetings (or unanimous written resolutions), registered office, banking and payment accounts, inventory or fulfilment operations, and tax compliance functions are genuinely carried on in Canada; the mere holding of an internet domain, A/B testing or drop-ship arrangement with a foreign fulfillment centre may create a permanent establishment risk in the foreign jurisdiction and a substance challenge domestically.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion - Violation: Wilful omission / false statements

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under ITA s.239(1): on indictment up to 5 years imprisonment and a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded; on summary conviction up to 2 years less a day and a fine of 50% to 200%; ITA s.238 for failure to file a return: CA$1,000–CA$25,000 fine and/or up to 1 year imprisonment; GST/HST-related evasion under ETA s.240 carries fines under the same range; the CRA also imposes 50% civil gross-negligence penalties under s.163(1).

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly participate in the evasion are individually prosecuted and cannot hide behind the corporate veil; criminal restitution orders can require personal repayment; the Crown enforces personal asset seizure and garnishment of personal accounts; a conviction triggers director disqualification under the CBCA and potential passport/travel enforcement through a criminal arrest warrant; the CRA seeks court orders for production of personal bank records under the Income Tax Act seizure powers.
Transfer Pricing and Intercompany Pricing Restructuring

Statutory sanction: Under ITA s.247(2), the CRA adjusts the transaction to the arm's-length amount and disallows the deduction; the s.247(8) penalty is 10% of the gross transfer-pricing adjustment (minimum CA$1,000) and s.163(2) penalties for false statements or omissions of up to 50% of the understated tax; the reassessment period is extended to 5 years where a taxpayer failed to report a foreign affiliate transaction or did not file the required forms, and interest is charged at the prescribed rate on the reassessed balance.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally examined in audit under oath; for unremitted amounts assessed to the corporation, the directors jointly and severally risk liability under ITA s.227.1; wilful participation in a transfer-pricing scheme to strip profits from Canada constitutes a criminal offence under ITA s.239 and the financial institutions are required to report suspicious transfers to FINTRAC; in insolvency proceedings, the CRA files a proof of claim that may be transferred to the Crown's priority over unremitted withholdings.
General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) / Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: The GAAR under s.245 ITA allows the CRA to ignore the avoidance transaction and re-determine tax payable, impose interest and, for abusive arrangements, apply the normal reassessment period extended to 6 years; the 2021 SCC decision in Alta Energy confirms DTA abuse is testable under the GAAR; penalties for arranging abusive tax products are asserted against promoters under s.163.2 (penalty up to CA$100,000 plus the planner's gross compensation); Canada can also withdraw treaty benefits by mutual agreement procedure (MAP) notification to the foreign state.

Director personal liability: The directors signing the tax opinion and corporate resolutions for the conduit arrangement may be named in a s.163.2 third-party penalty assessment; on insolvency, courts have ordered directors to pay equitable compensation to tax authorities where they shared in the tax advantage; criminal charges of counselling an offence against a law of Canada under the Criminal Code s.465 are available where the arrangement includes misrepresentation; professional advisers face disciplinary sanctions in their home jurisdiction.
Shell Company / Dormant Corporation and Dissolution Risk

Statutory sanction: The BC Registrar may dissolve the company under the BC Business Corporations Act for failure to file the annual registry report, and the federal Director under the Canada Business Corporations Act may dissolve it administratively; failure to file the T2 return allows the CRA to assess arbitrary income tax under s.152(7) on the best available information and to issue a requirement to file with penalties of 5% of the assessed tax and 1% per month thereafter; dissolution causes properties to escheat to the Crown and extinguishes right to sue.

Director personal liability: After dissolution and before revival (up to 5 years), directors are personally liable for all debts of the company under the applicable corporate statute; directors are individually liable for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST under s.227.1 ITA and s.323 ETA only if they knew or ought to have known of the failure and did not act with due diligence; the CRA may also issue a certificate of debt against directors personally and register it as a lien after obtaining a federal court judgment; travel bans are reserved to criminal cases, while civil director liabilities are collectable through the ordinary courts.
Anti-Money Laundering and FINTRAC reporting

Statutory sanction: Under the PCMLTFA and its Regulations, failure to report suspicious transactions, large cash transactions (CA$10,000+), or to maintain a compliance regime exposes the corporation to administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) of up to CA$1,000,000 per violation for entities and CA$500,000 for individuals; criminal offences carry fines of up to CA$2,000,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years; where the company holds a money services business licence, FINTRAC may revoke registration and the BC Financial Services Authority may suspend the business; all penalties are non-deductible.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly caused the corporation to conceal a reportable transaction are personally liable as principals (not merely as corporate officers) under the PCMLTFA; conviction results in a criminal record affecting US-Canada cross-border travel under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act (inadmissibility due to serious criminality), subject to travel bans and removal proceedings; the corporate veil is pierced for deliberate participation in money laundering, and the Crown makes confiscation orders over personal property and bank accounts.
