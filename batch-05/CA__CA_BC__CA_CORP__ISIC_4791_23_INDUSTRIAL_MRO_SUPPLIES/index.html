Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

Permissible but subject to strict conditions. Under s. 53 of the Business Corporations Act (BC), the registered office must be a physical address in British Columbia at which the corporation’s records and minute books are maintained and at which process may be served during normal business hours. A virtual office provider that provides a physical street address, a dedicated mailing address, and accepts service of process during statutory business hours is acceptable. However, use of a post-office box or a non-physical virtual address is not permissible. The BC Registry requires the registered office to be at a location that is not merely a mail-forwarding address. For an e-commerce MRO supplies business that does not maintain an inventory warehouse in BC, a virtual office is accepted as long as the provider is a licensed corporate services provider or law firm that can receive legal process and forward maintains records. Physical on-site inspection likelihood: Low-to-Moderate for the corporate registry; the BC Registrar does not conduct routine unannounced inspections of registered offices for ordinary business corporations. However, if the company is selected for a compliance review, a spot-check visit to the registered office may occur to verify that it is not a mail-only address. For an e-commerce company with no inventory on site, the likelihood of an unannounced inspection is below 5% annually (based on registry administrative risk). FINTRAC may conduct unannounced on-site examinations of the corporate’s compliance program if the company is a reporting entity under the PCMLTFA. Since this entity is e-commerce and not selling money services, FINTRAC AML compliance obligations are limited to reporting suspicious transactions and certain recordkeeping; OSFI does not typically inspect non-FI entities. The quantified risk of physical inspection for the registered office is 2%–5% in the first three years, increase to 15% if the VC address is a high-density shared office center flagged for shell companies.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation - a resident Canadian corporation is taxed under ITA Part I on worldwide income at the combined federal 15% + BC 12% general corporate rate (27%); no check-the-box or pass-through eligibility; a non-resident-owned corporation is still a Canadian resident if incorporated in Canada (ITA s. 250(4)(a)); foreign shareholders are subject to Part XIII withholding on dividends (25% under the ITA, potentially reduced to 5%/15% under an applicable tax treaty).

Available election forms

No check-the-box election exists under Canadian law; US LLC/partnership entities are per se taxable corporations. Available elections include: ITA s. 261 functional-currency election, CCA class elections under Income Tax Regulations s. 1100, ETA s. 156 election for closely related corporations to exclude internal taxable supplies from GST/HST, and foreign-affiliate clean-surplus/capital-dividend elections (ITA ss. 93(1) and 83(2)). Hybrid-mismatch ordering rules (ITA ss. 18.4, 18.6, 18.6.1) apply from July 1, 2022 and disallow deductions for payments that are deductible in Canada but not included in the recipient's jurisdiction without any elective override; use of an unlimited liability company (ULC) may trigger entity-classification mismatches in cross-border planning.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (GST/HST small-supplier threshold); the threshold is tested on the total consideration from taxable supplies made in Canada in the last four consecutive calendar quarters and, for corporations, also in any single calendar quarter. BC PST: registration is required once a vendor's taxable sales of tangible personal property in BC exceed CAD 10,000 per calendar year (small-vendor exemption).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act: (1) zero-rated if the supplier ships the tangible personal property (MRO hardware) to a destination outside Canada and possession passes at that destination; (2) if a GST/HST-registered recipient removes the goods from Canada, zero-rating applies where the recipient exports the goods within 60 days of delivery and retains proof of export; (3) services performed on goods that are exported (drop-ship/logistics support, technical documentation) may be zero-rated under s. 7 of Part V when supplied to a non-resident or in respect of exported tangible property; (4) sales delivered within BC attract 5% GST + 7% PST unless the purchaser obtains a direct-pay/exemption certificate; advertising/promotional expenses for the catalog are subject to GST/HST under the place-of-supply rules.

  • CBSA Canada Customs Export Declaration (B13A) filed via the Canadian International Trade Single Window (CITSW)/G7 customs reporting
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill evidencing physical shipment to the foreign consignee and foreign destination
  • Inward remittance records (bank credit advice/SWIFT MT103) evidencing receipt of export consideration from the foreign buyer

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, API pre-clearance, or digital stamping for e-invoices. Electronic filing of GST/HST returns is compulsory for registrants whose annual worldwide taxable revenues exceed CAD 1.5 million (GST/HST NETFILE or EDI); BC PST returns are paperless through eTaxBC. B2B e-invoicing is voluntary and may adopt PEPPOL/EBMS; CRA record-retention rules require 6 years of supporting books and records in Canada.

CRA's normal reassessment window is 4 years, extended indefinitely where a taxpayer has made misrepresentation or fraud; unwarranted zero-rating claims are clawed back with daily compounded prescribed-rate interest. Civil gross-negligence penalty under ETA s. 285.1 is 25% of the tax sought to be evaded; criminal prosecution under ETA s. 329 carries, on indictment, fines up to 50% of the evaded tax and/or 5 years imprisonment. Every export claim must be substantiated contemporaneously by B13A export declarations, shipping/waybills, and remittance evidence; failure results in retrospective GST/PST assessments, denial of input tax credits/refunds, penalties, and denial of the 0% rate for both GST and BC PST.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible under IMF Article VIII. Capital-account and current-account transactions are generally free from exchange-control approval, including capital injections, intercompany loans, trade settlements, and profit repatriation. Dividend remittances are subject to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax, normally 25% statutory or reduced under an applicable income tax treaty. Practical remittance hurdles include source-of-funds verification, FATCA/CRS beneficial-ownership reporting, Investment Canada Act notification in the narrow case of an acquisition of control, and the bank's own sanctions and anti-money-laundering screening.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory repatriation timeline, and no prescribed conversion ratio. Exporters may retain export receivables indefinitely in CAD or any freely convertible foreign currency. Statutory timeline: none. Mandatory conversion ratio: 0%. Cross-border currency movements of CAD 10,000 or more require a CBSA/FINTRAC declaration for reporting purposes only and do not create surrender or conversion obligations.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); cross-border funds-transfer administration is shared with FINTRAC and the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA), but Canada has no dedicated exchange-control authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Conditional Medium. Off-shore digital EMIs such as Wise, Airwallex, and Currenxie are feasible as secondary cross-border payment vehicles for B2B MRO catalogue receipts and supplier disbursements, but they are not chartered banks and cannot replace a domestic Schedule I bank operating account. Funds held with EMIs are typically in omnibus or partner-bank accounts, which creates counterparty and ring-fencing risk. If the offshore EMI solicits customers in Canada or provides Canadian-facing remittance services, it may require FINTRAC Money Service Business registration and provincial money-services licences. For prudential safety, large-value CAD and USD treasury flows must settle through Payments Canada and SWIFT correspondent accounts at a Canadian chartered bank.
