Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); federal and provincial business registrations facilitated through Corporations Canada and the British Columbia Corporate Registry; CRA service portal: My Business Account and GST/HST NETFILE (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses.html).

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must obtain a nine-digit Business Number (BN) with a GST/HST program account (RT####). Non-resident corporations submit Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts) or Form RC264 (Non-Resident Corporation Application) by mail to the applicable CRA tax centre, generally through a Canadian agent or attorney. The application must include foreign incorporation evidence, a Canadian address for service, and projected Canadian taxable supply amounts. Registration is mandatory once worldwide taxable supplies made in Canada exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or across four consecutive calendar quarters; voluntary registration is available to non-residents making zero-rated exports from Canada to claim input tax credits, with a two-year minimum registration commitment.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style 'check-the-box' entity classification election. A federal or provincial Business Corporation is an independent taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act, is subject to GST/HST as a separate registrant, and has no flow-through election. Available elections are transactional: section 85 corporate rollover on asset transfers, section 167 GST/HST joint election for transfer of a business as a going concern, section 216 election for non-residents on Canadian rental income, and section 273 reduced-rate treaty election for non-resident corporations eligible under the Canada-United States tax treaty.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (Canadian dollars) in annual worldwide taxable supplies made in Canada, triggered when exceeded in a single calendar quarter or across four consecutive calendar quarters (the 'small supplier' threshold); registration is also mandatory for any non-resident performing a commercial activity in Canada, such as parcel consolidation and linehaul services for cross-border e-commerce goods.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A BC parcel sorting, consolidation, and direct-injection linehaul service is zero-rated where (i) the supply is a freight transportation or handling service in respect of tangible personal property that is exported from Canada, and (ii) the service is supplied to a non-resident of Canada or to an exporter who exports the goods, with the goods physically leaving Canada within a reasonable time (typically within two years). Section 1 of Part V of Schedule VI zero-rates a supply of property for export; sections 7 and 8 of Part V of Schedule VI zero-rate freight, transport, loading, unloading, and consolidation services in respect of exported goods. Input tax credits associated with the entire export supply chain remain fully claimable.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Canadian Export Declaration filed via the Canadian Export Reporting System)
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill naming the foreign destination, overseas warehouse, or non-resident consignee
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank or FX confirmation evidencing receipt of foreign currency proceeds from the non-resident customer)

Canada does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government API pre-clearance regime for invoices. A GST/HST invoice must contain the prescribed data under the Excise Tax Act, but issuance remains business-controlled. CRA mandates electronic filing for GST/HST returns via NETFILE or EDI through My Business Account for registrants carrying on business in Canada, and requires all accounting records to be preserved in machine-readable format under ETA section 286 and ITA section 286. Voluntary Peppol-based e-invoicing pilots are underway, but e-invoicing is not yet statutorily mandated in Canada.

CRA audits require an unbroken documentary chain linking the zero-rated supply to the physical export; where export proof is inadequate, the supply is re-rated at 5% GST, the net tax is reassessed with arrears interest, and an 8% penalty applies for any false or untrue statement under Excise Tax Act section 285; gross negligence triggers a penalty equal to the greater of CAD 25,000 and 20% of net tax under ETA section 285.1. Misstatements of export data under the Customs Act are separately penalized up to CAD 25,000 per violation, with potential seizure of goods and criminal prosecution under ETA section 327. CRA may reassess within four years for ordinary errors, with no limitation period in cases of misrepresentation or fraud, and zero-rating claims are retroactively clawed back at the point of export documentation failure.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Both the current account and the capital account are freely convertible in Canada; CAD is a high-liquidity global currency with no approval, quota, or repatriation licensing regime. Non-resident capital contribution and dividend repatriation face no currency-control hurdles. The only substantive frictions are tax-driven: Part XIII non-resident withholding tax of 25% on dividends (treaty-reduced to 15% to 5% for qualifying 10% corporate shareholders), NR4 reporting, and potential deemed-dividend treatment under section 84.1 where paid-up capital (PUC) is overstated. Capital injections via share subscription must be carefully documented to fix stated capital and PUC, preserving tax-free extraction of equity on future wind-down.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No surrender obligation exists. Canada repealed the Foreign Exchange Control Act and lifted exchange controls in 1951. Exporters may retain, invest, or convert foreign-currency proceeds at their discretion, with no mandatory conversion ratio, no statutory conversion timeline, and no central-bank debtor inspection. Only statistical reporting requirements apply: enterprises with significant cross-border transactions report to Statistics Canada under the International Transactions Reporting System (ITRS), which is data-collection only and not a gatekeeping mechanism.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BOC). Canada operates a fully floating exchange-rate regime under the Currency Act and Bank of Canada Act. The Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises banks and FX settlement risk, while FINTRAC administers AML/ATF reporting. No ministry-level FX-licensing or exchange-control authority issues permits for current or capital transactions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Airwallex and comparable payment fintechs are viable for ancillary treasury and cross-currency settlement, rated Medium-High for this use case. They operate in Canada as Money Service Businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC, not as Schedule I depository banks; therefore they cannot offer insured Canadian deposit products, credit facilities, or equitable KYC protection for an operating company. They are legally constrained by the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) and, once the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) regime is fully in force, by Bank of Canada registration and safeguarding requirements. They may hold customer funds in pooled trust arrangements with Canadian banks; use them only for vendor payouts and small FX conversions, while the primary operating and escrow accounts must sit with a domestic Tier 1 bank.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Combined federal and British Columbia general corporate income tax rate of 23% (federal Part I rate 15% + BC provincial rate 8%). A Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) with active business income may qualify for the federal small business deduction and BC small business deduction, giving an 11% combined rate on the first CAD 500,000 of eligible active business income; a non-resident-controlled corporation is generally not a CCPC and remains at 23%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident person. Treaty relief may reduce this to 5% for dividends paid to a corporate shareholder resident in a tax treaty country that owns at least 10% of the voting shares, or to 15% in most other treaty portfolio cases; where no treaty applies, 25% is the operative rate.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due no later than six months after the corporation's taxation year-end. The balance of tax due is normally payable two months after year-end; a CCPC that was eligible for the small business deduction in the preceding year may have a three-month balance-due date. Installments are due when federal and BC tax payable exceed CAD 3,000 in a year.

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T106 is generally due on the same date as the T2 return, namely six months after the corporation's taxation year-end. The underlying transfer pricing documentation must be contemporaneous and completed by the T2 filing due date.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate fair market value of reportable non-arm's length transactions with a non-resident in a taxation year is the general threshold triggering contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation and T106 reporting. A Master File and Country-by-Country Report are required where the multinational group's consolidated revenue exceeds CAD 750 million; CRA Form RC4647 is used for Country-by-Country reporting. The BC parcel consolidation hub must maintain contemporaneous Local File documentation irrespective of materiality if CRA requests it.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply offshore-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR). Instead, the Income Tax Act General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under subsection 245(2) denies tax benefits arising from avoidance transactions lacking genuine economic substance, commercial purpose, and bona fide business motive. The parcel consolidation hub must have a substantive physical presence in BC, including a sorting facility, operative linehaul agreements, employees, local management decisions, and real commercial risk; a shell or conduit corporation with no operational control will be recharacterized for Canadian tax purposes.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under Income Tax Act subsection 239(1) may result in a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and/or imprisonment up to two years; civil gross negligence penalty under subsection 163(2) is 50% of the understated tax plus prescribed interest; CRA may also charge penalties for repeated failure to report and reassess outside the normal period in cases of misrepresentation.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who authorize or participate in evasion are jointly and severally liable for the corporate tax debt through director's liability rules and can be prosecuted personally. CRA may freeze personal assets, garnish wages, issue an enforcement garnishment, and obtain court orders requiring personal restitution. In criminal cases, bail conditions may include travel bans and reporting requirements.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement / Missing Documentation

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act subsection 247(3) imposes a transfer pricing penalty of 10% of the total transfer pricing adjustment if the taxpayer has not made reasonable efforts to determine an arm's length price; the CRA may also reallocate income and deny deductions, and gross negligence penalties may apply if false or misleading TP documentation is submitted. Interest accrues on all adjustments.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly approve sham intercompany agreements, false benchmarks, or fabricated service descriptions can be made personally liable under the CRA's joint and several liability provisions for corporate tax penalties; tax professionals involved may also face penalties under subsection 163.2. Director compensation notwithstanding, personal liability is assessed where funds are distributed as dividends or management bonuses while tax liabilities are left unpaid.
Unremitted Part XIII Withholding / Profit Repatriation Violation

Statutory sanction: The Canadian payer is personally liable to the CRA for the full amount of unremitted Part XIII tax, plus prescribed interest and late-remittance penalties (5% of the remittance plus 1% per month up to 12 months). Thin capitalization rules may disallow interest deductions on excessive debt; GAAR may recharacterize all or part of the interest as a dividend subject to Part XIII. Criminal prosecution is possible for intentional non-remittance.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable under section 227.1 for unremitted Part XIII withholding taxes whether the corporation is solvent or not. CRA may assess directors personally, register certificates in federal court, attach personal bank accounts, garnish director remuneration, and prohibit personal cross-border transfers where money is moved abroad to frustrate collection.
AML / Shell Company / Sham Conduit

Statutory sanction: The General Anti-Avoidance Rule may deny the expected tax benefits and recharacterize the arrangement; the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) allows administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 for compliance failures or false reporting; money laundering under Criminal Code section 462.31 is an indictable offense carrying up to ten years' imprisonment. The BC Business Corporations Act also permits the registrar to strike off a corporation for repeated compliance failure.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil can be pierced where the corporation is used as a cloak for fraud or sham transactions, exposing directors to personal liability for corporate debts. Directors who direct, participate in, or have knowledge of laundering proceeds may be individually criminally charged; adverse findings can also lead to discretionary sanctions, travel restrictions alongside criminal proceedings, disgorgement orders, and personal fines.
